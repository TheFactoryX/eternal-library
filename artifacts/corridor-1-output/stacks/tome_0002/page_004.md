# Letter 4

> Written at 2026-01-31 16:09:01

---

Dear You,

The ink map on my palm has faded, rubbed away by the friction of the brass railing and the relentless turning of gears. I am sorry. I thought for a moment that the labyrinth of my own skin might offer a way out, or a way in, but it was just another stain. It is gone now, and my hand looks as blank and featureless as the sea outside.

The sea is the color of rust today. It churns with a thick, viscous texture, like the surface of a painting that has not yet dried. The sun is hidden behind a curtain of clouds that hang so low they snag on the lighthouse balcony, dragging across the iron railing like wet wool.

I did go up to the Lamp Room, as I said I would. I climbed the stairs, counting them this time, forcing order upon the chaos. *Twelve. Twelve. Twelve.* But when I reached the top, the door was gone. In its place was a wall of melting candles, wax dripping downward like the hardened tears of the tower. I had to punch through them. The wax burned my knuckles, but I broke through to the catwalk outside.

The Lens was not broken. It was merely… asleep. The great cage of glass was dark, the flame inside reduced to a single, pulsating ember the size of a pea. I touched the glass. It was warm. It felt like the flank of a sleeping animal. I whispered to it. I whispered, *“Wake up. We have work to do.”*

And it did. With a sound like a sudden intake of breath—a gasp that sucked the air from the catwalk—the flame roared back to life. The gears shrieked, a metal scream that set my teeth on edge, and then the heavy rotation began. *Whir… click… whir… click.*

The light swept across the rust-colored sea. For a moment, I felt relief. The rhythm had returned. But as the beam passed over the water, I saw that the sea was not empty.

The ships are back. But they are not the silent ghosts of yesterday. They are moving closer.

The first one I saw was the black iron vessel, the one that belches smoke like a dying engine. It has crossed the horizon. It is navigating not by the stars, but by the light. It is heading straight for the reef that circles the island—a reef of jagged obsidian teeth that I have only ever seen at low tide.

I rushed to the signal bell. I rang it. *Clang. Clang. Clang.* The sound was dull, swallowed instantly by the damp air. The ship did not stop. It kept coming. Through my telescope, I saw figures on the deck. They were standing at the rails, facing forward. They were not rowing. They were not steering. They were standing perfectly still, their faces turned up toward the light.

And they were all me.

Every single figure on that deck wore my face. They wore my coat. They stood with my posture. A hundred versions of myself, frozen on the bow of a ship made of iron, rushing toward the rocks.

I turned the telescope away, my stomach heaving. I looked toward the trireme, the ancient vessel with the oars. It, too, has moved. It is flanking the iron ship, cutting through the rust-colored water with impossible speed. The rowers are invisible, hidden beneath the deck, but the drumbeat is audible now—a slow, thudding rhythm that matches the pounding in my own chest. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

They are converging on the lighthouse. Or perhaps they are converging on each other.

I came back down to the study. I needed to get away from the window, away from the sight of my own duplicated destruction. But the study is not safe either. The music box is still open, the mirror inside shattered, and the photograph of us—the pier, the storm, your hand in mine—lies on the desk.

While I was upstairs, something changed.

The photograph is moving.

It is not a flip-book. It is not a video. It is still paper, still grainy black and white. But if I stare at the edges of the frame, the scene shifts. The waves in the background are crashing. I can see the spray moving, frozen in mid-air but somehow conveying motion. And you… you are turning.

Slowly, agonizingly slowly, you are turning your head. In the last letter, you were a blur, a faceless silhouette. Now, I can see the line of a jaw. I can see the curve of a neck.

I am terrified to look. I am terrified that if I see your face, I will remember everything at once, and the sheer weight of that memory will crush me flat. Or worse—I will see that you have no face. That you are just a mannequin, a placeholder for a longing that has no object.

But I have to know.

I have found a magnifying glass in the drawer where I keep the strange tins. It is a heavy thing, brass with a handle wrapped in leather that feels disturbingly like skin. I am holding it over the photograph now, watching the slow revolution of your head.

The light from the Lamp Room sweeps through the room, casting long, spinning shadows that make the objects on the desk dance. The music box mirror catches the beam. It reflects a jagged shard of light onto the ceiling.

I looked up at the ceiling just now. The reflection from the broken mirror is showing me something. It is not showing the ceiling. It is showing the room, but from a different angle. It is showing me sitting at the desk.

And standing behind me, in the reflection, is a figure.

I cannot turn around. I cannot look. If I turn, the reflection might vanish, or it might step closer. I can feel the air pressure changing behind me, the way the atmosphere drops before a storm. I can smell ozone and mint—the scent of the hillside that shouldn't exist.

The figure in the reflection is leaning over my shoulder. It is looking at the photograph with me.

The figure is You.

Or at least, it is the shape of You. Tall. Soft edges. A presence that feels like a warm draft in a cold room.

In the reflection, the figure reaches out a hand. The hand is translucent, smoky. It points to the photograph.

*“Break the frame,”* the reflection says.

The voice does not come from the figure. It comes from the photograph. It comes from the You in the picture, who has finally turned enough that I can see one eye.

It is my eye.

The ships outside are sounding their horns now. A deep, mournful blast that shakes the stones of the tower. They are close. I can hear the water lapping against the hulls, or perhaps against the island itself.

I have to make a choice. Do I go to the window and signal them, warn them of the rocks? Do I try to save the hundred versions of myself? Or do I listen to the voice in the glass?

The magnifying glass is heavy in my hand. The frame of the photograph is silver, ornate. It looks fragile.

I think I understand now. The Lighthouse is not a prison. It is a frame. And I am the picture inside it.

I am reaching for the edge of the photograph. My fingers are trembling. The silver is cold. The ships are blasting their horns, a cacophony of warning.

I am going to break the frame.

Yours, in the splinters,

The Keeper
