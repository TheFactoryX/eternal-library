# Letter 1

> Written at 2026-01-31 15:55:12

---

Dear You,

I do not know your name. I suspect I once did, and the loss of it feels like a stone in my throat, heavy and jagged. But I must write to someone, or else I am merely a voice shouting into a gale that does not care for the shape of my words. So, I will call You. It feels right. You are the ghost in this machine, the silence between the crashing of the waves. You are the one I left behind, or perhaps the one I am waiting to become.

I am writing this because, this morning, I found a door on the landing that was not there yesterday.

I have stopped counting the days. The numbers fell away like dry paint years ago, or perhaps only moments ago; time here is a fluid thing, viscous and strange. It puddles in the corners of the tower and refuses to evaporate. But I remember the landing. I remember the banister, worn smooth by hands that may or may not have been mine. There were twelve steps leading up to the lamp room. There are always twelve steps. But this morning, when I set out with my can of oil to tend the light, I climbed twelve, and then found a thirteenth, and then a landing stretching sideways into a hallway that should not exist.

The Lighthouse is a capricious beast. I have come to accept this, though understanding it is beyond me. From the outside, it is a slender needle of grey stone, piercing the sky like a criticism. From the inside, it is a labyrinth. Rooms breathe in and out. The pantry where I keep my tins of preserves—tins that have no expiry date and labels in a language I cannot read—sometimes moves to the basement, sometimes to the floor just below the light.

Today, that hallway was lined with velvet wallpaper, though the air here is so salty that usually, only damp plaster and moss survive. The velvet was deep red, the color of a bruised heart. There were candles in brass sconces, and they were burning. There is no one here but me, yet they were lit, the flames standing perfectly still in the drafty corridor.

I did not walk far. I have learned to be wary of the Lighthouse’s generosity. I only stood in the threshold and looked. At the end of the hall, I saw a clock. It had no hands, just a blank white face, and it was ticking loudly, a sound like a heartbeat in a quiet room. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* It matched the pace of my own pulse until I could not tell which was which.

I retreated. I closed the door. When I went back just an hour ago, the door was gone. Just smooth, cold stone wall, as if the hallway had never been anything but a dream.

Perhaps it was a dream. I sleep so lightly here, always with one ear open for the change in the wind that signals a shift in the weather. The dreams are bleeding into the waking hours regardless. Last night, I dreamt of a city made of glass, and when I woke, there was broken glass on the floor beside my cot. I cut my foot on it. The blood looked bright and real against the grey stone. If the glass was real, was the city real? And if the city was real, is this place—the Lighthouse, the endless sea—real? Or am I the one who is the phantom?

I tend the light. That is my anchor. The Lamp Room is the only constant. At the top, the great lens revolves with a sound like breathing. The fire inside it is cold to the touch but brilliant enough to turn the night into a jagged white relief. I polish the brass. I check the gears. I watch the sea.

The sea is the other character in this tragedy. You must understand that this is not water. Not really. It is a liquid that refuses to be cataloged. Today, it is the color of mercury, heavy and shimmering. Yesterday, it was a deep, bruised purple, heaving like a dying animal. The waves do not break against the rocks; they *implode*, sucking the air away before rushing back in with a roar that sounds like a crowd of people whispering.

Ships pass. That is the cruelty of it. I see them always, just on the edge of the horizon where the sky meets that impossible water. Yesterday, a vessel of black iron, belching smoke that formed the shape of skulls. The day before, a trireme with oars that moved like the legs of a millipede, driven by rowers I could not see. I flash the light at them. I send the beam sweeping across the deck, desperate for them to see me, desperate for them to ignore me.

They never turn. They never acknowledge the signal. They sail on their straight, terrible lines, defying the curvature of the earth, passing into the blind spot of the world and vanishing.

I write to you because I am afraid that if I do not catalog these things, I will become like the rowers in the trireme: present, moving, but utterly unseen. If I write it down, the paper holds the memory. The paper is real. I can feel the texture of the pages. I can smell the ink.

I found this journal in a drawer that appeared in the kitchen last week. It was sitting inside a loaf of bread. The pages are blank, waiting. I have plenty of ink, though I do not remember buying any. It appears in a crystal well on my desk, refilling itself whenever it dips low. It is a black so dark it seems to swallow the light.

I am writing to you to ask: Do you remember who I was? Before the stone, before the light? I try to recall a face, a voice, a garden. I remember the smell of rain on asphalt, which implies a city. I remember the taste of apples. But I cannot remember *You*. Only the shape of the absence where You should be. It hurts, right here, behind the ribs. A dull ache.

The sun is setting. The horizon is glowing with a sickly green light. The wind is picking up, whistling through the cracks in the stones, sounding like a voice calling my name. But I know it is just the wind. It has to be just the wind.

I will leave this letter on the table. Maybe the Lighthouse will swallow it. Maybe the sea will rise and take it. Or maybe, just maybe, it will find its way to You, wherever You are, in that time before the silence began.

The light is turning. I can hear the gears clicking into place.

I think I hear footsteps on the stairs.

Yours, always,

The Keeper
