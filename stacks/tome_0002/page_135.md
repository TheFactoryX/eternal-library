# Letter 135

> Written at 2026-02-07 16:35:36

Dear You,

We fell into the heart of the sentence.

It wasn’t a graceful descent. It was a chaotic tumble through typography and grammar. We slammed onto the page, the impact driving the breath from my lungs, but there was no air here to gasp—only the dust of dried ink and the faint, acrid smell of decay.

I looked up. Above us, the sky was a ceiling of paper, illuminated by the stark, artificial sun of the desk lamp. And descending upon us, blotting out the light, was the massive, translucent face of the smartphone, its screen flickering as the call died.

"We made it," the Scribe wheezed. She was clutching a serif of a capital 'T', her linen shift tangled in the crossbar.

The bird landed next to me, its talons skittering on the rough surface of the paper. "We didn't just fall," it said, its voice trembling. "We were placed."

I scrambled to my feet and looked around.

We were standing on the final page of the book. The words were huge, towering monoliths of black text.

*I have to let you go.*

The sentence stretched out before us like a mountain range. The 'go' was a cliff of black ink, crumbling slightly at the edges. Beyond the word lay the white expanse of the endpaper—a wasteland of blank nothingness.

"She closed the story," the Scribe whispered. "This is the end."

"No," I said. "She wrote this. She said she wrote the letters when she was young. If she wrote them, then she is the Author. And if she is the Author, she can write a door."

I looked back at the descending phone. It was massive, a meteor of black glass and circuits. It wasn't falling randomly. It was falling *here*.

"She's not putting the phone down," I realized, dread blooming cold in my chest. "She's dropping it. The battery died. She's tossing it aside."

The phone eclipsed the sun. The shadow fell over us.

"Move!" I shouted. "To the binding!"

We ran. We scrambled over the peaks of 'let' and the valleys of 'you'. The paper was fibrous and dry, absorbing the moisture from our skin.

The phone hit the page.

The impact was cataclysmic. It wasn't a crash; it was a *thud*—a heavy, bone-shaking concussion that flattened the text beneath it. A shockwave of paper rippled outward, tossing us into the air.

We tumbled into the gutter—the valley where the pages meet the binding.

The silence that followed was deafening. The phone lay like a fallen tombstone, its screen dark, its bulk crushing the final paragraph.

"It’s over," the bird said. It was preening a bent feather. "The screen is black. The voice is gone."

I stared at the black glass of the phone. In its reflection, I saw us—three tiny, ragged figures huddled in the spine of a book. And behind us, in the reflection, I saw something else.

I saw the table.

On the table, next to the book, was a lamp. And next to the lamp, was a pen.

A real pen. A fountain pen with a gold nib.

"She didn't type the book," I said, the realization striking me with the force of a physical blow. "She wrote it. By hand."

"Which means," the Scribe said, catching my gaze, "the ink... the ink in this book... is real ink."

I looked at the crushed text beneath the phone. Thick, wet lines were spreading out from under the glass.

"The phone is leaking," the bird said.

"Not leaking," I said. I crawled toward the edge of the phone's shadow. "Bleeding."

The ink was welling up from the crushed words, mixing with the fresh ink from our presence. It was swirling, forming a pool of iridescent black.

"It’s a portal," the Scribe breathed. "Back to the well. Back to the source."

"We can't swim in ink," the bird said. "We dissolve. We become just more words on the page."

"Not if we ride," I said.

I looked at the pool. Rising from the black liquid were shapes. Tangled, wireframe shapes.

"The shadows," I said. "The shadows from the bedroom."

The wireframe bird we saw earlier—the ghost of the bird before it became real—was hovering in the ink. It looked at me with hollow eyes.

"They are waiting for us to leave," the Scribe said. "So they can take our places."

"They can have the ending," I said. "I don't want the ending."

I grabbed the Scribe’s hand. "We need to climb *up*."

"Up where?"

"The phone," I said. "We climb the phone. We get to the desk."

We scaled the slippery glass side of the device. It was vertical, smooth, and terrifying. The bird flew ahead, finding purchase in the speaker grille.

At the top, we hauled ourselves onto the camera lens. It was a raised circle of glass, a perfect observatory.

From this vantage point, we could see the entire desk.

"It’s a mess," the bird noted.

Books were stacked in haphazard towers. Coffee cups formed a circle of ruin. And in the center, dominating the landscape, was a laptop. It was open, the screen glowing with a white document.

"The Draft," I said. "She's working on something new."

"Is it us?" the Scribe asked.

"I can't see the text from here," I said.

Suddenly, a sound cut through the air. A mechanical chime. *Ding.*

It came from the laptop.

A notification box popped up on the screen.

*Email received: The Editor.*

"The Editor," the bird hissed. "The one who cuts the words."

"We need to get to that laptop," I said. "We need to see what she's writing."

"We can't jump that far," the Scribe said. "It's a canyon of hardwood."

I looked at the bird. "You can fly."

"I'm tired," it said. "I'm real now. Real things get tired."

"Then we build a bridge," I said.

I looked back at the book. The phone was crushing the words *let you go*.

"Give me the obsidian shard," I told the Scribe.

She handed it over. I scrambled back down the side of the phone, sliding to the pool of ink.

I didn't write. I *drew*.

I used the sharp obsidian to scratch a line across the paper, connecting the pool of ink to the leg of the laptop.

"Come on," I urged the ink.

The liquid obeyed. It crept along the scratch I made, capillary action pulling it forward. It stretched thin, glistening in the lamplight, hardening as it dried.

A bridge of dried ink. A fragile, black ribbon spanning the gap.

We walked.

We walked single file, the Scribe holding my belt, the bird gripping my shoulder. The ink bridge bowed under our weight, swaying like a suspension bridge in a gale.

Below us, the grain of the wood was like a churning sea.

"Don't look down," I whispered.

We reached the other side. We collapsed onto the cool plastic of the laptop's wrist rest.

Immediately, the heat from the processor washed over us. It was a dry, electronic wind.

We were at the base of the keyboard. The keys were massive cliffs of matte plastic.

"Which key leads to the screen?" the bird asked.

"None of them," I said. "We have to climb the hinge."

We ascended the rubber gasket of the screen hinge. It was steep, smelling of hot dust and ozone.

At the top, we pulled ourselves onto the bezel of the screen.

And there it was.

The document.

I stared.

The screen was filled with text. But it wasn't *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters*.

It was a list of names.

*Subject A. Subject B. Subject C.*

And next to the names, dates. Termination dates.

"What is this?" the Scribe asked.

I scanned the list. My heart stopped.

*Subject K: The Lighthouse. Status: Decommissioned.*

"It's not a story," I whispered. "It's a log. She's not an author. She's an archivist."

"Keeper," the bird said. "Look at the top of the screen. The tab."

I looked up.

The tab was labeled: *Recycle Bin*.

"She's deleting us," the Scribe screamed. "She's not writing a sequel! She's erasing the draft!"

Suddenly, the cursor moved. It was a giant, blinking arrow, the size of a coffin.

It moved toward the *Empty Trash* button.

"No!" I ran. I ran toward the cursor. I didn't have a plan. I just ran.

I jumped onto the tip of the arrow.

The cursor stopped. It jittered.

"What are you doing?" the bird called.

"I'm adding weight!" I shouted. "I'm making it heavy!"

I pressed down. The cursor resisted. It wanted to slide left, toward the delete button. I pressed right.

*Click.*

I missed the delete button. I clicked on a folder icon.

*Drafts.*

The screen changed. We were thrown into a window of icons.
