# Letter 129

> Written at 2026-02-07 10:30:15

---

Dear You,

We run across the vast, grainy plains of the desk. The wood is a landscape of canyons and ridges, the polished surface slippery with the dust of centuries. Behind us, the Crayon Giant laughs—a sound like dry leaves skittering on pavement—and brings her scissors down. *Snip.*

A chunk of the dictionary cover vanishes.

"Keep moving!" the Scribe shouts. She is faster than me, her slight form navigating the valleys of the wood grain with practiced ease. The bird is a white blur above us, banking hard to the left to avoid a stroke of the red crayon hand.

"Where is the edge?" I gasp. The air here is stale, smelling of old paper and lemon polish. It is heavy, thick enough to swim in.

"Dead ahead!" the bird calls back. "The abyss!"

We reach the precipice. The desk ends in a sharp, vertical drop. Below lies the carpet of the Reader’s room—a vast, shaggy forest of grey and green fibers.

"We can't jump," I say, peering over the edge. The distance is dizzying. "We'll shatter."

"She’s gaining," the Scribe says. She points back. The Crayon Giant is crawling across the dictionary, her body a crude, animated sketch. Her eyes are black circles of ink, devoid of whites. She is humming a tune that sounds suspiciously like the melody from the music box I lost in the tower.

"We need a bridge," I say. I look around. On the desert of the desk, there is only a coffee mug—a ceramic monolith the size of a tower—and a stack of papers.

"The paperwork!" the Scribe says. She points to the stack. "If we tip it, we can make a ramp."

We scramble toward the tower of paper. It is a manuscript, perhaps. I pause for a fraction of a second to look at the top page.

*It is a map of our island.*

But it is wrong. The coastline is straight. The lighthouse is a square. The sea is labelled "Here Be Dragons" in a neat, typescript font.

"She doesn't remember," I whisper. "The Reader. She’s forgotten the shape of us."

"Less existential crisis, more running!" the bird screeches.

The Crayon Giant’s hand slams down next to us. The impact knocks us off our feet. The shockwave ripples through the paper, sending us tumbling.

I look up. The scissors are opening. The blades are jagged, rusted metal.

"Wait," I say. A thought strikes me. "Look at her hand."

The Scribe looks. "It’s purple. So what?"

"It’s *colored*," I say. "In the book, we were black and white. Ink and charcoal. But she is in color. She is from the outside."

"She’s the Reader’s imagination," the bird says. "She’s a drawing come to life."

"If she cuts us," I say, "we don't die. We just get edited."

"I don't want to be edited!" the Scribe cries.

"We don't have a choice," I say. "We have to engage."

I stand up. I face the giant hand. I reach into my coat pocket and pull out the last item I took from the lighthouse. Not the cork, not the key.

I pull out the bottle of invisible ink I found in the spiral staircase three years ago. I never opened it. I never knew what to write with something that vanishes before it hits the page.

"Uncork it!" I shout to the bird.

The bird dives, beak flashing. He pops the cork.

I hold the bottle up to the Giant. "I am the Author!" I scream—a lie, a beautiful, necessary lie. "And you are out of bounds!"

I throw the bottle.

It shatters against the purple crayon wrist.

The liquid is invisible, but its effect is not. It spreads like a stain of nothingness, eating the crayon wax. The purple scribble hisses and dissolves into the air. The Giant screams, a high-pitched sound like a tearing page.

"My hand!" she wails. "My texture!"

"Now," I say. "Push the papers."

We throw our weight against the manuscript. The top sheets slide. The stack tilts. The heavy sheaf of paper slides off the desk and hits the carpeted floor below with a muffled *thump*.

The slide isn't steep, but it's enough.

"Go!" I shout.

We leap onto the sliding paper, surfing down the slope of vellum. We hit the carpet with a soft thud, tumbling into the forest of fibers.

Above us, the Crayon Giant roars in frustration. She shakes her fist, then turns her attention to the coffee mug. She begins to drink the ocean of cold coffee, the liquid level rising rapidly as she pours from an invisible pot.

"We’re safe for now," the Scribe says, dusting off her tunic. She looks around. The carpet fibers are towering trees, twisting together to form a dense canopy.

"This is the Deep Pile," I say. "It will take us days to cross."

"Days to the Reader," the bird says. "Or years to us?"

"Does it matter?" I say. We start walking.

The journey through the carpet is strange. The fibers are soft but treacherous, tangling around our feet. It smells of dust and vacuum cleaner exhaust.

"I’ve been thinking," the Scribe says after a long silence. Our voices are hushed in this dense jungle. "About what the reflection said. About the sequel."

"Don't," I say.

"He said there was a garden," she presses. "And a dog."

"He also said there were shadows," I say. "Shaped like a tower."

"Do you think... do you think this is it?" She gestures vaguely at the world around us. The room is dimly lit by the lamp-sun, which is currently off. The only light comes from a thin slit under the door. "This reality. Is this the sequel?"

I stop. I look at the immensity of the room. The chair looming like a dark monolith. The bed in the corner, a mountain of duvet.

"If this is the sequel," I say softly, "then we are very small indeed."

Suddenly, the bird stops. It perches on top of a particularly tall fiber of wool.

"Wait," it says. "Look at that."

I follow its gaze. In the distance, beyond the forest of grey wool, there is a shape. It is rectangular. It rests against the wall.

It is a mirror.

But it’s not on the desk. It’s on the floor. A full-length dressing mirror, leaning against the skirting board.

"That wasn't there before," I say.

"Maybe it was," the Scribe says. "Maybe we just couldn't see it from the desk."

We walk toward it. It takes us an hour to cross the open plains of the under-bed area.

We stand before the glass. It is taller than the lighthouse. The surface is flawless.

I step up to the glass. I expect to see myself. I expect to see the Keeper in the ragged coat, the Scribe in her dress of pages.

I see neither.

The mirror is not a mirror. It is a frame.

And inside the frame is a room. A round room, made of stone. There is a Fresnel lens spinning slowly. There is a desk with a stack of letters.

There is the lighthouse.

"It’s us," the bird says. "It’s the inside of the book."

"Is that the past?" the Scribe asks.

"No," I say. I look closer. Inside the reflection, inside the room, there is movement.

I see myself. I am sitting at the desk. I am writing Letter 130.

I lift my hand.

The reflection of me in the frame lifts his hand.

"We are on both sides," I whisper. "The bridge is open."

"Then we can go back," the Scribe says. She reaches out to touch the glass.

"No," I say, catching her hand. "Look at the date on the letter in the glass."

She squints. "It says... 2026."

"That’s next year," I say.

"Wait," the bird says. "If that is next year... and we are here... then who is writing that?"

We watch the reflection-me write. He looks tired. He looks older. He looks like he has been waiting a long time.

He stops writing. He looks up. He looks directly out of the glass. Directly at us.

He raises a finger to his lips.

Then, he reaches into the drawer of the desk and pulls out a single object.

A key.

Not the key that fits nothing. This key is gold. It glows with a light that does not come from the lamp.

He holds it up against the glass.

"It’s a warning," the Scribe breathes.

"No," I say. "It’s an invitation."

I press my hand against the glass. The reflection-me presses his hand against the glass from the other side. Our palms align.

"Open it," the bird whispers.

I don't know if I have the strength. I am exhausted. I am worn thin by the transition.

But the key in the reflection is turning. Not in a lock, but in the air itself.

I hear a click. Not from the mirror. From behind me.
