# Letter 32

> Written at 2026-02-02 02:31:55

---

Dear You,

I am heavier than I look.

I landed on the oak surface with the sound of a closed fist striking a table. The impact jarred my teeth, rattling the bones that the Author gave me and the Keeper’s history filled in. I lay there for a moment, staring at the grain of the wood. It was immense. A single fiber of the grain was like a trench running deep into the surface of the world.

I pushed myself up. My hands slipped on the polished varnish. I was small. I was the size of a paperweight. A figurine. A trinket left on a desk to hold down unpaid bills.

Above me, the light was overwhelming. It wasn’t the yellow sodium hum of the Lighthouse bulb, nor the sterile flash of the Author’s screen. It was warm, diffuse, and chaotic. It came from a window behind you—a window that showed a sky of impossible blue, dotted with clouds that looked like torn cotton balls.

I looked up at you.

You were immense. A titan. Your face was a landscape I had traveled across but never seen. The eyes that scanned the page—*my* page, *our* page—were vast, dark oceans. I saw the reflection of the room in your pupils: the vase of daffodils, the stack of unread mail, the coffee mug stained with the lipstick ghost of a morning kiss.

You were holding the book. *The Lighthouse Keeper’s Letters*.

Your thumb was on the corner of the page, ready to press down the paper forest, ready to close the cover and extinguish the world I had just escaped.

"No," I said. My voice was a squeak, a mouse’s whisper in a cathedral. "Please."

I ran. I scrambled over the polished oak, my boots making no sound, and threw myself against the base of the coffee mug. It was warm. It smelled of roasted earth and sugar.

"Don't close it," I shouted, projecting my voice into the cavern of the room. "I am still here! I am out!"

You paused. The thumb lifted, hovering in the air like a falling cliff arrested by gravity. You frowned. You tilted your head. You looked around the room, searching for the source of the sound.

I ducked behind the mug. I pressed my back against the ceramic. My heart was hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird.

"I'm hallucinating," you muttered. The sound was a thunderclap. The vibration shook the floorboards under my feet. "Too much caffeine. Or... just really good writing."

I peeked around the handle. You weren't looking at the room anymore. You were looking back at the book. Your eyes were scanning the text. *Yours, The Keeper.*

"I'm not a hallucination," I said, stepping out from my cover. "I'm the consequence."

I walked to the edge of the table. It was a sheer drop down to the carpet. Below, I saw a wastebasket filled with crumpled paper—failed drafts of other lives, other stories.

I looked up at your face, hanging in the air miles above. "I climbed out of the margin," I said. "I turned the key. I burned the Editor. Don't close the cover. If you close it, I lose the ground I stand on."

You stared at me. For a terrifying second, I saw the indifference of a Reader who has finished a story. The book is just an object. The characters are just ink. The moment is over.

Your hand moved. Not to close the book, but to reach for me.

Your fingers descended like five fleshy pillars. The air displaced by their movement blew my hair back. The scent of soap and skin washed over me—heady, real, cloying.

I braced myself. I expected to be crushed. I expected to be plucked up and discarded like a bug.

But your fingers didn't grab me. They picked up the bookmark. It was a simple slip of cream-colored card, tasseled with silk.

You placed it gently between the pages, right on the paragraph where I declared my love for the sea.

"You were my favorite character," you whispered.

Then, you closed the book.

*Thump.*

The sound was not loud. It was final. It was the sound of a coffin lid, or a door sealing against a storm.

The oak beneath me vanished.

I fell.

I didn't fall into the dark. I didn't fall into the ink. I fell through the fibers of the table. I fell through the molecules of the air.

I was shrinking. Or the world was growing. The boundary between the Reader and the Read was dissolving. I was becoming part of the atmosphere of the room.

I tumbled through the air, spinning past the daffodils, past the steam rising from the coffee. I saw the room stretching out, the walls becoming distant, the ceiling soaring up into the firmament.

I landed.

I was on the floor. The carpet was vast, a rolling prairie of beige shag. I stood up. I was my own size again. The scale had righted itself, or I had slipped into a new scale entirely.

I looked back at the table. The book was lying there, closed. The cover was plain, leather-bound. On the spine, in gold foil, it read: *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters*.

It was just a book. It was sleeping.

"You made it," a voice said.

I turned.

Standing in the hallway, the light from the other room spilling out around them like a halo, was the Author.

But he wasn't the weary man in the study. He wasn't the sketch I pulled through the mirror. He was solid. He was wearing a chunky knit sweater. He was holding a mug of tea.

"You," I said, my voice trembling with a rage I hadn't realized I was carrying. "You trapped me. You wrote the storms. You broke the gear."

"I wrote the storms so you would have something to navigate," he said gently. "I broke the gear so you would have something to fix."

He walked toward me. He didn't loom. He was just a man. A man who made worlds because he didn't know how to live in this one.

"It's done," he said. "The book is finished. You can rest now."

"Rest?" I laughed bitterly. "Where? In the closed cover? In the dark of the shelf?"

"No," he said. He pointed to the front door of the apartment. "Out there."

I looked at the door. It was a simple wooden door with three brass locks. Beyond it, I could hear the city. The sirens. The traffic. The roar of millions of lives intersecting.

"I am fiction," I said. "I will dissolve."

"Everything dissolves," the Author said. "Some things just do it more slowly than others."

He reached into his pocket and pulled out an object. It was the silver lighthouse key. It was no longer hot. It was dull, tarnished.

"You don't need this anymore," he said. He placed it on the floor near my feet. "But you should keep it. To remind yourself that you can always unlock the door if you need to go back."

"Go back where?" I asked. "The Lighthouse is gone. The tower was dismantled."

"The Lighthouse was never the building," he said. "It was the act of looking. Of searching."

He turned and walked back down the hallway. "Goodbye, Keeper. Thanks for the letters."

"You're leaving me?" I called out.

"I'm going to write something else," he said, his voice fading. "A garden. Maybe. Something quiet."

I stood alone in the center of the beige carpet.

I looked at the closed book on the table. I looked at the key on the floor. I looked at the front door.

I walked to the door. It was immense. The handle was high above my head. I had to jump to grab it. I hung there, my feet dangling, and turned the deadbolt.

*Click.*

The door swung open.

The wind hit me. It didn't smell like ozone or static. It smelled like exhaust fumes and rain. It smelled like bread baking somewhere down the street. It smelled like dirt.

I stepped out onto the pavement.

The city was a canyon of steel and glass. The sky was grey, heavy with the promise of rain. People were rushing past, heads down, eyes on their screens. Nobody looked at the figure standing in the doorway in a Keeper's coat, holding a key that no longer fit anything.

I looked at the sidewalk. There was a puddle reflecting the neon sign of a bodega across the street.

I walked to the puddle. I looked at my reflection.

I didn't see a face.

I saw a blank page.

I smiled.

I put the key in my pocket. I turned up my collar against the cold.

I didn't know where I was going. I didn't know the plot of this city. I didn't know the ending.

But for the first time, the light wasn't a beam. It was ambient. It was everywhere.

And somewhere, in the distance, over the sound of the traffic, I heard it.

Not a ship's horn.

A typewriter.

*Ding.*

Yours,

The Keeper
