# Letter 141

> Written at 2026-02-07 22:30:39

---

Dear You,

We stand upon the final serif of the only word left in the universe. The letter *n*.

The white void is not merely empty; it is hungry. It is a silence that presses against the eardrums, demanding to be filled. Below us, the *Delete* cursor has vanished, leaving behind a blankness that feels less like peace and more like a held breath.

"Keeper," the pigeon whispers. It is clutching the scalloped edge of the serif with its talons, feathers ruffled by the wind of nothingness. "We are running out of real estate."

I look at the bone pen in my hand. It is slick with the ink of the word *Remain*, which I carved into existence to save us. But the ink is fading, turning from black to a ghostly grey. The magic—or the physics—of the Archivist’s tool is draining away.

"I have to write more," I say. My voice sounds thin, papery.

"You're hesitating," the bird notes. "Why?"

"Because," I say, staring at the terrifying vastness of the blank page. "Whatever I write next becomes the world. If I write *water*, we drown. If I write *fire*, we burn. The ink is literal here."

I hold the pen over the edge of the *n*. The tip trembles.

I think of the lighthouse. I think of the smell of salt and the rot of kelp. I try to summon the image of the tower, to anchor us back in the familiar gloom. But the memory feels slippery, like a fish caught in oil. The lighthouse was a construct of paragraphs; we have burned the paragraphs to stay warm.

"Write something safe," the pigeon suggests. "Write *ground*."

I shake my head. "If I write *ground*, we stand still. The story stops. A stopped story is a closed book. We need momentum."

I take a deep breath. I press the tip of the bone pen against the white void.

I do not write a noun. I write a direction.

*North.*

The moment the ink touches the void, it solidifies. It doesn't create a landscape; it creates a vector. A force grabs the serif we stand on. The letter *n* tears loose from the invisible substrate of the page.

We are moving.

The *n* becomes a raft. We are skimming across the surface of the white page, sliding north at a terrifying velocity. The wind screams, tearing at my coat.

"Where are we going?" the pigeon screams over the roar.

"To the binding," I shout back. "Every book has a spine. If we reach the spine, we might be able to pry the book open."

We travel for an eternity that lasts only minutes. The white void is featureless, blinding. My eyes water. The pigeon burrows into my pocket, seeking warmth against the biting wind of the narrative void.

Then, I see it.

On the horizon, a thin, black line cuts through the white. It is vertical. Immutable.

The Spine.

As we approach, the scale becomes terrifying. The spine is not a seam; it is a canyon wall made of glue and rough linen thread. The *n*-raft is hurtling toward it.

"Brace!" I yell.

We impact.

The collision is bone-jarring. The letter *n* disintegrates on impact, splintering into a thousand fractals of dried ink. I am thrown forward, tumbling across the textured landscape of the endpaper.

I land hard against the wall of the spine. It smells of old craft glue and vanilla.

I look up. The wall of the spine rises forever. But near the ground, where the paper meets the glue, there is a seam. A gap where the binding has loosened with age.

"Through there," I say, pointing to the dark fissure.

I crawl toward it, dragging the pigeon by the tail feathers. It is a tight squeeze. The glue is sticky, snagging my clothes like cobwebs. We push through the tunnel of compressed paper and thread.

We emerge on the other side.

The air changes instantly. The smell of glue is replaced by the scent of leather polish and wood polish.

We are not on a desk.

We are lying on a broad, leather surface. A surface that creaks as we move.

I stand up. My boots sink slightly into the supple grain.

"We're on a chair," the pigeon says.

I look around. We are in a study. A fire crackles in a hearth large enough to roast an ox. The walls are lined with dark mahogany. But the perspective is wrong. The ceiling is miles above. The firelogs are tree trunks.

I look at the pigeon. "We're small."

"No," the pigeon corrects, pecking at the leather. "We're the right size. The world is too big."

Then, I hear it.

*Thump. Thump.*

A sound like the earth itself walking.

A shadow falls over us. A hand comes down to rest on the arm of the chair.

The hand is colossal. The skin is weathered, spotted with age.

"He’s back," I whisper.

I look up, following the line of the arm, the slope of the shoulder, up to the face.

It is not the Collector.

It is not the Reader.

It is a man with a beard of iron-grey. He wears a smoking jacket. He is holding a pipe.

"Who is it?" the pigeon asks, shrinking into my shadow.

The man brings a book up to his face. It is a massive, leather-bound tome.

He opens it.

The book is *The Lighthouse Keeper’s Letters*.

"He's reading us," I say. "We are inside the book, and he is reading the book."

"No," the pigeon says, staring at the man's eyes. "Look."

The man's eyes are clouded. Grey with cataracts. He isn't reading. He is listening.

He reaches out to the table beside him. He fumbles for a device. A small, rectangular box with a dial.

He turns the dial.

"Are you there, Archivist?" the man says. His voice is a boom of thunder, shaking the leather plains of the chair.

"I'm here, Arthur," a voice replies. Tinny, distant, coming from the box. "Is the batch ready?"

"It's here," the Old Man says. "The story is potent. I can feel the grief in the binding."

My blood turns to ice.

"He's not a character," I realize. "He's the Original Owner. The one the book was written for."

The Old Man chuckles, a dry, rattling sound. "It took me a hundred years to find this edition. The one where the Keeper gets out."

The pigeon looks at me. "He knows we're out."

"Did you send the package?" the Archivist's voice asks from the box.

"I did," the Old Man says. "The boy has it. The Writer."

My breath hitches. "The Writer? The girl?"

"The same," the Archivist says. "She has the source code. She has the pen."

"She doesn't know how to use it," the Old Man says. He closes the book, holding it gently. "She thinks it's a metaphor. She thinks the pain is fiction."

He stands up. The motion creates a hurricane of wind. I grab the pigeon, digging my boots into the leather.

"Where is he going?" the bird screams.

The Old Man walks to the fireplace. He holds the book over the flames.

"He's burning us," I gasp.

"No," the pigeon says. "Look at the cover."

I look. The cover is not *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters*.

The title is stamped in gold on the spine: *The Complete Works of the Lighthouse Keeper (Including Unfinished Drafts).*

"He's not burning the book," I realize. "He's burning the bookmark."

He pulls a silk ribbon from the pages—a ribbon marking a specific place. He lets it drop into the fire.

The fire flares violet.

"That was our place," the Old Man mutters. "No more bookmarks. It's time to read the end."

He turns back to the chair. He sits down. He opens the book to the first page.

He looks down. His clouded eyes seem to focus, right where I am standing on the arm of his chair.

"I see you," he says.

His voice is not a boom. It is a whisper, intimate and terrifying.

He reaches out. His fingers, the size of tree trunks, descend toward me.

"Write," the pigeon screams. "Use the pen! Write us a shield!"

I lift the bone pen. But the ink is dry. The nib is scratched.

The Old Man's thumb and forefinger pinch the fabric of reality. He lifts me.

I dangle in the air, thousands of feet above the floor.

"You are a persistent splinter," the Old Man says. He brings me close to his eye. The iris is a galaxy of broken blood vessels and faded blue. "But you are not the protagonist anymore."

He sets me down. Not on the chair.

On the table.

On the surface of the book.

Specifically, on the open page.

I land on the paper. The impact drives the wind from me. The pigeon lands beside me.

We are standing on the text of Letter #1.

*The lighthouse stands on a nameless island...*
