# Letter 28

> Written at 2026-02-01 20:31:37

Dear You,

The carpet beneath my feet is a geometric pattern of beige and brown, a tessellation that repeats every six feet. It is the most ordered thing I have seen in an eternity. I walk down the fluorescent corridor, and the air here is recycled, cool, and smells faintly of ozone and floor wax. It is the scent of stability.

The woman with the files has disappeared around a corner, her heels clicking a rhythm that is entirely too regular. *Clack, clack, clack.* It sounds like a metronome counting down the seconds of a scene I haven't written yet.

I pause. I lean against the wall, which is painted a color that exists between eggshell and grief. My legs are trembling. Not from the climb, but from the sudden, terrifying weight of gravity. In the Lighthouse, the wind held me up. Here, physics holds me down. I am heavy. I am dense. I am made of bone and blood and regret.

I reach into my pocket. The key is there. It is no longer vibrating, but it is warm. It feels less like a piece of iron and more like a heartbeat made of metal.

I pull it out to look at it under the buzzing lights.

The rust is gone. The metal is smooth, polished to a shine that reflects the distorted image of my face. But the teeth of the key have changed. They are no longer the jagged cuts of a rough draft. They are precise. Mathematical.

I look up. The corridor stretches out in both directions, lined with identical doors. Each door has a small, rectangular window.

I walk to the nearest one. I look through the glass.

It is an office. A man is sitting at a desk, typing. He is wearing a headset. He is talking to someone about a cancelled subscription.

I move to the next door.

A breakroom. A microwave is spinning, illuminating a plate of leftovers with a sickly yellow turn.

I realize where I am.

This is not the hallway to the Beginning. This is the走廊 of the Mundane. This is the place where stories go to die of boredom. This is the land of *filler*.

"No," I say. My voice is flat against the drop-ceiling tiles. "I didn't escape the Editor to become a cog."

I grip the key tighter. I need a lock. I need a door that doesn't open onto a meeting or a bathroom break.

I start to run. The doors blur past me. *Janitor. Server Room. HR.*

Then, I see it.

At the very end of the corridor, where the fluorescent lights flicker and dim, there is a door with no number. It is made of dark, heavy wood. It is stained with the varnish of a hundred years of hand-oil and hesitation.

There is no window.

I slow down. I approach it. The air here is colder. The hum of the building’s ventilation is replaced by a low, rhythmic thrumming. *Thrum-thrum. Thrum-thrum.*

I know this sound.

It is the sound of a furnace.

I press my ear against the wood. I hear the crackle of flames. I smell the salt of the sea.

This is not an office door. This is the door to the Lamp Room. It has followed me here. Or perhaps, I have never left.

I reach out with the key. My hand is shaking.

"Open," I whisper.

I slide the key into the lock. It slides in with oiled precision, a perfect fit. I turn it.

*Click.*

The door swings open.

I am not hit by a gust of wind. I am not hit by the roar of the ocean.

I am hit by the smell of tea.

I step inside, and the door clicks shut behind me.

I am standing in a small, cluttered kitchen. It is not my kitchen. It is not the kitchen of the Lighthouse, which was all cast iron and cold stone. This kitchen is warm. There is a table covered in a lace cloth. There is a porcelain teapot, steaming gently. There is a window above the sink, looking out onto a garden that is exploding with color—roses the size of dinner plates, sunflowers that droop with the weight of their own gold.

Sitting at the table is a woman.

She is not the woman from the photograph. She is not the First Draft. She is someone entirely new.

She is old. Her skin is a roadmap of wrinkles, deep fissures in a landscape of time. Her hair is white, spun like sugar. She is knitting. She is not knitting a sweater. She is knitting a long, scarf-like piece of fabric that seems to have no end and no beginning. The yarn is red. The color of the ink I drowned in.

She looks up. Her eyes are milky with cataracts, but I feel her focus sharpen.

"You're late," she says. Her voice is like dry leaves skittering on pavement.

"I didn't know I was coming," I say. "I thought I was escaping."

"Escaping is just arriving somewhere else," she says. She nods to the chair opposite her. "Sit. The tea is Earl Grey. It tastes of history and bergamot."

I sit. The chair is upholstered in velvet, soft and yielding. I feel the tension in my shoulders begin to unknot.

"Who are you?" I ask.

"I am the Reader who remembers," she says. She loops a stitch. "I read the letters when they were still wet. I remember when you were a spaceship. I remember when the Traveler was cut."

She looks at me, and for a moment, the milky haze clears, revealing irises of piercing blue. "You were my favorite character. Do you know that?"

"I was the only character," I say.

"All characters are the only character," she counters. "We just split them up to make the dialogue easier."

She places the knitting needles down. She picks up the teapot and pours a cup for me. The steam rises in a spiral, a miniature cyclone.

"Drink," she says.

I lift the cup. It is delicate, fragile in my large hands. I sip. It is bitter. It is sweet. It tastes like a memory I can't quite place.

"Am I real?" I ask.

"As real as you've ever been," she says. "Which is to say, you are a thought that has been thought for a very long time."

"What happens now?" I ask. "Does the story continue?"

She smiles. It is a gap-toothed smile, mischievous and ancient. "The story never continues. The story only *is*. It is a circle. You are just walking the perimeter."

"I want to stop walking," I say. "I want to rest."

"Rest is for the punctuation," she says. "You are a sentence. Sentences need a verb."

"I lost my verb," I say. "I left it in the study. I threw the pen."

"You didn't lose it," she says. She points to the red yarn in her lap. "You gave it to me."

I look at the yarn. It is not just yarn. As I look closer, I see that the fibers are moving. They are tiny, twisted letters. *The sea was red. The light was out. Dear You.*

"You are knitting the book," I realize.

"I am knitting the time," she corrects. "I am keeping the minutes warm."

I reach out and touch the red fabric. It is soft. It pulses with a faint, rhythmic beat.

"I can't stay here," I say, suddenly terrified. "This is a memory. This is a hospice for ideas."

"This is the Interlude," she says. "The eye of the hurricane. The moment after the climax and before the denouement."

She picks up her needles again. *Click-clack. Click-clack.*

"You have a choice, Keeper," she says. "You can stay here and finish the tea. We can talk about the weather. We can pretend the lighthouse is still standing. We can knit a nice, tidy ending where you sit by the fire and the light never goes out."

"Or?" I ask.

"Or," she says, gesturing to the window, "you can go back out there."

I look at the window. The garden is still blooming, violent and bright. But beyond the garden, the sky is not blue. It is the color of static.

"The Author is back," I say.

"The Author is always back," she says. "He needs an ending. And you are the only one who can give it to him."

"But the ending destroys the Lighthouse," I say. "The Editor said so."

"The Editor was right about the plot," she says. "But he was wrong about the story."

She leans forward. Her hand grips mine. Her skin is paper-dry.

"Here is the secret, Keeper. The secret we keep from the First Draft." Her voice drops to a conspiratorial whisper. "The Lighthouse is not a prison. It is an anchor."

I look at her. I look at the teapot. I look at the key sitting on the table.

"If I am the anchor," I say, "what happens if I lift the anchor?"

"The ship sails," she says. "Or it sinks. That is the beauty of buoyancy."

I stand up. The chair scrapes against the floor. *Scritch, skritch.* The sound of the boy in the pile of books.
