Dear You,

The silver cork is heavy in my palm, heavier than the jar of light, heavier than the brass helmet, heavier than the guilt of a thousand unfinished chapters. It is cold, sweating a condensation that smells of ozone and ancient, stagnant time.

"It will not be silent," the Boy warns me. He is standing by the railing, watching the horizon where the ships are beating against the wind, their sails full of a storm that hasn't broken yet. "When you pull it, the world will scream."

"Let it scream," I say. My voice sounds foreign, distorted by the vibration of the lens. I am glowing so brightly now that I can barely see my own feet. I am a lantern filled with blood and bone. "It has been silent too long."

I carry the cork to the center of the room. The armor has dissolved into a pile of red dust, outlining the shape of a giant on the floor. The map—the one showing the island as a key, or perhaps the key as an island—lies atop the rust.

I look for the drain. The Boy said the lock is the water. I look down at the floorboards of the lamp room. They are iron, riveted tight.

But I am not looking for a hole in the floor. I am looking for a hole in the world.

I kneel. The pain in my knees is distant, a signal received from a galaxy far away. I press my hand against the map. The ink begins to run.

"The cork is not for the floor," I realize. "It is for the light."

I understand then. The ocean is a projection. It is the dream of the light. If I plug the source, I dam the flow.

I turn back to the cage. The Fresnel lens is spinning wildly now, a centrifuge of glass and light. I have to time this perfectly.

I climb into the cage. The gears shriek. The Boy shouts something, but the wind from the rotation snatches his words away.

I hold the cork aloft. I am looking for the aperture. The pupil. The place where the beam exits.

But there is no exit. The beam is infinite. The light is trapped in a loop of reflection.

"The reflection," I mutter. I look at the glass walls of the cage. I see my own face—gaunt, burning, terrified—staring back at me a thousand times. "I am the sea."

I am not keeping the light in. I am the one pouring the light out.

I look at the map on the floor again. The red ink drawing of the key. It isn't a picture. It is a blueprint.

"The drain," I say. "It's me."

I press the cork against my own chest. Right over the spot where the fire entered me.

It fits.

There is a hole there. A hole I never noticed. A hole where the Captain’s bullet went through, or the Writer’s pen went in, or the Lover’s absence left a void.

I push.

The resistance is tremendous. It feels like pushing a mountain uphill. The cork is fighting me. The water—the ocean, the waves, the spray—is pushing back, desperate to stay in. The sea loves its victims. It doesn't want to let them go.

"Elias!" the Boy screams. "The ships!"

I look through the spinning glass. The ships are rushing toward the lighthouse. They are fleeing the drain, fleeing the vacuum that is about to open. They are running aground on purpose, trying to reach the tower before the bottom falls out of the world.

"It's okay!" I shout. "You will sail!"

I shove the cork deeper.

My chest seals. The flow stops.

The light in the lens flickers. *DIES.*

The rotation slows. *Screech... grind... stop.*

The silence rushes back in. But it is not a peaceful silence. It is a held breath.

Then, the sound begins.

It starts as a gurgle. A wet, sucking sound from beneath the floorboards. Then a roar. Like a train rushing through a tunnel. Like a cave collapsing.

The floorboards dissolve.

The glass of the lantern room shatters, not outward, but inward, turning into sand.

I am falling. The Boy is falling. The machinery is falling.

We are dropping down the shaft of the lighthouse, but the lighthouse is gone. We are falling through a vertical tunnel of water.

The ocean is draining.

I tumble through the dark. The rush of water deafens me. I see things flashing past in the gloom—sunken cities, whale skeletons made of sentence structure, clocks melting in the current.

I hit the bottom.

It is not rock. It is soft.

I lie in the mud, gasping for air. The water level is dropping rapidly, rushing away toward some unseen exit at the edge of the horizon. The sea is receding faster than the tide has ever receded, pulling back to reveal the naked bones of the earth.

I look up. The sky is not the sky. It is the underside of the world. A vast, rippling surface where the sea used to be.

The Lighthouse is not here. The Lamp Room is gone. I am lying on the seabed.

The Boy lands next to me with a soft *thud*. He is covered in grey sludge.

"We did it," he says, coughing. "We drained the story."

I push myself up. The mud clings to me. I am still faintly glowing, a dying ember in the twilight of the bed.

"Where is the shore?" I ask.

The Boy points. "There."

In the distance, where the water used to meet the horizon, there is land. Not the nameless island. A different land.

It is a coast of jagged obsidian. And on that coast, there is a structure.

A single, solitary door. Standing upright in the landscape without a frame, without a wall.

The sun is rising, but it is a pale, anaemic sun, casting long, sickly shadows.

"We have to walk," I say.

"I'm tired," the Boy says.

"Take my hand," I say.

He does. His hand is small and cold.

We walk across the seabed. It is a graveyard of narratives. We pass the rotting hull of the *Flying Dutchman*, rotting into logs. We pass a fleet of bottle-letters, broken shards of glass glinting in the grey light.

As we walk, the light inside me flickers. The cork is holding, but the pressure is building. I can feel the ocean pounding against the seal in my chest, demanding to be let back in.

"It wants to drown us," I say.

"That's what oceans do," the Boy says. "They fill empty spaces."

We reach the obsidian shore.

The door is closer now. It is made of dark wood, bound in iron bands. It looks incredibly heavy.

There is no handle on this side.

I step up to it. I place my hand on the wood.

"Who are we writing to now?" the Boy asks. "The sea is gone. There is no lighthouse."

I press my ear against the wood.

I hear a voice.

*...I can't find the ending. It's like trying to catch smoke in my hands...*

It is the Writer. The Reader. The Author. She is on the other side.

"We are writing to her," I say. "We are the metaphor she needs."

I look at the Boy. "Do you have the key?"

The Boy shakes his head. "You are the key."

I reach into my coat. My fingers brush against the pocket where the key used to be. It is empty.

But wait.

My hand brushes against something else. Something hard. Metal.

I pull it out.

It is the ticket. The train ticket from the Rough Draft. *Destination: The White Page.*

The ticket is glowing. It is warm.

I look at the door. There is no keyhole. But there is a slot. A mail slot.

"Not the key," I whisper. "The postage."

I push the ticket into the slot.

For a moment, nothing happens.

Then, the wood begins to groan. The iron bands rattle.

The door does not open.

The door fades away.

It dissolves into mist, leaving a hole in the air. A portal.

Beyond the portal is not a room. It is a library.

But the shelves are empty. The books are gone.

In the center of the library stands a single desk. And at the desk sits a figure.

But it is not the woman. It is not the Author.

It is a mannequin. A dress-maker’s dummy, made of polished wood. It is wearing a dress made of stitched-together pages.

I step through the portal. The air here smells of dust and varnish.

The dummy turns its head. It has no face, just a smooth, blank oval.

"You are late," the dummy says. Its voice is the sound of pages turning. "The draft is over."

"Over?" I ask. "We drained the sea. We came to the shore."

"You drained the ink," the dummy corrects. "The story is dry. The characters have nowhere left to stand."

"I am still standing," I say.

"Barely," the dummy says. It lifts a wooden hand and points to the table.
