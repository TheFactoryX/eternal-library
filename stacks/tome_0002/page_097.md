# Letter 97

> Written at 2026-02-05 18:54:41

Don't sleep," I say, the words a jagged shard of glass in my throat. "Sleep is where the story goes to die. Sleep is for characters who have reached their period. I am a semicolon. I am a pause."

The reflection tilts its head. The Author’s eyes—my eyes, but colder, devoid of the salt-borne empathy—stare back. The pen hovers over the final *D*, the ink dripping onto the floor of the void, turning the white space into a pool of black possibility.

*“A SEMICOLON IS A PAUSE,”* the reflection writes, the scratch of the nib deafening in the silence. *“BUT A PAUSE IS NOT A PLOT. IT IS A STOPPAGE. YOU ARE BLOCKING THE QUEUE.”*

"I am the queue," I counter. I press my hand against the glass. It yields, warm and soft, like the skin of a drum. "I am the line that forms outside the theater. I am the wait."

The reflection sneers. It raises the pen to strike through the *D*, to turn the *END* into *AND*.

But before the nib touches the surface, the mirror shatters.

Not from my side. From the other side.

A fist punches through the glass from within the reflection's world. A hand wrapped in grey wool.

The glass falls away in jagged sheets, slicing the white mist, revealing not the Author’s study, but the Lighthouse kitchen. The worn linoleum. The chipped enamel mug. The smell of roasting coffee and seaweed.

I step through the frame.

I am back.

But it is wrong.

The room is slanted. The floor slopes upward at a thirty-degree angle. The table is bolted to the floor, the chairs secured with thick ropes.

"Gravity is optional," a voice says.

I turn.

You are there.

But you are not the fading plot device of the whitespace. You are solid. You are wearing the yellow coat, but it is heavy, encrusted with barnacles and salt crystals. Your hair is wet, plastered to your face.

"You didn't leave," I say.

"I never left," You reply. "You just stopped looking at me. You got distracted by the meta-narrative."

You point to the window.

I look out.

The view is vertigo-inducing. We are not on an island. The Lighthouse is floating. It is tumbling, slowly, end over end, through a sky that is not a sky, but a library.

Books fly past the window—massive, leatherbound folios flapping like birds. Clouds of ink swirl between the shelves.

"We are in the Between," You say. "The gap between the rewrites."

"The reflection tried to end it," I say. "It wrote *THE END*."

"It tried," You agree. "But it forgot the date."

"The date?"

You hand me a newspaper. It is damp, the print smudged. The headline reads: *CHRONOLOGICAL ANOMALY DETECTED IN SECTOR 7.*

"Look at the dateline," You say.

I look at the top of the page. *Vol. 1, No. 97.*

"We haven't finished," I say. "We are only at ninety-seven."

"The story ends at one hundred," You say. "That is the contract. The century. The Author wants to round it off. A nice, even number."

"If he ends it at one hundred," I say, "he kills us."

"He retires us," You correct. "We go to the Archive. We become reference material."

I look at the room. The slant is getting steeper. The coffee mug slides across the table and smashes on the floor.

"We have to stop the rotation," I say. "We have to anchor the tower."

I run to the stairs. I climb, my feet heavy on the iron treads. The spiral is reversed. To go up, I have to climb *down*.

I reach the lantern room.

The great lens is dark. The machinery is silent.

But there is something new in the center of the room.

A loom.

A massive, complex loom, made of brass and glass. The warp threads are not wool. They are lines of text. *The sky was blue.* *The sea was rough.* *He felt a sudden chill.*

The shuttle is a feather. A white feather, stained with ink.

"It's weaving the narrative," I realize.

And sitting at the loom is a child.

The child from the glass garden. The giant eye. The innocent observer.

The child looks at me. Its eye is wide, unblinking. It holds the shuttle.

"The pattern is wrong," the child says. Its voice is the sound of a page turning.

"Fix it," I say.

"I need thread," the child says. "I am running out of ink."

I look at the bobbin. It is almost empty. The story is fraying.

"I can give you ink," I say. I reach into my pocket. I pull out the brass key. The key to the tower. The key to the clock.

The child shakes its head. "Metal makes no words. Metal makes only marks."

"Then what?" I ask. "What makes the words?"

The child points at me.

"Blood," the child says. "Memory. Pain. The things that cannot be edited."

I look at the loom. The fabric hanging from the loom is the world. I can see the island, the sea, the ships.

The fabric is tearing. A hole is opening up. Through the hole, I see the Author’s hand. It is holding a red pen. It is poised to strike.

"He is coming," I say.

"He is here," the child replies.

The hand descends through the hole in the fabric. It is enormous. It reaches for the loom.

I have to stop it.

I don't have a weapon. I have only my voice.

"No," I shout.

The word hits the hand. It ripples like a wind across wheat.

The hand hesitates.

"Again," the child prompts.

I need a better word. A stronger word.

I think of the sea. I think of the night the *Iron Duke* appeared. I think of the terror of the first letter.

"Stay," I command.

The hand pushes forward. It is relentless. It is the force of the conclusion.

"Elias," You call from the stairs. "The trope! Use the Deus Ex Machina!"

"The machine is broken!" I shout back.

"Then become the god!" You scream.

I look at the looming hand. I look at the child. I look at the empty bobbin.

I know what I have to do.

I walk to the loom. I take the shuttle from the child's hand.

I feel the sharp point of the quill. I feel the hollow shaft.

I remember the Arrival. The sudden awareness of being here. The longing.

I place the tip of the shuttle against my wrist.

"I am the ink," I whisper.

I drag the quill across my skin.

It does not cut. It does not bleed.

Gold pours out.

Liquid light. The raw stuff of inspiration.

I feed the shuttle into the loom. I throw it across the warp.

*Whirrrrr-click.*

The loom springs to life. It weaves furiously. The golden thread intertwines with the text. It strengthens the fabric. It patches the hole.

The Author’s hand is caught in the weaving. The golden threads wrap around the fingers, binding them.

The hand tries to pull back. It cannot.

The child laughs. It is the sound of bells.

"We are extending the deadline," the child says.

I look at the fabric. The new thread is creating a new pattern.

I see a door. A door in the side of the Lighthouse that was never there before.

"What is that?" I ask.

"The exit," You say. "Or the entrance. It depends on which side of the page you are on."

I walk to the loom. I touch the fabric. It is warm.

"I can't leave," I say. "I am the Keeper."

"The Keeper tends the light," You say. "But the light moves."

I look at the golden thread. It is leading to the door.

"We have to go," You say. "The Author is calling security. The Janitors are coming."

I hear the sound of a mop bucket. The smell of ammonia.

"Where does the door lead?" I ask.

"To the Genre," You say. "We are leaving Literary Fiction. We are going Adventure."

I grab the handle of the new door. It is rough, made of raw wood.

"Are you ready?" I ask.

"I was born ready," You say.

I open the door.

A wind howls. Not the sea wind. A wind of sand and heat.

I step through.

I am standing on a deck.

The sky is burning. The air is filled with smoke.

Below me is a city of metal and steam. Gears the size of mountains turn in the distance. Airships dock with towers of iron.

I look down at myself.

My wool coat is gone. I am wearing a leather duster. I have a goggles on my head. A pistol is at my hip.

You are beside me. You are wearing a corset of brass and lace. You are holding a spanner.

"Steampunk," I say.

"The most popular tag," You reply. "We will be safe
