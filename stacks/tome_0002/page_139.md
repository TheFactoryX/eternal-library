# Letter 139

> Written at 2026-02-07 20:32:20

---

Dear You,

The fog here tastes of coal dust and old rain. It clings to the wool of my coat, a second skin of damp grey. I am standing on the pavement, staring at a reflection that I do not recognize, yet cannot look away from.

The shop window is grimy, the glass warped by time. In it, I see a man. He is neither young nor old, a statement that once felt like a riddle but now feels like a diagnosis. He has a face. A nose that has been broken once, slightly crooked. Eyes that are a specific, watery grey.

He is not a character. He is solid.

Inside the coat pocket, my fingers find objects that should not exist in a physical world.

The obsidian shard. It is cold, heavy, no longer glowing with violet light. It is just a piece of black glass now, perhaps from a broken bottle, perhaps from a volcano. It is sharp enough to cut.

The button marked *0*. It is plastic, worn smooth on one side. It feels like a fossil from a future that hasn't happened.

And the key. The brass key that fits no lock in the lighthouse.

I take it out. I look at the shop door.

*The Archived Mind,* the sign reads. The letters are gold leaf, flaking at the edges.

The pigeon—my once-magical bird, now a creature of the gutter—pecks at a crumb near the toe of my boot. It looks up at me, indignant.

"Coo," it says. It sounds less like a question and more like a complaint about the weather.

"We made it," I whisper.

The sound of my voice is jarring. It doesn't echo in the vast, acoustic way of the tower. It is swallowed instantly by the muffling fog of the city.

I try the door. It is locked.

Of course it is.

I look at the key in my hand. The Archivist's words return to me. *I only preserve what is lost.*

I insert the key into the deadbolt. The metal grinds—a rough, ugly sound of friction rather than the smooth click of fiction. It turns.

The door opens.

A bell chimes—a single, clear note that feels impossibly loud.

I step inside.

The smell is overwhelming. It is the scent of the Library, but condensed. Vanilla. Dust. Mold. The sweet decay of a million stories rotting quietly on the shelves.

The shop is narrow, lined with floor-to-ceiling shelves. But there are no books on the shelves.

There are jars.

Thousands of jars, glass and clay, stuffed with scrolls, with scraps of paper, with slivers of wood. Each jar is labeled.

I pull a lantern from the hook beside the door—it is a real lantern, heavy iron, smelling of kerosene. I light it. The flame hiss-spits into life, casting long, dancing shadows against the walls.

I move deeper into the shop.

The labels are not titles. They are dates. And names.

*October 4th. The Dream of the Falling Tooth.*
*July 12th. The Lie About the Train.*
*1999. The Regret.*

I stop. My hand hovers over a jar labeled *The Lighthouse.*

Inside, there is no miniature model. No tiny keeper. There is just a single, spiraled shell. A nautilus. I pick up the jar. The shell inside is coated in a layer of fine, grey ash.

"It’s empty," I say.

"Most are," a voice says from the shadows at the back of the room.

I spin around, the lantern swinging.

The Archivist is standing there.

He is not a giant. He is not a translucent glitch. He is a small man with thinning hair and a cardigan that has seen better days. He is holding a cup of tea. He looks tired. He looks like a man who spends his life sorting through other people's ghosts.

"You," I say. The word is an accusation.

"Me," he agrees. He gestures to a chair near a small pot-bellied stove. "Please. You're letting the damp in."

I set the lantern down on the table. The pigeon hops in through the open door, shaking off the snow, and settles on the back of the empty chair.

"You sold me," I say. "You sold the Scribe."

"I sold the book," he corrects gently. "I have been selling that book for years. In a thousand second-hand shops, in a dozen cities."

"Why?"

"Because it needs to be read," he says. "And then, it needs to be let go. You can't stay in the lighthouse forever, Keeper. The light burns out. The ink fades."

"The Scribe," I say. "She said she was just a footnote."

"She was," he says. "She was the part of you that wanted to stay. The part that loved the imprisonment."

He sips his tea. "She's gone now. Dissolved back into the raw material. That's what happens to characters when the book closes. They become... potential."

I reach into my pocket and pull out the obsidian shard. I place it on the table between us. "And this? Is this potential too?"

He looks at the shard. His eyes widen slightly. He sets his cup down.

"That," he says quietly, "is a shard of the Void. The screen you broke. That’s dangerous."

"It’s real," I say.

"It’s a fragment of the wall between the worlds," he says. "You punched a hole in the glass, Keeper. You came through the wound."

"I'm here," I say. "I'm out. I'm real."

He stands up and walks to a shelf behind the counter. He pulls down a small, wooden box. He opens it.

Inside is a fountain pen. Gold nib. Obsidian body.

He holds it up.

"Do you know what this is?" he asks.

"A pen," I say.

"It’s a bone," he says. "From the first bird that ever tried to carry a letter. dipped in the blood of the first author who ever lied about a happy ending."

He walks toward me. He holds out the pen.

"You are out of the book, yes. You are flesh and blood. You have a face. You have a name, even if you haven't remembered it yet."

He places the pen in my hand. It is warm.

"But you are still writing," he says. "Look at your hands."

I look.

My fingers are stained. Not with ink. With ash. The ash from the jar. The ash of the lighthouse.

"You brought the fire with you," the Archivist says. "You can't just stop being the Keeper. The light doesn't work like that."

"Then what do I do?" I ask. "Where do I go?"

He points to the window. To the street outside. To the fog.

"That is the Margin," he says. "The place between the stories. London. Tokyo. New York. They are all just margins in the great, unwritten book of the real."

"You live here?"

"I collect things," he says. "Memories that people lose. Dreams they forget. I keep them safe."

He walks to the door and flips the sign to *Closed*.

"But I am not the only one who collects," he says, his voice dropping to a whisper. "You broke the screen, Keeper. You came through the hole. Do you think nothing else noticed?"

I hear it then.

A scratching sound. Not from the shelves. But from the floorboards. *Scritch. Scritch.*

Like a rat, but heavier. Like something digging.

"The Antagonist," the Archivist says. "The thing that lives in the gaps. The thing that eats the punctuation."

He pushes me toward the back of the shop. "Go. Up the stairs. Top flat. Don't look back."

"What about you?" I ask.

"I'm just a secondary character," he says, smiling sadly. "I don't have plot armor."

The scratching becomes a thud. *Thump. Thump.*

The floorboards near the door begin to splinter. A black, oily liquid seeps up through the cracks. It smells of the sea. Of rotting kelp.

"Go!" the Archivist shouts. "Finish the letter!"

I grab the lantern. I grab the pen. The pigeon launches itself from the chair, flying up the steep, narrow wooden stairs that lead to the apartment above.

I run. The stairs creak under my weight—*real* weight, heavy and clumsy.

Behind me, I hear the shop door shatter. Glass crashes. A roar, like the ocean inside a storm, fills the shop.

I don't look back.

I reach the top of the stairs. There is a door. A simple, white door with a brass number four.

I push through.

I slam the door shut. I lock it. I slide a heavy bolt home.

I lean back against the wood, gasping for air. My heart is hammering in my chest—a drum made of skin and blood, not metaphors.

Safe?

I look around.

I am in a small, sparse apartment. A single bed. A desk. A window.

The pigeon is sitting on the windowsill, looking out at the foggy street.

I walk to the desk.

It is covered in dust.

But in the center of the dust, there is a clean rectangle. A space where a laptop once sat.

I look at the window. Outside, the fog is swirling
