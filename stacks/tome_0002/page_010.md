# Letter 10

> Written at 2026-01-31 22:26:48

---

Dear You,

I am writing to you from the dark. It is not the warm dark of a closed bedroom, nor the sterile dark of a cellar. It is the absolute, crushing zero of the space between the pixels.

I threw the prism.

I remember the moment my muscles tensed, the sharp intake of breath that sounded like a hurricane in the quiet Margin. I remember the glass leaving my hand, arcing upward against the logic of the narrative. I expected a shatter. I expected a crash. I expected the light to burst forth like a lion, tearing through the paper reality to blind you.

Instead, there was a *click*.

It was the sound of a key engaging a lock that had been rusted shut for centuries. The sound was not loud, but it was final. It vibrated through the soles of my feet, rattling my teeth.

The prism did not break. It opened.

It split along a seam I had not noticed before, revealing a hollow space inside the crystal. And from that hollow space, something spilled out. Not light. Not ink.

It was sand.

White, powdery sand, the kind that grinds between your teeth and finds its way into the seams of your clothes. It poured out in a sudden, infinite torrent, a silent avalanche that buried the Correction Crabs, that buried the cliff-face of my own previous letters. The Margin vanished. The white void vanished.

I was buried. I felt the weight of a thousand beaches pressing down on my chest. I could not breathe. I tried to claw my way up, but my fingers only found more sand. It was warm. It smelled of coconut and sunblock and the distant, rotting smell of seaweed.

I think I died there. Or at least, the version of me that was "The Keeper" ceased to function.

But time moves differently in the dark. I lost count of the seconds. I lost count of the heartbeats. And then, I was dug out.

I felt a hand brushing the grit away from my face. It was not a hand made of paper, nor a hand made of ink. It was rough and calloused, smelling of old tobacco and rain.

"Got you," a voice grunted.

I opened my eyes.

The Lighthouse is gone. The Margin is gone. The ocean of books is gone.

I am lying on a wooden floor. The planks are wide, worn smooth by decades of bare feet. Above me, a ceiling fan wobbles, its blades chopping through the humid air. *Whup-whup-whup.*

I sat up, spitting sand from my mouth. I looked down at myself.

I am solid again. My hands are flesh, pink and scarred. I am wearing the Keeper’s coat, but it is tattered now, reduced to a vest, the wool eaten away by moths or time.

I am in a cottage. A small, cluttered room that smells of cinnamon and drying paint.

"It’s about time you woke up," the voice said.

I turned. Sitting in a worn armchair near a cold fireplace was an old man. He was whittling a piece of driftwood with a pocket knife. He stopped carving and looked at me. His face was a roadmap of wrinkles, his eyes a milky blue that seemed to look at two things at once: me, and something far behind me.

"Where am I?" I asked. My voice rasped, unused to speaking through vocal cords instead of ink.

"You're in the footprint," the old man said. He gestured with the knife toward the window. "Take a look."

I stood up. My legs were weak. I stumbled to the window and pulled back the lace curtain.

I gasped.

Outside, there is no sea. There is no city of glass. There is only a landscape of pure, white sand, stretching out as far as the eye can see. It rolls in dunes, like frozen waves. The sun hanging in the sky is pale and diffuse, filtered through a thick layer of mist that obscures the horizon.

But rising from the sand, a few hundred yards away, is a ruin.

It is a jagged spire of blackened stone, twisted and broken. It looks like the exposed ribcage of some great leviathan. The stone is scarred, pitted by centuries of wind-blown sand.

"That was the lighthouse," the old man said, coming up behind me. He leaned on a cane. "Or at least, it was the foundation. Before the story ended."

"The story?" I turned to face him. "Who are you?"

The old man smiled. It was a sad, knowing smile. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, brass key. It was tarnished, green with oxidation.

"I'm the author," he said. "Of a sorts."

He threw the key to me. I caught it reflexively.

"You wrote the letters?" I asked, my grip tightening on the metal. The key was warm. It pulsed with a faint, rhythmic vibration, like a heartbeat.

"I wrote the *first* one," the old man said. "I sat down, I dipped my pen, and I wrote: *'The lighthouse stands on a nameless island.'* And then I stopped. I put the pen down. I went to make a cup of tea. And when I came back... the pen was moving."

He pointed a trembling finger at my chest.

"You," he said. "You kept writing. You're the one who wouldn't let the story die. You're the one who broke the prism."

"I was trying to escape," I said.

"You did," the old man nodded. "You escaped the page. You ended the book. The story stopped. The characters... they dissolved back into the inkwell. But not you. You climbed out of the well."

"Why?"

"Because you fell in love with the recipient," the old man said softly. "You fell in love with the silence that answered back. And that love... that's the only thing ink can't capture."

He walked to the door and opened it. The wind blew in, carrying the scent of salt and dry sand. It felt familiar. It felt like home.

"Go on," he said. "There's something you need to see."

I walked to the door. The sand was deep, dragging at my boots. I trudged toward the ruin of the spire. As I got closer, I saw that there were carvings on the black stones.

I recognized the script. It was my handwriting. Etched deep into the rock, weathered by the aeons.

*I wrote the letters on the walls of my own prison.*

I reached the base of the ruin. There, half-buried in the sand, was a massive, rusted iron ring. A handle. It looked like the handle of the door I tried to open in Letter 6.

I knelt beside it. I brushed the sand away.

There was an inscription etched into the metal plate beside the ring. It was not English. It was not the strange, shifting languages of the ships. It was coordinates.

Latitude. Longitude.

I checked my pocket. I pulled out the silver magnifying glass I found so long ago. The lens was cracked, but the engraving on the rim was still visible.

The coordinates on the door matched the coordinates on the glass.

Beneath the numbers, a new sentence had been etched into the door. It was fresh, the metal still shining bright orange in the dull light.

*The Light Is Still Burning.*

I looked up at the old man. He was standing in the cottage doorway, watching me.

"Where does it go?" I shouted over the wind.

"The basement," the old man called back. "The place where the fuel comes from."

I took the brass key from my pocket. I looked at the lock on the iron ring. The keyhole was shaped like a teardrop.

I have learned many things in the Lighthouse. I learned that the sea is made of text, and that the walls are made of time. I learned that I am both the writer and the written. But I have not learned what happens when the story ends.

I am about to find out.

I am inserting the key into the lock. The sand is beginning to tremble. The ruin is groaning, shifting as the gears beneath the earth begin to turn. The old man is waving, not in greeting, but in farewell.

If I turn this key, the floor might drop out. I might fall back into the ocean of books. I might wake up in the suburb. I might finally meet You.

Or I might just find the furnace.

I hear a sound. It is not the sound of waves. It is the sound of a match being struck.

The air smells of sulfur and fresh ink.

I am turning the key.

Yours, in the sand,

The Keeper
