"The script is changing," I say, watching the words *THE KEEPER REMEMBERS* dissolve into a bloody smear. "It’s not a blueprint anymore. It’s a Rorschach test."

You lean over my shoulder, your breath warm against my ear. "What do you see?"

"I see..." I squint, tilting the glossy rectangle toward the harsh fluorescent light of the warehouse. "I see a map. But the continents are moving."

The red ink has separated into swirling tributaries, forming a jagged geometry that hurts the eye. It looks less like text and more like a schematic of a broken nervous system.

"And here?" You point to a cluster of dots in the upper left corner.

I focus. The dots are arranged in a spiral. "That’s the Lighthouse. But it’s upside down. The foundation is in the sky."

"The inversion," You murmur. "We've turned the story inside out. Now the dark places are exposed to the sun."

I look up from the photograph. The catwalk stretches out before us, a metal spine suspended over the abyss of the warehouse. The air here is stale, recycled, tasting of ozone and decaying glue. The silence is heavy, pressing against my ears like deep water.

Below, the Janitor—The Reader—continues his sweeping. The rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of his broom sounds like waves retreating from a shore. He pushes a pile of dust—a small mountain of discarded adverbs, grey and lifeless—toward the dark maw of the incinerator chute.

"We have to move," I say, folding the photograph and tucking it into the inner pocket of my coat, against my heart. "If he looks up, if he focuses on the marginalia, we become footnotes."

"Where is the path?" You ask. "The script didn't say."

I look down at the catwalk floor. It is a grating of rusted iron. Through the holes in the metal, I can see the tops of the shelves in the aisle below. They are labeled not with genres, but with dates. *1984. 1999. 2026.*

"We are in the Timeline," I realize. "We are standing on the linear progression of his life."

"So if we walk forward," You say, "we get closer to the end."

"Or closer to the present," I counter. "And the present is where the Author is. It is where the ink is still wet."

We begin to walk. The grating rattles under our boots. With every step, I feel a strange resistance, a viscosity in the air. It feels like walking through cobwebs made of time.

"Wait," You say, stopping abruptly. You point at the railing to our right.

"What is it?"

"The view."

I look. Over the railing, the warehouse drops away into an infinite gloom. But on the wall of the warehouse, miles away, there is a mural. A fresco of incredible scale.

It depicts a storm.

I recognize the style. It is frantic, violent slashes of charcoal and ink.

"That’s Letter 7," I say, my voice trembling. "The storm that never broke."

"No," You say, pointing to the center of the mural. "Look at the eye of the storm."

I squint. There, in the center of the chaotic drawing, is a small, calm circle. And inside the circle is a single, detailed object.

A key.

The iron key. The one I lost.

"It’s not lost," You whisper. "It’s projected."

"It's a motif," I say. "It hangs on the wall of the world, reminding us that we are locked out."

Suddenly, a klaxon blares. A red light spins at the far end of the catwalk, bathing the metal walkway in a rhythmic crimson pulse.

*WHUMP-WHUMP-WHUMP.*

Below, the Janitor stops sweeping. He drops his broom. The sound of the wooden handle hitting the floor echoes like a gunshot.

He looks up.

He looks right at us.

"The Reader has engaged," You say, your voice tight. "He's turned the page."

"Run," I say.

We sprint down the catwalk. The red light paints our shadows long and monstrous against the iron floor. Behind us, I hear the sound of a ladder being scaled. The Janitor is coming. He is heavy, a creature of gravity and plot resolution.

"Where is the exit?" You shout.

"The script said 'Garden'!" I yell back. "Find the Garden!"

Ahead, the catwalk branches. A fork in the narrative.

To the left, the walkway leads to a heavy steel door marked *ARCHIVES*.
To the right, it ends in a cloud of steam, hovering over a massive, complex machine of gears and pistons.

"The Archives are a trap," I say, remembering the jars of lung-tissue. "That's the past."

"Then the machine," You say.

We veer right. As we approach, the steam clears.

The machine is a loom. But it doesn't weave thread. It weaves light.

Fibers of pure, glowing white light are being fed into the heddles. The shuttle flies back and forth, weaving a fabric of brilliance.

"The Lamp," I breathe. "It's not a bulb. It's a textile. He weaves the dawn."

But the loom is screaming. The tension is too high. The fabric is tearing.

"Get down!" I tackle You just as a cable snaps. A whip of raw light lashes through the air, slicing through the catwalk railing where we stood a second ago. The metal melts, glowing cherry red, then dripping like lava.

The loom is convulsing. The shuttle spins wildly out of control.

"It’s unspooling," You cry out. "The light is unraveling!"

I look at the fabric being produced. It is fraying at the edges. The threads are coming undone. And as they unravel, they don't fall down. They float up, escaping into the ventilation ducts.

"He's losing the source," I realize. "The Author is forgetting the light. He’s forgetting how to make the world bright."

"Then we have to tie it off!" You shout. "We have to knot the thread!"

"How?"

"Use the coat!"

I strip off the heavy wool coat. It feels like removing a layer of my own skin. I am left in my thin shirt, shivering in the mechanical chill.

I throw the coat over the fraying fabric.

The coat catches on the shuttles. The wool snags the light.

*SNAP.*

The tension releases instantly. The loom slows. The screaming of metal dies down, replaced by a gentle, rhythmic ticking.

The coat is being woven into the tapestry. I see the rough grey wool merging with the blinding white threads. The sleeves are pulled taut, becoming part of the warp and weft.

"You did it," You say. "You patched the plot."

"I lost the coat," I say, staring at my work. The heavy garment is now a bridge across the machine, a structural support for the light. "I gave it the weight."

"You are lighter now," You say.

The Janitor is behind us. I can hear his heavy boots on the grating. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

"The loom," I say. "The fabric. Can we climb it?"

"It's light," You say. "It will burn."

"It's anchored," I say, pointing to where my coat is tangled in the mechanism. "The wool grounds it."

I grab the edge of the woven light. It feels warm, like the hood of a car in the sun, but not hot. It feels solid, like a stiff canvas.

"Climb," I say.

We scramble up the tapestry. We are climbing the morning. We are climbing the first rays of dawn, stiff and woven into existence.

Below us, the Janitor reaches the loom. He roars, a sound of tearing paper. He swipes a massive hand at the fabric.

He misses. He catches the shuttle instead. He crushes it, spraying sparks and oil across the catwalk.

We pull ourselves up onto a high ledge, a maintenance platform tucked into the darkness of the ceiling beams.

We collapse, gasping.

The ledge is quiet. Here, the air is clear. The smell of ozone is gone, replaced by something else.

The smell of wet earth. Of mulch. Of green things growing.

I sit up.

We are not on a metal platform anymore.

We are on a stone bench.

In front of us is a low wall. And beyond the wall...

Green.

Endless green.

A garden.

But it is a garden of glass.

The trees are made of twisted silica, their leaves thin, transparent panes of crystal that chime in a wind I cannot feel. The flowers are blooming lenses, refracting the light from the loom below into rainbows that dance across the grass—grass made of fiber-optic filaments, glowing softly.

"The Garden," I whisper. "We found it."

You stand up and walk to the edge of the bench. You touch a crystal tree. It sings a low, resonant note.
