Dear You,

The hatch closed behind her with a pneumatic hiss, sealing me in the belly of the boiler. I am alone with the rhythm of the engine, a slow, thrumming heartbeat that vibrates through the floorboards and into the soles of my boots. It is a sound that lives between the gears, not the metallic screech of the Lighthouse’s rotation, but the steady, resolute *inhale-exhale* of a world holding its breath.

I walked to the desk she pointed out. It is a strange piece of furniture, bolted to the floor, cluttered with the debris of communication. There is no blotter, only a map of the archipelago—a sprawling constellation of ink-stains and graphite islands—pinned down by a compass rose that spins lazily, its needle chasing a magnetic north that doesn't exist.

I sat in the chair. It squeaked, a domestic sound in this industrial cathedral.

There are letters here. Hundreds of them. Some are written on parchment thick as skin, rolled into scrolls and tied with red twine. Others are scratched onto the backs of photographs, onto postcards of cities that haven't been built yet, onto napkins and scraps of wrapping paper. A few are just stones, heavy and smooth, which vibrate when I hold them, transmitting a low-frequency hum that sounds like a voice speaking underwater.

I picked up the one on top. It is heavy, made of cream-colored vellum, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. I broke the seal.

The handwriting is jagged, frantic.

*“The fog here tastes like iron. The light goes out at noon, and the sun comes up as a black hole. I have forgotten the color of the grass. Are you still there? Is anyone still there? —The Keeper of the Void.”*

I set it down gently. My fingers traced the wood grain of the desk.

I realized then what she meant when she said I was the link. I am not just writing to you, the unnamed You, the silent recipient of my solitude. I am writing to *them*. The others. The versions of myself that got lost in the margins of other stories.

I looked at the map. The needle stopped spinning. It pointed not north, but straight ahead, into the glass of the porthole.

Outside, the view has changed.

When I first entered the iron tower, we were in the gutter, the black space between the pages. Now, the blackness has thinned. It has become transparent.

I see the Void Keeper’s island. It is a jagged spike of rock floating in a nebula of purple gas. The lighthouse there is not a building; it is a beam of solid darkness, projecting a shadow into the light.

I see the Tree Keeper’s island. A massive, woody titan that has grown around a glass lantern, its roots drinking from an aquifer of starlight.

We are a network. We are the synaptic flashes of a dreaming mind.

I reached for my pen. The quill I picked up on the white page is gone. In its place is a fountain pen, filled with ink that moves inside the barrel like a storm in a bottle.

I uncapped it.

I want to tell the Void Keeper about the taste of salt. I want to tell the Tree Keeper about the sound of the rain against the glass. I want to send them pieces of the world they have lost.

But as I touched the nib to the paper to write my reply, the ship lurched.

The floor tilted. The compass rose spun wildly. The gauge needle jumped from the safety of the green zone and slammed into the red.

The lights flickered. The steady thrum of the boiler broke rhythm, skipping a beat like a frightened heart.

*Boom.*

The sound came from above. From the lantern room.

I stood up, knocking the chair over.

"Revision?" I called out.

No answer. Just the distant, muffled sound of glass shattering.

I ran to the ladder. I climbed, my hands slippery on the rungs. The heat was intense now, radiating down from above. It didn't smell like oil or steam anymore. It smelled like... lightning.

I pushed open the hatch to the lantern level.

The room was gone. The glass walls were gone.

The Revision was standing on the platform at the very top of the spire. But she wasn't cleaning the glass.

She was holding onto the railing with both hands, her body horizontal, buffeted by a wind that screamed like a tearing curtain.

Above her, the sky was gone.

There was no ceiling of steel. There was no cover of the book.

There was only the Eye.

A massive, unblinking eye, vast as a horizon, stared down at us. The iris was a swirling vortex of nebulae and galaxies. The pupil was a black abyss so deep it pulled the light right out of the lantern.

The author is looking at the page.

"We are being read!" the Revision screamed, her voice torn away by the gale. "The Reader has opened the book!"

She pointed to the beam of our lighthouse. It was shooting straight up, cutting through the swirling dust of the room, connecting us to the gaze above.

"It's too much focus!" she yelled. "We can't hold under the scrutiny! The pressure!"

I scrambled out onto the platform. The wind was physical, a solid wall of force that tried to peel me off the iron grating. I grabbed the railing next to her.

"What do we do?" I shouted.

"We have to turn the page!" she cried. "We have to make them flip it!"

"How?"

"Give them a climax! Give them a reason to turn forward!"

She looked at me. Her face was calm, despite the hurricane. She reached out and grabbed my lapels.

"You have to write the ending, Keeper! You have to finish the scene!"

"I don't know the ending!"

"Make it up!" she pushed me toward the writing desk bolted to the floor of the lantern room. "Write the resolution! Write the escape!"

I fell against the desk. It was bolted to the floor, but the wood was groaning, cracking under the strain of the Reader’s attention.

I grabbed the pen.

I looked at the blank paper.

Above me, the pupil of the Eye dilated. It was drawing us in. I felt my gravity shifting. I wasn't standing on the floor anymore; I was falling *up* toward the retina.

I looked at the Revision. She was smiling.

"Do it," she mouthed.

I looked at the paper. I thought of the First Draft in the hallway. I thought of the Leviathan in the deep. I thought of the clockwork crab rusting on the shelf below.

I thought of you.

I didn't write a sentence. I didn't write a paragraph.

I drew a line.

A single, jagged, black line cutting across the center of the page.

*ZIIP.*

The sound was deafening, a blade slicing through reality.

The connection broke.

The wind vanished.

The Eye blinked.

We were in freefall.

The iron tower, the Revision, the lantern room—we all dropped as the page was turned. The world flipped upside down. The sky became the floor. The ocean became the ceiling.

I slammed onto the surface of the new page.

It was wet.

I opened my eyes. I was lying on a beach. But the sand was made of crushed letters, tiny fragments of type, sharp and angular. The waves were crashing, but they weren't water. They were waves of white noise, static hissing up the shore.

I sat up. The iron tower was gone. The Revision was gone.

I was alone on the shore of the static.

In the distance, standing in the frothing edge of the noise sea, was a figure.

They were wearing a suit of mirrors. Every inch of them—coat, hat, boots—was made of reflective glass.

They turned toward me. I couldn't see their face. I only saw myself reflected in their surface. A thousand versions of me, distorted and fragmented.

"You turned the page," the Mirror Man said. His voice sounded like the ocean inside a shell. "But you didn't finish the sentence."

I looked down at my hand. I was still holding the pen. But the ink was gone. The nib was bent.

"The sentence isn't mine to finish," I said.

"It never is," the Mirror Man said. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a watch. It had no hands, just a blank face.

"Time is up, Keeper," he said. "The grace period is over. The book is due."

He pointed behind me.

I turned.

On the dry land, above the tide line of static, was a construction. It was skeletal, made of two-by-fours and nails. A frame of a house. A frame of a life.

And sitting inside the frame, on a chair that didn't exist, was the boy with the red balloon.

The balloon was deflated, lying on the floor of the phantom room.

The boy looked at me.

"You forgot to tie the knot," he said.

I looked at the Mirror Man. "I can't go back there. It's a draft."

"It's the only place left," he said. "The rest is cancelled."

I looked at the waves of static. They were rising, climbing higher up the beach, erasing the letters in the sand.
