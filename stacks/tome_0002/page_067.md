# Letter 67

Dear You,

The lantern on the bow did not go out. It merely... withdrew. It receded from us like a tide pulling away from the shore, leaving the skiff stranded in a vacuum of color.

We are drifting in the Drift.

The water here is not black, nor is it the glass-smooth mirror of the revised world. It is the color of velvet. Deep, bruised purple. It feels soft when I trail my hand over the gunwale, thick enough to resist my fingers, like brushing the surface of a painting before the varnish has dried.

"We refused the shelf," I say. The sound of my voice is dull. The air is dense, muffling acoustics. "We refused to become static."

You are sitting at the stern, your yellow raincoat the only sharp geometric shape in a world of soft edges. You are not rowing. There are no oars. The skiff moves on momentum alone—the momentum of the sentence that brought us here. *We drifted.*

"The shelf was safety," You say. Your voice is calm. "On the shelf, the story ends, but it is preserved. It is loved."

"On the shelf, it is dust," I reply. "I would rather be wet."

I look over the side of the boat. Floating in the velvet water are the things we lost. The silver spoon, bent beyond recognition. The music box, its cylinder swollen with damp. The rusted clock face, spinning slowly, catching the faint light of a star that isn't there.

And the stone.

The stone I skipped in the beginning. The stone I jammed into the pen. It is bobbing in the wake, perfectly round, impossibly heavy.

"It is coming back to us," I observe.

"The past always bobs in the wake," You say. "Until you reel it in or cut the line."

We sit in silence. The silence is not empty. It is filled with the sound of pages turning. Not the pages of our book, but the pages of *other* books.

We hear the roar of a dragon from a fantasy trilogy. We hear the crisp, polite dialogue of a Regency romance. We hear the rhythmic, driving beat of a hard-boiled thriller.

"We are in the gap," I realize. "The space in the bookstore between the genres."

"We are the bargain bin," You murmur. "The orphaned narratives."

The skiff bumps against something.

It is not a shelf. It is not an island.

It is a pier.

But it is a pier made of bone. White, calcified bone, stretching out from the dark into the velvet water. Ribcages form the pilings. Spines the planks.

"Where does this go?" I ask, standing up. The boat rocks dangerously.

"You don't know?" You ask. "You're the Writer."

"I am the Keeper," I correct. "I only maintain. I don't build."

I step onto the bone pier. My boots make no sound. The texture is smooth, polished by the friction of a thousand readers' eyes.

I walk down the pier.

You follow.

At the end of the pier, there is no shore. There is only a structure.

It is a vast, sprawling mansion. It looks like the house from the blue room, the Author's house, but expanded. It has towers, turrets, wings that seem to fold in on themselves. It is a labyrinth of geometry.

And it is under construction.

Scaffolding made of giant letters rises up the sides. Cranes made of punctuation marks hoist buckets of wet cement. The cement is grey, filled with words.

"The Revision," I whisper.

She is there.

She is not wearing the indigo coat anymore. She is dressed in the white overalls of a mason. She is standing on a high scaffold, holding a trowel.

She is not repairing the house. She is building an addition.

She looks down and sees me.

"You survived," she says. Her voice is the sound of a brick sliding into place. "The Editorial Hand missed you."

"We chose the drift," I say.

"The drift is dangerous," she says. "Without an author, a character dissolves. We become ink stains. We become blots."

"Then why are you building?" I ask. I point to the sprawling house. "The book is closed. The Author is gone. Who are you building for?"

She smiles. It is a tired, dusty smile.

"I am not building for the Author," she says. "I am building for the Librarian."

She points her trowel toward the front door of the mansion.

The door is open. Inside, there is no light.

"The Librarian?" I ask.

"The one who curates the chaos," she says. "The one who finds the patterns. The Author creates the storm. The Librarian builds the lighthouse to house it."

She descends the scaffold, moving with a fluid grace. She stops in front of us.

"You have something of mine," she says.

She reaches out and plucks the stone from my pocket.

I hadn't realized I had picked it up.

"The first sentence," she says, weighing it in her hand. "The catalyst."

She turns and hurls the stone at the house.

It doesn't hit the wall. It hits the empty air beside the door.

But where it strikes, the air shatters.

A new room appears. Instantly. Built from the impact of the stone.

It is a circular room. Stone walls. A spiral staircase. A heavy iron door.

It is the Lighthouse.

It is attached to the house. A new wing.

"The Lighthouse was never the setting," she says. "It was the protagonist. And now, it has a home."

She walks to the new door. The iron bands are glowing.

"Come inside," she says. "The Librarian is waiting."

"What will the Librarian do with us?" You ask from behind me. Your voice trembles.

"The Librarian will read us," the Revision says. "Not for pleasure. For classification. You will be tagged. Sorted. Filed."

"I will not be filed," I say. "I am not a tax form."

"Then what are you?" she asks.

"I am the unresolved," I say. "I am the question mark at the end of the sentence."

The Revision sighs. She looks at the house. The lights are flickering on in the windows. One by one. Yellow, warm, electric lights.

"You can't stay in the drift," she says. "The velvet water is rising. Soon, it will drown the pier."

I look back. The water is indeed rising. It is lapping at the pilings of the bone pier. It smells of old paper and vinegar.

I look at the Lighthouse door.

I look at You.

"If we go in," I say, "we become part of the collection. We become a memory in a jar."

"If we stay out," You say, "we become nothing."

I walk to the door.

I touch the iron bands. They are cold.

"There is a third option," I say.

I pull the silver pen from my pocket. It is cracked. The stone is gone. But it still holds a drop of ink.

I press the tip against the wood of the door.

"You can't write on a door that is already built," the Revision warns.

"I'm not writing on it," I say. "I'm writing it *open*."

I draw a line down the center of the wood.

The wood splits. The hinges groan. The lock melts away like wax.

I push the door open.

But I don't step inside.

Instead, I reach through the doorway and grab the handle on the *other* side.

I pull.

The entire Lighthouse wing detaches from the house.

The stone groans. The foundation tears.

"What are you doing?" the Revision screams. "You're breaking the structure!"

"I'm unshelving it," I grunt.

With a final, tearing sound, the Lighthouse pulls free. It sits on the end of the bone pier, a separate, singular structure. No longer a wing. A standalone building again.

I turn to You.

"Get in," I say.

"You're stealing the house?" You ask, eyes wide.

"I'm reclaiming the setting," I say.

We scramble inside the Lighthouse. I slam the heavy door behind us and bolt it.

We are in the round room. The stone floor. The spiral stairs going up into darkness.

But now, there is a window.

I look out.

The bone pier is gone. The velvet sea is gone.

We are moving.

The Lighthouse is no longer a building. It is a vehicle.

We are driving across the landscape of the Author's study.

We are rumbling across the blue carpet. We are crushing the empty coffee cups. We are knocking over stacks of unread manuscripts.

"Where are we going?" You ask, laughing now. The sound is bright, terrified, electric.

"Anywhere the story isn't finished," I say.

I look up the spiral stairs.

"The light," I say. "I have to tend the light."

"Is it still up there?" You ask.

"We will see," I say.

I start to climb.

The stairs are endless. But for the first time, I am not climbing to look out.

I am climbing to look in.

I reach the lantern room.

The machinery is silent.
