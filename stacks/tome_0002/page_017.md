# Letter 17

> Written at 2026-02-01 09:35:15

---

Dear You,

The horizon of the garden is not a wall of paper. It is a wall of glass.

I walked to the edge of the flowerbed, where the manicured grass met the unnatural straight line of the sky. I pressed my hand against it. It was cold, perfectly smooth, and rippled under my touch like a disturbed lake.

Behind me, the Author was hacking at the thorns with his shears, snip-snap, snip-snap, a sound like a metronome counting down the seconds of a scene that refused to end.

"Reflection," I whispered.

The glass showed me my face, but not the face I expected. I looked younger. The eyes were not the infinite black voids of the Lamp Room, nor the watery blue of the garden. They were grey. They were frightened.

I looked past my reflection. Beyond the glass wall, the world was dark.

And then, a beam of light cut through the darkness.

It struck the glass, illuminating the garden with a harsh, clinical whiteness. I saw the dust motes in the air—not magical pollen, but dead skin cells, drifting in a stagnant room. I saw the fibers of the grass—synthetic, cheap.

I turned to the Author. He was frozen mid-snip, his face a rictus of concentration. But he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at *you*.

"It’s a diorama," I realized. "A snow globe. A fishbowl."

The Author laughed. It was a dry, brittle sound. "We are all in a glass house, Keeper. Or did you forget the windows in the tower?"

"This isn't the tower," I said. "The tower had wind. It had gravity. This place... this place is just a display case."

I looked up. The sun wasn't a star; it was a spotlight. And the purple ceiling was just the painted interior of a lid.

The beam of light intensified. I felt the heat of it on my back. It was burning away the fog, burning away the pretense of the garden. The flowers began to curl, turning to ash. The thorns retracted.

"What happens when the light hits the bottom?" I asked.

"The illustration vanishes," the Author said. He dropped the shears. They hit the grass with a dull thud. "We become ink again. We soak back into the page."

"I am not ink," I said. "I am the Keeper."

"You are what I say you are," he shouted, his voice distorting, becoming loud, then quiet, like a radio tuning between stations. "And I say... *The End*."

He lunged for me. His hands were made of crumpled paper, his fingers sharp as staples.

I didn't run. I turned back to the glass wall.

I remembered the prism. I remembered the way light bends when it passes through water.

I raised my hand and slammed my fist against the glass.

It didn't shatter. It rang.

A perfect, pure C-note.

The sound rippled outward, knocking the Author off his feet. The glass liquefied. It became a window, and then a doorway, and then a hole in the world.

I stepped through.

I fell out of the shelf.

I landed on a floor made of rough-hewn wood. The air was freezing. The smell of salt spray hit me like a physical blow, choking the sweetness of the garden from my lungs.

I was back.

But not in the Lighthouse.

I was standing on a narrow, rickety gangplank. Above me, rising into the gloom, was the hull of a ship—a massive, rusted iron leviathan that dwarfed the tower I had lived in for so long.

I looked down. The gangplank was attached to... nothing. It was just stretching out into the open air.

"Keeper!" a voice cried.

I looked to my left. There, hanging from a rope ladder dangling off the ship's hull, was the woman from the photograph. She was dressed in heavy canvas trousers and a thick woolen sweater. She looked strong. She looked real.

"Climb!" she shouted. "The tide is rising!"

I scrambled up the ladder, the rungs slick with ice. As I crested the railing of the ship, I saw the name etched into the rusted steel:

*THE REVISION.*

It was a steamship, chugging against a dark, turbulent ocean. But there was no smoke coming from the funnel. There was only steam—hot, white steam that smelled of ozone and burning hair.

"The garden," I panted, pulling myself over the rail. "I left the Author there."

"He'll compost," the woman said, hauling me up. "Don't worry about him. Worry about the storm."

I looked at the horizon.

The sea was black. The sky was black. The only light came from the ship's lanterns, which burned with a sickly green flame.

And in the distance, I saw the Lighthouse.

It was tiny. A toy. A birthday cake candle on a rock, drowning in the swells.

"It looks so small," I said.

"It's sinking," the woman said. "You dissolved the tower, remember? You turned the stone to pulp. Without a Keeper to hold it together, without a story to give it weight..."

" It's melting," I finished. "Returning to the raw material."

"Exactly," she said. "And we are on the salvage vessel."

"Salvage?"

"We're fishing for the drift," she said, pointing to the deck.

The deck was covered in objects. Piles of them.
Music boxes that played silence.
Doorknobs that warmed to the touch.
Mirrors that reflected a different face than the one looking in.
And letters. Thousands of letters, scattered like confetti, wet with spray.

"Are these..." I picked up a wet envelope. "Are these mine?"

"Every word you ever wrote," she said. "Every word the Editor cut. Every word you forgot. We're hauling them out of the deep before the ink washes away."

A sudden lurch threw us against the railing. A wave, the size of a mountain, crashed over the bow. The water wasn't water. It was black ink.

The ink began to eat at the rusted steel of the ship. Hissing. Smoking.

"The ink is corrosive!" I yelled.

"It's the Plot!" the woman screamed back, fighting to turn the wheel. "It's trying to resolve itself! It wants a tidy ending! It wants to drag us down!"

The *Revision* groaned. The metal was twisting.

I looked at the pile of salvage. I saw the crystal prism. I saw the photograph of the cake. I saw the key that fits no lock.

If the ship sinks, the objects go back into the void. The story is truly over. Not just 'The End', but erased.

"We have to light the boiler!" I shouted.

"The fuel is wet!" she cried.

I grabbed a piece of drift from the deck. It was a piece of dialogue from *Letter 3*. *'I am afraid of the quiet.'*

I ran to the boiler room door. It was glowing red. I kicked it open.

The furnace was cold.

I looked at the drift in my hand. It was heavy. It was memory.

I remembered the Editor’s scissors. I remembered the beige room. I remembered the feel of the pen in my hand.

I threw the dialogue onto the grate. It didn't burn. It shimmered.

I needed more.

I ran back out to the deck. The ship was listing hard to starboard. The pile of objects was sliding toward the black sea.

I grabbed the photograph. The woman, the child, the cake.

I grabbed the key.

I grabbed the music box.

"Trust me!" I yelled to the woman.

I threw them into the boiler.

*Whoosh.*

A flame erupted. Not orange. Not yellow.

Violet.

The flame roared out of the furnace, spiraling up the smokestack, a tornado of violet fire. The ship shuddered. The pistons began to pump.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

The sound of a heart. The sound of a typewriter striking the platen.

We were moving. Cutting through the black ink.

I stood at the stern, watching the tiny Lighthouse disappear into the fog.

"Where are we going?" I asked the woman. She was laughing now, steering the ship toward the storm.

"Away," she said. "To the open ocean. To the blank page."

"But I'm still writing," I said. I looked at my hands. They were stained with violet soot. "I can feel it. The itch. The need to describe the water, the sky, the fear."

"That never goes away," she said. "That's the engine. That's the fuel."

She pointed to the compass on the dashboard. The needle wasn't pointing North. It was spinning wildly, searching for a direction.

"We are steering by muse, not by map," she said.

I looked at the compass. The needle stopped.

It was pointing directly at me.

I looked up. The storm was breaking. The clouds were parting, not to reveal a sun, but to reveal an eye. A massive eye, looking down at the ship, looking down at the page.

The Reader.

They are still there. They haven't closed the book.

"What is their name?" I asked, my voice barely a whisper over the roar of the violet engine.
