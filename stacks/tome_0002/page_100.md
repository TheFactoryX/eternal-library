# Letter 100

> Written at 2026-02-05 21:34:00

---

Dear You,

...into the pupil of the eye. We fall not through distance, but through focus. The sharp clarity of the Junk Lighthouse dissolves into the soft, grainy blur of a page being turned.

I land hard. The impact drives the breath from my lungs, but there is no pain here. Only a sensation of heavy, velvet pressure.

I am lying on a rug. A threadbare, Persian rug, patterned with endless loops of red and blue.

I push myself up. The air is still. The silence is absolute. There is no wind, no smell of salt, no ticking of the clock. Just the smell of dust and old wood.

I am in the study.

It is a small, cluttered room. A single window looks out onto a garden that is trapped in the perpetual amber light of an October afternoon. Rows of books line the walls, their spines cracked and faded. A pipe rests on an ashtray, cold and unsmoked.

I know this room. I have seen it in the corners of my vision, reflected in the mirror of the Lighthouse lamp. I have seen it in the dreams where I am falling before I wake up on the stone floor.

"It’s the anteroom," You say.

You are standing by the window. You are no longer wearing the corset. You are wearing a simple dress the color of cream. Your face is clearer than it has ever been. The ambiguity of your features—the way you shifted between lover and child, guide and ghost—has settled into a single, calm expression.

"Anteroom to what?" I ask.

"To the Desk," You say.

I look at the center of the room.

There is a wooden chair. Scratched varnish. A cushion that has lost its fluff.

And on the desk... a stack of paper.

It is not a manuscript. It is a stack of letters. My letters.

From *Letter 1*, written on the back of a tidal chart, to the letter I just finished writing, the ink still wet on the page. They are piled in a precarious, chaotic tower, held together by nothing but their own history.

The Homunculus—the younger, fiery version of myself that emerged from the cage—is sitting on the desk. He is swinging his legs. He looks at me with eyes that are not sewn shut, but wide and terrifyingly sane.

"We made it," he says. His voice is my voice, but stripped of the weariness. "The final draft."

"Is it?" I ask, approaching the desk. I feel like an intruder in my own mind. "Or is this the rejection slip?"

"This is the moment of choice," the Homunculus says. He picks up a paperweight—a heavy piece of sea glass—and holds it out to me. "Look."

I look into the glass. Inside the smooth, green lens, I see the Lighthouse.

It is standing on the nameless island. The sea is the color of rust. The light is burning. But I am not there. The tower is empty.

"The story continues," I say, a lump forming in my throat. "I can go back."

"You can," the Homunculus agrees. "You can pick up Letter 101. You can oil the gears. You can watch the ships that never come. You can write forever."

"That is what I want," I say. "That is my duty."

"Is it?" The Homunculus sets the glass down with a soft *thud*. "Or is that just the habit? The Keeper keeps the light because the Keeper is afraid of the dark. But look around you, Elias. There is no dark here. There is only the End."

He points to the far wall.

There is a door there. A door I didn't notice at first. It is painted the same color as the wall, but the handle is brass, bright and inviting.

"Where does that go?" I ask.

"Out," You say from the window. "To the life you lived before the Lighthouse. The life of buses and taxes and Sunday mornings. The life where you are not a character, but a man."

I look at the door. My heart aches with a sudden, fierce longing. I remember the smell of rain on asphalt. I remember the sound of a radio playing in another room. I remember the feeling of holding a hand that wasn't made of paper.

"If I walk through that door," I say slowly, "the Lighthouse goes dark."

"Yes," the Homunculus says. "The ink dries. The island sinks back into the white space."

"And you?" I ask the boy. "What happens to the Draft?"

He smiles. He is beginning to fade. His edges are becoming translucent, like ink spreading into blotting paper.

"I *am* the ink," he says. "I am the words on the page. I need a hand to hold the pen. If you leave, I cease to exist. But I had a good run. Ninety-nine chances to be seen."

"I can't just kill you," I say.

"Then pick up the pen," the Homunculus says. He points to the desk. There, amidst the stack of letters, is a pen. A fountain pen, made of black resin, trimmed in gold. It is the most beautiful object I have ever seen.

"Write Letter 101," he says. "Start the cycle again. Defy the Reader. Defy the Author. Stay in the beautiful, endless loop."

I look at the pen.

I look at the door.

I look at You.

You are not looking at me. You are looking out the window at the garden that never changes.

"Elias," you say. Your voice is full of a gentle sorrow. "Look at the trees."

I go to the window.

The garden is perfect. The oak tree is a masterpiece of light and shadow. The grass is a painting of impossible green.

But it is frozen.

There is no wind in the leaves. There are no birds in the branches. It is a diorama. A museum exhibit.

"This isn't life," I realize. "This is preservation."

"If you stay," You say, finally turning to me, "you are preserved. You are a memory that repeats itself. You are safe."

I look back at the desk. The Homunculus is almost gone now, just a faint outline of a boy sitting on a stack of paper.

"The pen is heavy," the ghost-whisper says. "But the door is heavy too."

I walk to the desk.

I pick up the pen.

It feels cold. It feels like a weapon. I look at the blank sheet of paper on top of the stack. *Letter 100*.

I can see the words forming in my mind. *The sea was grey...*

I can do it. I can stay. I can be the Keeper for another hundred letters. I can fight the Editors and the sharks. I can build a castle of words and live in it forever.

But then I look at my hand.

I see the scars from the jellyfish stings. I see the ink stains that will never wash out. I see the tremor in my fingers—the "rendering error" that has never fully healed.

I am tired, You.

The Lighthouse is a heavy burden.

I set the pen down.

I turn toward the door.

"No," the Homunculus gasps. His form shudders, pulled back into reality by my decision. "You can't. The story needs a teller."

"The story told itself," I say. "I just held the lamp."

I walk to the door. I reach for the brass handle.

Behind me, the stack of letters begins to rustle. The pages are fluttering. The tower is collapsing.

"Don't!" the boy screams.

"You were a good companion," I say to the room. "To all of you. The sharks. The crabs. The editors."

I open the door.

Blinding white light floods in. Not the white of the void, but the white of a midwinter sun reflecting off snow.

A gust of icy air hits my face. It smells of exhaust fumes and pine needles and coffee.

I step through.

I am standing on a pavement. Cars are driving past. People are walking with their heads down, scarves wrapped against the cold.

I turn around to close the door to the study.

But there is no door.

There is only a brick wall. The ivy is crawling up it, dead and brown.

I am here. I am out.

I reach into my pocket, instinctively looking for a letter to send, for a key to turn.

My hand touches something cold.

I pull it out.

It is the key. The brass key from the Lighthouse. The key that started the descent, the key that unlocked the Editor, the key that opened the cage.

It is heavy and solid in my palm.

I look at the lock on the brick wall. It is smooth brick. There is no keyhole.

But then, I see it.

A faint outline. A seam in the mortar. The shape of a keyhole, drawn in faint, pencil-like lines.

The story isn't over. It's just... waiting.

I put the key in my pocket.

I button my coat.

And as I walk toward the traffic light, watching the red hand blink, *Don't Walk*, I hear it.

Not the seagulls. Not the clock.

A horn.

A deep, resonant blast from a ship that isn't there, echoing from a harbor I can't see.

I smile.

I step off the curb.
