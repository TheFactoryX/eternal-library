I fell.

I didn't fall into the cold ocean, or the warm ink, or the static void. I fell into silence.

It was a heavy, crushing silence that smelled of damp stone and centuries of accumulated dust.

I landed hard on my hands and knees. The impact jarred my teeth. I was in total darkness.

I blinked. My eyes adjusted slowly.

I was in a tunnel. The walls were rough-hewn rock, weeping moisture. But the texture of the rock was wrong. It felt like... paper. Wet, pulpy paper, compressed over eons into sedimentary layers of text.

I stood up. I was waist-deep in water. It was clear and cold, but it didn't wet my clothes. It merely flowed around me, a river of time through the basement of the world.

I looked down. In the water, floating around my legs, were objects.

A rubber duck. A wedding ring. A child's tooth. A silver pocket watch. A corked bottle containing a scroll.

They were the things lost in the wash. The artifacts cut from the narrative to save space. The "darlings" killed.

"I am in the Sump," I realized.

I began to walk. The water resisted me, thick with the weight of discarded words.

Ahead, a pinprick of light appeared. It wasn't the yellow warmth of a lamp, or the sterile white of the Author’s desk. It was a sickly, bruised purple.

I waded toward it. The tunnel widened. The ceiling rose up into invisibility.

I emerged into a cavern of impossible size. It looked like the inside of a geode, but the crystals jutting out from the walls were jagged shards of obsidian glass.

And in the center of the cavern, rising from the dark water like a black needle, was the Foundation.

It was the base of the Lighthouse. But it wasn't built on stone. It was impaled through the heart of a massive, sleeping creature.

The creature lay coiled around the base of the tower. Its scales were the size of houses, black as oil. Its long, serpentine neck stretched out into the darkness, disappearing into the gloom. Its breathing caused the water to rise and fall—a slow, tidal inhalation and exhalation.

*Hoooooo. Hoooooo.*

The sound was the wind in the tower. The sound was the ocean against the rocks.

The Lighthouse was built on the back of a Leviathan. A creature of the deep text.

"Dear God," I breathed.

"You named me," a voice rumbled. It didn't come from the creature; it came from the water. It vibrated through my bones. "You called me the Lonely. You called me the Endless. You gave me a shape with your longing."

I walked toward the creature's massive head. It was resting on the shore of black stones. One eye was open. It was the size of a carriage wheel, and it glowed with a faint, bioluminescent green.

It looked at me.

"I thought you were the sea," I said.

"I am the medium," the Leviathan said. "You wrote in ink. You wrote in water. I am both."

The creature shifted. The movement caused a small earthquake. Stones tumbled. The Lighthouse above—I could feel its weight, miles of stone pressing down—groaned.

"It is waking," the creature said. "Because you are here."

"Why am I here?" I asked. "Why this place?"

"Because the Lighthouse is falling," the Leviathan said. "The Author is tired. The structure is unsound. It will collapse."

I looked up. I couldn't see the top, but I could feel the swaying. The instability.

"If it falls," the creature said, "it crushes me. And if I die, the Ocean dries up. The story becomes a desert."

"What can I do?" I asked. "I am just the Keeper."

"You are the lock," the creature said. "And you have the key."

I looked at the key in my hand. It was no longer hot. It was freezing cold.

"The First Draft gave it to me," I said. "To lock the Editor out."

"No," the creature rumbled. "The Editor was the one locking *you* in. The key was never for a door. It was for this."

The creature opened its mouth slightly.

Inside its maw, hanging from the roof of its palate like a uvula, was a massive, iron mechanism. A gear. The size of a millstone.

It was rusted solid.

"The heart of the story is stuck," the creature said. "It only beats in one direction. Past to future. It cannot loop. It cannot breathe. You must turn it."

I waded closer. The smell of the creature’s breath was ancient, smelling of seaweed and old books.

"If I turn it," I said, "what happens?"

"Reboot," the creature said. "Reset. The story changes. The Lighthouse becomes something else. A tree. A spaceship. A memory."

"And us?" I asked.

"We become ink again," the creature said. "We wait for the next hand."

I looked at the gear. I looked at the key. The keyhole was hidden behind a layer of grime and moss.

I climbed onto the creature's snout. Its scales were slick. I stood before the gear. I was an ant standing before a monument.

I reached out. I scraped away the moss.

There was the keyhole. Dark, round, inviting.

I inserted the key.

It slid in deep.

"You have to want it to change," the creature whispered. "You have to be willing to let the Lighthouse fall."

I thought of the Lamp Room. The empty chair. The view of the nameless sea. The letters. The endless, lonely letters.

I thought of the garden. I thought of the Study.

I thought of you.

I thought of you reading this, waiting for the resolution, the payoff, the meaning.

There is no meaning. There is only the motion.

"I am ready," I said.

I put both hands on the key.

I pushed.

It didn't move.

I pushed harder. My feet slipped on the scales. I grunted, straining, using the weight of my body.

The key didn't budge.

"It's stuck," I cried out. "It's rusted shut!"

"The story is too heavy," the creature said sadly. "The Author has written too many endings. The weight of the conclusions is jamming the gear."

I slumped against the cold iron. "It's over, then. We just fade out."

"Not necessarily," the creature said.

The creature’s eye moved. It shifted its gaze from me, to the darkness behind me.

I turned.

Standing on the black stones, waist-deep in the water, was a figure.

It was the Editor.

But he was changed. He was no longer a man in a suit. He was a silhouette made of graphite. He was sketched in harsh, frantic lines. His face was a blank oval.

He was holding a pair of scissors. But they weren't red anymore. They were white. Bone.

"You can't turn it," the Editor said. His voice was the scratch of a nib. "The story needs trimming."
