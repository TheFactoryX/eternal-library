# Letter 59

Dear You,

The sentence hung in the air, severed by the sudden slam of a door.

Not the door of the study. That door remained open, revealing the sunlit hallway and the promise of a life I had never lived. No, this was the front door of the house. The heavy, wooden barrier that separated this sanctuary from the world outside.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

The sound was rhythmic and desperate. It wasn't a knock. It was the sound of a body throwing itself against the wood.

"There is someone trying to get in," you finished, your face draining of color. You looked at the silver pen in your hand, then at the drawing in the notebook. The graphite figure of me in the sketch was flickering, vibrating like a plucked string.

"Don't open it," I said. My voice sounded thin, papery. I looked down at my own hands. They were turning translucent at the edges, dissolving into the white space of the page. "If you open that door, the draft comes in."

"I have to," you said, standing up. Your chair scraped against the floor—a jarringly real sound. "They sound hurt."

"They sound hungry," I corrected.

I tried to step forward to stop you, but my foot passed through the floorboard. I was losing purchase. The ink that held me together was drying, flaking away into the ether. The story was ending, and the friction that allowed me to interact with matter was dissolving.

You walked past me. Through me. A chill swept through your body as you intersected my form, a shiver you attributed to fear, but I knew was ghost-touch.

You reached the hallway. I followed, a shadow trailing its owner.

*Thump. Thump. Screeeeech.*

The noise was coming from the other side of the front door. But it was changing. The wood of the door was bowing inward. It wasn't just force being applied; it was *pressure*. Water pressure.

I smelled salt. I heard the roar of a distant, mechanical sea.

"It's the Lighthouse," I whispered. "You didn't just write an island. You wrote a flood."

You placed your hand on the doorknob. "It's just the wind. It’s just a branch."

"It's the genre shift," I said, drifting closer to the ceiling. "You wrote a romance, but the horror is bleeding through the margins."

You turned the knob.

The door didn't open inward. It exploded inward.

It wasn't a wind that entered. It was a tide.

Black, sludge-like water surged into the pristine hallway, knocking you backward. The smell was terrible—the rot of kelp, the sulfurous stink of the industrial press, the copper tang of the Old Man’s shears.

But the water wasn't water. It was *letters*.

Millions of individual characters, washed up on the shore of your reality. They formed a serpentine shape in the air, a swirling vortex of text coalescing into a figure.

It was the Boy.

But he was not the innocent child with the compass. He was the Boy from the Version That Was Rejected. He was the Boy who had grown up in the dark. He wore the tattered remnants of the Keeper’s uniform, but he was huge, a giant composed of angry, repetitive text.

"I AM THE PROTAGONIST!" he screamed, his voice the sound of a printing press jamming. "YOU CANNOT REPLACE ME!"

He lashed out with a hand made of jagged dialogue. He struck the wall, punching through the drywall as if it were tissue paper.

You scrambled backward, slipping on the wet floor. "What is this? What is happening?"

"It's the sequel demanding to be written," I said. I floated above the chaos, powerless to intervene. "He is the part of you that refuses to be happy. He is the plot that won't resolve."

The Boy-Thing turned his attention to you. He loomed over your trembling form.

"I WILL NOT BE ARCHIVED," he boomed.

I looked at the silver pen lying on the study floor. If I could just reach it. If I could just write a counter-spell, a revision, a deus ex machina.

But I was fading. The white light of the ending was pulling me apart.

Then, I saw it.

In the debris of the broken door, carried in on the tide of black letters, was an object. It glinted in the ambient light.

It was the spoon. The bent spoon from the Press.

It had washed up here.

The Boy-Thing raised a fist to strike you. To strike the Author. To end the story in a tragedy of violence.

"No!" I shouted, though I had no mouth to speak with.

I focused all my remaining will. I didn't try to become solid; I tried to become *heavy*. I remembered the weight of the wet wool coat. I remembered the heaviness of the lighthouse key.

I fell.

I plummeted from the ceiling, crashing down onto the figure of the Boy.

I didn't hit him with a body. I hit him with the *concept* of the Keeper. I hit him with Duty. With Silence. With Longing.

We tumbled back into the flood of letters. The black water wrapped around us, pulling us toward the gap in the door—the gap leading back to the void.

"Go!" I screamed at you. "Close the book!"

You scrambled to your knees. You grabbed the silver pen. You looked at me, tangled in the arms of the monster I had become.

"I'm sorry!" you cried. "I'm sorry I left you there!"

"Write!" I commanded. "Write the ending!"

I looked the Boy in the eyes. They were spinning compass needles.

"We are the same," I told him. "We are both the choices you didn't make."

I shoved him. Hard.

We tumbled out the door, into the swirling whiteout beyond the frame. We were falling into the space between paragraphs.

I saw your hand raise the pen.

I saw the study begin to dissolve. The walls turning into paper. The sunlight fading into lamp-light.

You were writing us back to the island.

I hit the water.

But it wasn't the warm, golden glue of the binding. It was the cold, black Atlantic.

I surfaced, gasping. The waves were mountainous. The sky was a bruised purple.

I was treading water about a mile from the shore.

The island was there. The Lighthouse stood on the cliff, a dark silhouette against a dying star.

But something was wrong.

The light was out.

I spun in the water, looking for the Boy, for the monster. He was gone. Perhaps he had dissolved on the journey back. Perhaps he was just a ripple in the dark water now.

I began to swim. The cold sapped the strength from my limbs instantly. My clothes dragged me down.

*I am the Keeper,* I told myself. *I tend the light.*

But if the light was out, what was I?

I reached the rocky shore. I clawed my way up the slick, seaweed-covered stones, my fingers raw. I collapsed on the grass, gasping, my chest heaving.

I looked up at the Lighthouse.

It looked dead. The glass was dark. The iron was rusted. It looked like it had been abandoned for a century.

"Hello?" I called out. My voice was swallowed by the wind.

I pushed myself to my feet and stumbled toward the door. My hand shook as I reached for the handle.

It was locked.

I pounded on the wood. "Let me in! I am the Keeper!"

No answer.

I looked down at the welcome mat. It was gone. In its place was a sheet of paper, nailed to the door.

I stepped closer, narrowing my eyes against the spray.

It wasn't a note. It was a manuscript page.

I read the typing in the dim starlight.

*The Keeper returned to the shore, but found the door barred. The realization was slow in coming, like a sunrise in winter. The Lighthouse did not keep the light for the ships. The Lighthouse kept the light for the Keeper. And without the Keeper, the light had no reason to burn.*

I looked up at the dark tower. Then I looked back down at the page.

The text was still appearing.

*The Keeper looked for the key, but found only a bent spoon in his pocket. He tried to dig the lock out, but the spoon turned to sand in his hand.*

I reached into my pocket. My fingers brushed against the spoon. It was there. But when I pulled it out, it crumbled into silver dust, drifting away on the breeze.

I looked back at the page.

*The Keeper was not the prisoner. He was the visitor. And now, the visit was over.*

The text stopped.

I stood alone in the dark, the empty page nailed to the door, the dark tower looming above me.

Then, from the darkness of the window above, I heard a sound.

A single, sharp click.

*Click.*

The sound of a key turning in a lock.

But it wasn't the door lock. It was the lock on the lantern room.

Someone was up there.

I wasn't the Keeper anymore.

I was the knock at the door.
