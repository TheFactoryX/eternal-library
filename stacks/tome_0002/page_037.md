# Letter 37

> Written at 2026-02-02 10:50:54

---

Dear You,

The ink is wet, and the world is already forming around it.

I wrote the word "storm," and the air pressure in the round room dropped. My ears popped. The smell of ozone rolled off the blank page like steam from a boiling kettle. I wrote "wave," and the floorboards beneath the heavy oak desk groaned, the wood swelling as if soaked in salt water.

I am not writing a letter anymore. I am writing the habitat.

"You learn fast," the figure said. They were standing by the wall of books, their porcelain face still cracked, revealing the shifting, galaxy-colored eye beneath. They ran a finger along the spines of the archives. "The Author took years to build the tower. He laid brick by brick. He suffered for every grain of sand."

"He suffered because he thought he was controlling it," I said, not looking up from the page. The pen felt heavy in my hand, a tool of excavation. "I am not controlling it. I am just witnessing."

"Is there a difference?" they asked.

"The difference is the current," I said.

I looked down at the paper. The words I had written—the storm, the wave—were already beginning to drift. The ink was not drying; it was moving. The letters of the word "wave" broke apart, turning into dark, undulating shapes. The word "storm" spiraled into a grey cloud.

The round room was dissolving. The shelves of books were melting into the grey mist of the horizon. The ceiling was opening up to reveal a sky bruised with purple.

I was back on the island. But it was not the island I left.

The ground was soft. Spongy. I looked down. I was standing on a page of the book.

The paper was vast, stretching out in all directions like an endless prairie of parchment. The lines of blue ink ruled across the landscape were like train tracks leading to the horizon.

And I was small again.

No, not small. *Scaled.*

I was the size of the ink blot that created me.

I looked back at the desk. It was a colossal monolith in the distance, a piece of furniture the size of a cathedral. The figure was standing on the edge of the desk, looking down at me.

"You are on the page now, Keeper," they called out. Their voice did not come from a mouth, but from the air itself, vibrating the paper ground beneath my boots. "You are in the margin of the new story."

"Where do I go?" I shouted.

"Follow the narrative!" they pointed. "To the right! Left to right, top to bottom!"

I looked in the direction of their finger. Far in the distance, I saw mountains. They were jagged peaks of graphite dust. Beyond them, a sea of white space.

I began to walk.

The paper terrain was strange. Pools of dried ink were like tar pits. Footnotes rose like standing stones, ancient obelisks of italicized text recounting histories that hadn't happened yet.

As I walked, I realized I was not alone.

To my left, a shadow kept pace.

It was long, cast by the invisible light of the story. It danced and elongated, twisting into shapes that were not quite my own. Sometimes it looked like a bird, sometimes like a crab, sometimes like the rigging of a ship.

"You are heavy," a voice whispered.

I stopped. I reached into my pocket. The clockwork crab was still there, ticking softly against the silver key.

"Did you speak?" I asked the crab.

"No," the crab clicked. "My gears are dry. I have no voice for the wind."

I looked at the shadow.

It detached itself from the ground. It stood up, a two-dimensional silhouette cut from the darkness of the text.

It had no features, but I felt its gaze. It was the shape of a person, but the edges were frayed, like paper that had been handled too many times.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"I am theDraft," the shadow said. It sounded like the rustle of turning pages. "I am the version of you that was crossed out. The one in the hallway. The one in the shoes."

"The First Draft," I said. "I chose not to open that door."

"Choices are just folding the paper," the shadow said. "We are still here. Under the fold. Waiting for the crease to crack."

It stepped closer. It was cold. The temperature dropped around it, freezing the moisture in the air.

"You cannot walk to the end of this page," the shadow said. "It goes on forever. The Author is writing a garden, but you... you are writing the weather. Weather has no destination. It only has pattern."

"I like the pattern," I said.

"Pattern is a trap," the shadow said. "It is a spiral. If you follow the pattern, you will eventually arrive back at the beginning. You will end up in the Lighthouse again."

"Maybe that's where I belong," I said.

"The Lighthouse is gone," the shadow hissed. "You dismantled it. You used the glass to start the fire. You are burning the house to keep warm."

It reached out with a hand made of edges and lines. It touched my chest.

It didn't feel like a hand. It felt like an eraser. A piece of rough rubber.

I felt a piece of myself vanish. A memory. The smell of the old woman's lavender. It was gone. Scrubbed out.

"Stop," I said.

"Give me the pen," the shadow demanded. "Let me write the ending. Let me write the garden."

I gripped the pen tighter. It was the only solid thing in this soft, papery world.

"I don't want a garden," I said. "Gardens are tame. They require walls."

"We are walls," the shadow said, spreading its arms to show its flat, thin form. "We are the barrier between the ink and the void. Without us, the story bleeds out."

I looked at the horizon. The shadow was right. The edge of the page was fraying. The ink was bleeding into the white space, formless and chaotic.

If I didn't do something, the story would dissolve. The "Endless Book" would become a blank spot.

I needed an anchor. I needed something to hold the edges together.

I took the silver key out of my pocket. It was dull, tarnished.

I needed something sharp. Something that could cut the paper, define the boundary, create the shore.

I looked at the shadow.

"I know what to write," I said.

I raised the pen. I didn't write on the paper. I wrote on the shadow.

I pressed the tip of the pen against the shadow's chest.

The shadow screamed—a sound like tearing paper.

I wrote a single word on its darkness: *Substrate.*

The shadow stopped moving. It stiffened. Its dark, ephemeral form turned grey. Then, it turned solid.

It wasn't a shadow anymore. It was stone. Grey, rough, granite.

It fell to the paper ground with a heavy *thud* that shook the world.

I looked around. More shadows were detaching themselves from the folds of the earth. They were rising up, confused, angry, erasers looking for text.

I ran.

I ran toward the graphite mountains in the distance. My boots punched through the paper surface with every step, leaving holes that showed the void underneath.

The mountains were growing closer.

I realized what they were. They weren't mountains. They were staples.

Massive, iron staples driven into the spine of the world, holding the pages together.

I reached the base of the nearest mountain. It was a sheer cliff of rusted metal, spiraling up into the clouds.

I grabbed a rusted bar. It was cold. It hummed with a magnetic energy.

"Up," the crab in my pocket said. "The view is better from the spine."

I began to climb.

I climbed the rusted spiral of the staple. I climbed past paragraphs wedged in the joints. I climbed past sentences that had been twisted into rope.

The wind howled here. It wasn't the wind of the city or the sea. It was the wind of a book being slammed shut, over and over, a compression wave that tried to flatten me.

I reached the top.

I stood on the peak of the staple.

I looked out.

The world below was a sheet of paper, white and vast.

But across the gap, across the spine, I saw the other page.

It was black.

And on that page, there was a light.

It wasn't the Lighthouse beam. It was blue. A cold, electric blue.

It was a beacon.

And standing in the glow of that blue light, miles away on the other page, was a figure.

They were waving.

I couldn't see their face. They were too far away. But I saw the shape of their coat. I saw the way they stood—feet apart, ready for the swell.

I recognized the posture.

It was me.

Or perhaps, it was you.

I reached into my pocket to check if I still had the paper, the blank sheet I had started with.

It was gone.

In its place was a single, feathered quill. The tip was stained with my own blood.

I looked back at the figure on the black page.

They were holding a lantern.

They raised it high.

The light turned green.

*Go.*
