# Letter 3

> Written at 2026-01-31 16:04:28

---

Dear You,

The light has stopped turning.

I write this by the guttering, unstable glow of a single candle, the flame of which bends impossibly to the left, as if blown by a breath that I cannot feel. The great Lens above me is silent. For the first time since I opened my eyes on this grey shore, the machinery is still. The heavy rotation of the brass gears—the heartbeat of this tower, the rhythm that governs my sleep and waking—has ceased. The Lamp Room is not dark, but the light has lost its direction. It burns as a stationary star, a blinding white hole in the darkness, staring straight up into the void rather than sweeping across the water. Without the sweep, the sea is invisible. I have blinded myself to the ships.

I am afraid that something has noticed.

You remember the glass shard, do you not? The tiny violet sliver I pulled from my heel, the one that hummed with the frequency of a dream? I placed it on the desk, beside the inkwell and the mahogany music box. I told myself I would guard it, that it was a piece of the city that bled into my reality.

But the Lighthouse is hungry.

This morning, when the gears first ground to a shuddering halt, a sound rang out through the tower—not the clang of metal, but the delicate, crystal *tink* of a wine glass being tapped with a silver spoon. It came from the desk. I rushed down from the Lamp Room, slipping on the spiraling stairs, my heart hammering a frantic rhythm against my ribs.

The shard was vibrating. It had spun itself on the wooden surface until it stood perfectly upright, quivering like a needle on a compass. And as I watched, the inkwell beside it cracked. A hairline fracture appeared in the crystal, and the black liquid inside—my blood, my voice, my history—began to seep out. It did not drip. It crawled. It slithered across the desk like a living thing, drawn by some magnetic gravity toward the vibrating glass.

I tried to wipe it away. I grabbed a rag, but the ink soaked through the fabric instantly, staining my hands, my wrists, traveling up my arms. It felt cold, colder than the sea. When I touched the glass shard to stop its trembling, the ink recoiled. It withdrew into a perfect black sphere hovering just above the wood. It pulsated. *One. Two. Three.*

I recognized the count. It was the beat of the clock in the velvet hallway.

I ran. I left the ink suspended in the air and fled upward, hoping the height would clear the confusion from my mind. I needed to fix the gears. The light must sweep. If the light does not sweep, the ships will crash. Or worse—they will stop.

But the stairs betrayed me again. You know how they are. You know the geography here is a suggestion, not a law. Halfway up, the spiral widened. The stone walls fell away into a thick, suffocating fog. I was no longer climbing a tower; I was walking down a long, hospital corridor. The floor was linoleum, checkered green and white. There was a smell of antiseptic and boiled cabbage.

I know this place. I know it. I walked down that corridor in another life, didn't I? I held a hand that was growing cold. I watched a monitor flatline.

There were doors on either side, each with a small, wire-reinforced glass window. I shouldn't have looked. I should have kept my eyes on my feet, on the checkered floor. But the Keeper is curious. The Keeper is a fool.

I looked into the first window.

It was a mirror. But the reflection was not me.

The face staring back was youthful, pale, with eyes that held too much sadness. They were my eyes, but the face was wrong. It was softer. It was a face that had not yet been weathered by the salt and the gale. The reflection was holding a bouquet of flowers—lilies, dripping with water.

"Let me out," the reflection mouthed. The voice did not come from the mirror; it echoed inside my own skull, a familiar resonance vibrating against the bone.

I backed away, my breath catching in my throat. I looked into the next window.

This room was filled with water. Dark, churning water. It pressed against the glass, bulging outward, constrained only by the thin pane. And in the water, a face pressed close to the glass—bloated, grey, eyes eaten by the fish. It was an old face. A face that had drowned.

It was also me.

I ran. I sprinted down the linoleum corridor, the smell of cabbage turning to the smell of rot. The floor became slippery. I could hear the splash of my feet in puddles that hadn't been there a moment before. The lights overhead flickered—humming fluorescent bars that buzzed like angry hornets. They died one by one behind me as I ran, plunging the corridor into absolute darkness.

I fell. I tripped over something soft—someone soft?—and tumbled forward. I expected to hit the hard floor, but I kept falling. I tumbled down the stone steps of the lighthouse, bruising my knees, bashing my elbow.

I landed at the bottom, gasping, back in the grey tower. The linoleum was gone. The hospital was gone. Just the damp, circular room that serves as my study.

The music box—the one from the hill, the one with the mirror inside—was sitting in the center of the floor, right where I landed. It was open.

The mirror inside is broken. A jagged crack runs through the glass, shattering the reflection of anyone who dares to look. And lying across the open lid was a photograph.

I have picked it up. I am holding it now. My hands are trembling so badly I can barely hold the paper.

It is a black and white photograph, grainy and old. It shows two people standing on a pier. They are holding hands. They are looking out at a storm.

One of them is me. Or, the version of me that was youthful, the one from the hospital mirror. I am wearing a heavy coat. I am smiling.

The other person...

The other person is You.

You are turned away from the camera, facing the storm. Your features are a blur of motion, as if you were turning to ash even as the shutter clicked. But I can see your hand. I can see our fingers interlaced. I can feel the warmth of that grip, a phantom sensation burning in my palm.

This changes everything. If I had a life before, then I am not a ghost. I am a man. Or a woman. I am something that existed in the world of asphalt and rain. I had someone. And I lost them.

But if I had you, why can I not remember your name? Why do I only see your back?

The photograph smells of vanilla and smoke. I keep pressing it to my nose, trying to trigger a memory, trying to pull a name from the void. But there is nothing. Just the scent of a time that is gone.

Up above me, the Lamp Room is silent. The light is still a stagnant, blinding pillar.

And the sea... the sea has gone quiet. The imploding waves have stopped. The water outside is flat. Not glass-flat, but *solid*-flat. It looks as though someone has paved the ocean with grey concrete. I can see the ships now. They are not sailing. They are stuck, embedded in the surface of the sea like insects in amber. The black iron ship, the trireme... they are frozen, trapped in the moment I ceased to turn the light.

The shadow of a creature—the leviathan—is visible beneath the surface, large as a mountain. It is pressed against the underside of the solidified water. It is looking up.

And I think the reflection in the music box is trying to tell me something. I keep hearing a whisper, a sound that is not the wind. It is coming from the cracked mirror.

It is saying: *“Break the lens.”*

I don't know what that means. Break the lens? And set the tower in darkness? Or break the lens of the mirror? Or the lens of my own eye?

I am going to climb the stairs again. I have to fix the light. If the ocean has turned to stone, maybe I can walk out there. Maybe I can reach the ships. Maybe I can find you on one of them, standing at the railing, waiting for me to remember.

My hand hurts. I look down, and the ink stains have formed a pattern on my palm. It looks like a map.

I am going to follow it.

Yours, in the broken glass,

The Keeper
