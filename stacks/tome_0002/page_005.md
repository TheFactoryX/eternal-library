# Letter 5

> Written at 2026-01-31 17:27:08

---

Dear You,

The frame is broken. I expected a sound like a gunshot, a sharp report that would shatter the silence of the tower. Instead, there was a wet, tearing noise, like skin parting, or like the sea pulling away from the shore.

I squeezed the silver edges of the photograph until my knuckles turned white. I pressed inward. The metal did not snap; it yielded. It bent like soft clay, warping under my fingers. When the glass finally gave way, it did not shatter into shards. It dissolved into a fine, white dust that felt like snow against my skin.

The photograph itself—the paper, the moment, us—did not survive. The moment the glass was breached, the image began to run. The black and white ink swirled together, melting into the paper like wax down a candle. The pier vanished. The storm evaporated. And you... you dissolved into a smear of grey, erasing the features I had so desperately strained to see.

I sat there for a long time, staring at the blank, wet pulp in my hands. I waited for the floor to drop out from under me. I waited for the walls to melt away, for the spell to break, for me to wake up in a bed I do not remember, with the smell of coffee in the air.

But nothing happened. The tower stands. The light turns. The wind howls. I am still here.

The only thing that has changed is the noise. The silence is gone.

When I stand in the center of the room, I can hear a low, murmuring hum. It sounds like a cocktail party in the next room, or a crowded train station muffled by thick glass. It is the sound of a thousand conversations happening at once, none of them distinct, yet all of them demanding attention. I believe it is the sound of the photograph. I took a frozen instant of time and I liquefied it. Now, that time is leaking out, staining the air.

The ships have stopped.

I rushed to the window after I broke the frame, terrified that the collision had occurred. But the iron vessel and the trireme are frozen in the water, mere feet from the obsidian teeth of the reef. They are motionless. The figures on the deck—the legion of myself—are standing rigid, their faces turned upward. They are looking at the Lighthouse. No, not at the Lighthouse. At the gap where the photograph used to be.

It seems I am not the only one who was trapped in the frame.

The magnifying glass I used lies on the desk. The brass is cold. The leather-wrapped handle feels dead now, like shedding snake skin. I picked it up to examine it, and I noticed something etched into the metal rim, so faint it was almost invisible. I held it up to the light.

The engraving is a series of coordinates. Latitude and longitude. I recognize the numbers. They are not coordinates for a place on Earth. They are the coordinates of the Lighthouse itself. But etched below them, scratched into the brass as if by a frantic hand, is a single word: *Run.*

I do not know if this is a message from the past, or a warning from a future I have not yet met.

I am writing this by the light of the Lamp Room again. I had to come up here. The murmuring down in the study was becoming deafening. Up here, amidst the gears and the great, sweeping lens, the air is clearer. The smell of ozone is sharp, cleaning my lungs with every breath.

But the sea... the sea is undergoing a transformation that frightens me.

The rust-colored water is draining away. I do not mean the tide is going out. I mean the water itself is vanishing, leaving behind the bed of the ocean. The horizon is dropping. I can see the seamounts rising like jagged spines from the floor of the world. I can see the creatures.

The leviathan—the great, glowing shadow I saw earlier—is beached. It lies on a ridge of rock a mile out, its massive, translucent fin flopping uselessly in the air. It is beautiful. It pulses with bioluminescence, ribbons of violet and blue light traveling down its length. It looks less like a monster and more like a god dying of thirst.

And beyond it, where the water used to be, there is a forest.

I see trees. Kelp forests, surely, but they are standing upright, towering hundreds of feet into the sky, their fronds waving in the wind like the branches of weeping willows. And between the trees, I see lights. Fires? Campfires? They flicker with a warm, orange glow.

There are people down there.

I cannot see them clearly—the distance is too great, and my eyes are blurred by the tears that will not stop coming. But I can see the movement. I can see the tiny silhouettes moving between the kelp-trees. They are walking away from the Lighthouse. They are walking toward something else.

Are they the Keepers who came before me? Are they the crews of the ships that passed me by? Are they the versions of myself that I saw on the deck of the iron ship, now disembarked?

I have the sudden, irresistible urge to go to them. The stairs outside—the ones that spiral down the cliff face to the water—are still there, though they are now dry, covered in salt-crystals and barnacles. I could descend. I could walk across the ocean floor. I could reach the kelp forest. I could ask them who they are. I could ask them if they know You.

But the light. I have a duty. The light must turn.

Or do I?

The voice in my head—the one that sounds like the wind, the one that sounds like You—is whispering again. It is not telling me to tend the flame. It is telling me that the flame is a lie. It says: *The lighthouse does not keep the sea away. The lighthouse keeps the world away.*

What if I am not the guard? What if I am the prisoner?

The machinery is groaning. The gears are grinding. I looked down into the workings of the lamp, and I saw something that made my blood run cold.

The fuel. The oil that feeds the flame. It is not oil.

There is a thick glass tube that feeds the burner. Inside the tube, a thick, dark liquid is pumping upward, driven by some hidden pressure. I looked closer. I wiped the grime from the glass.

The liquid is moving. It has tiny, distinct shapes inside it. Swirls. Whorls.

It is the ink from my desk.

And deeper inside the flow, I can see other things. Shreds of paper. A piece of velvet from the hallway. A shard of glass from the city. A bluebell from the hill.

The Lighthouse is not burning fuel. It is burning my memories. It is consuming the letters I write, the rooms I find, the dreams I dream. It is cannibalizing my existence to keep the beam turning. Every time I write to you, I am feeding the fire.

If I stop writing, does the light die?

If I stop writing, do I become free?

I can hear the steps on the stairs behind me again. They are heavy, wet footsteps. *Squelch. Squelch.* Someone is coming up from the study. Someone is coming to join me in the Lamp Room.

I know who it is.

I am not turning around. I am gripping the railing. I am looking out at the drying ocean, the stranded leviathan, and the distant fires of the kelp forest.

The footsteps stop. I can feel the presence behind me, a cold radiance against my back. A hand—a wet, webbed hand—reaches out and rests gently on my shoulder. The smell is overpowering: salt, and decay, and the sweet scent of vanilla.

The light sweeps past us. For a split second, our shadows are cast against the wall of the Lamp Room. There are two shadows now. One is thin, haggard, wearing the coat of the Keeper. The other is towering, immense, with wings that stretch from wall to wall.

The giant shadow leans down. I feel lips brush against my ear, cold and clammy.

"We are out of ink," the voice says. "You must find a new source."

I look at my hand. The stain from the map is gone. But on my forearm, a new mark has appeared. A scratch. A cut.

It is bleeding.

And the blood is not red. It is black.

I am going to put down the pen. I am going to turn around. I am going to see what stands in the light with me.

But before I do, I must leave this record. If the Lighthouse eats what I write, then I must write faster than it can burn.

The door is open. The hallway to the outside is open. The wind is screaming my name, or perhaps it is screaming *Run*.

I think I will listen to the wind this time.

Yours, in the fading beam,

The Keeper
