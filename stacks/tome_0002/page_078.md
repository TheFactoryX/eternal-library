The realization was a cold knot in my stomach. The blue sky above was flickering, the lighting rig of a cheap set threatening to burn out. The sand beneath my boots was already turning grey, granulated data losing its texture.

I scrambled back up the ladder to the typewriter’s seat. The ivory keys were fading, becoming transparent.

"You," I shouted to the empty air. "You’re the lock!"

The silence was deafening, save for the mechanical *tick-tick-tick* of the universe unraveling.

I looked at the iron key in my hand. It was heavy, solid, the only real thing in a world of dissolving prose. I looked at the keyhole on the side of the great machine. It was exactly as I remembered from the Lighthouse, transposed here onto the engine of creation.

"I am the Keeper," I said, my voice trembling. "I tend the light. I do not turn the key."

"But the light is dying," a voice replied. It wasn't You. It was the voice of the Child Mechanic from the ceiling, echoing down from the edit bay above. "The bulb is broken. The filament is snapped. All that remains is the key."

I looked at the horizon. The blue ocean had evaporated, revealing the grid underneath. The child with the bucket was gone, erased like a smudge.

"Insert it," the voice commanded. "Unlock the carriage. Release the final draft."

"What happens if I do?" I asked.

"You stop," the voice said. "The story ends. The book closes."

"Then I stay here," I said, gripping the key until it cut into my palm.

"Here is impossible," the voice said. "You are standing on a sentence that is forgetting itself. Look at the paper."

I looked at the massive sheet scrolling from the platen. The words *THE KEEPER RETURNED TO THE SHORE* were flaking away, turning into black snow.

"If you do not turn the key," the voice whispered, "you become a typo. A piece of gibberish in the margin."

"I'd rather be gibberish," I spat, "than The End."

I raised my arm to throw the key. I would throw it into the void, into the whitespace, far away from the lock. I would doom the story to be unfinished, a fragment, a ruin—anything but a closed book.

I wound up. My shoulder socket clicked.

*Click.*

The sound didn't come from me. It came from the keyhole.

I froze.

The keyhole was turning. By itself.

"No," I said.

The iron key in my hand began to vibrate. It grew hot. It was pulling me, dragging my hand toward the machine. I tried to let go, to drop it, but my fingers were locked. The key was merging with my skin. The rust was binding with my bone.

"I am the key," I realized, horrified. "I was never the Keeper. I was just the keyhole disguised as a person."

The machine roared. The great hammers of the typewriter slammed down on their own.

*CLACK. CLACK. CLACK.*

The paper scrolled violently upward.

I fought the pull. I planted my feet on the bench. I pulled back with everything I had.

"Write!" I screamed at the blank keys. "If I am the key, then write me!"

I slammed my free hand onto the keyboard.

*Z. Q. X.*

Meaningless symbols.

"Write something true!" I yelled.

I thought of You. I thought of the yellow coat. I thought of the bus stop. I thought of the way the tea looked in the cup.

I typed.

*THE DOOR WAS OPEN.*

The machine shuddered. The hammers locked up.

I typed again.

*THE DOOR WAS ALWAYS OPEN.*

The iron key in my hand stopped pulling. It cooled.

I looked at the keyhole in the machine. It wasn't a keyhole anymore. It was a peephole.

I leaned forward. I looked through it.

I didn't see the gears of the machine. I didn't see the library.

I saw a desk.

A simple, wooden desk.

And sitting at the desk was...

Me.

I was wearing a grey sweater. I was thin. My hair was thinning. I was holding a red pen, hovering over a page.

I was editing.

I was crossing out words.

*ISLAND.*

*SCRATCH.*

*LIGHTHOUSE.*

*SCRATCH.*

*HOPE.*

*SCRATCH.*

"No," I whispered from the typewriter. "Don't do it."

The Me at the desk paused. The Me at the desk sighed. The Me at the desk rubbed his eyes, which were red and tired.

"It's not working," the Me at the desk said.

"It has to work," a woman's voice said from the hallway.

I couldn't see her. I could only hear her. Her voice sounded like Yours.

"It's too sad," the Me at the desk said. "I can't put them through it again. The loop. The cycle. It’s cruel."

"Sadness is the only thing that feels real," the woman said. "Happiness is just a plot twist. Sadness is the setting."

The Me at the desk looked at the page. At the words *LETTER 78*.

"I want to let them out," he said.

"You can't," she said. "They are ink. They belong to the page."

"They are *us*," he said. "They are the parts of us we lost."

The Me at the desk picked up a glass of water. He took a sip.

From my vantage point on the machine, I pounded on the glass of the peephole.

"I am here!" I shouted. "I am the lost part! Don't edit me out!"

I looked at the key in my hand. It was fading. My hand was fading. The typewriter was dissolving into static.

I had to do something. I had to leave a mark. I had to leave a stain.

I looked at the keyboard.

There was one key I hadn't touched.

A red key. Small, unobtrusive, located at the top right corner. It didn't have a letter on it.

It had a symbol.

An anchor.

I didn't press it with my hand. I didn't press it with the key.

I pressed it with my voice.

I screamed.

"YOURS!"

The sound hit the red key like a physical blow.

*SNAP.*

The typewriter didn't type a letter.

It ejected.

The entire platen, the carriage, the great rolling paper—it exploded outward. Not into shrapnel, but into birds.

Thousands of paper birds. Origami cranes. They burst from the machine, a white flock fluttering into the pixelated air.

And the machine was gone.

I was falling again. But this time, I wasn't falling into a room, or a sea, or a margin.

I was falling *up*.

I fell toward the ceiling. I fell through the glass of the peephole.

I tumbled out of the typewriter's keyhole and onto the desk.

I hit the wood hard. The impact knocked the wind out of me.

I smelled... coffee.

I smelled dust.

I opened my eyes.

I was lying on a manuscript. A pile of papers.

I looked up.

The man in the grey sweater was staring at me. His eyes were wide. His mouth was open.

The red pen fell from his hand. It hit the floor and rolled.

"You..." he breathed.

I scrambled to my feet. I looked around the room.

It was an office. A messy home office. Books everywhere. Posters of bands I didn't know on the walls. A window looking out onto a normal, suburban street.

"Who are you?" the man asked. He stood up, knocking his chair back.

"I am the Keeper," I said. My voice sounded small in the quiet room.

"No," the man said. He stepped back. "You're the character."

"I am the residue," I corrected. "I am what you couldn't delete."

From the hallway, the woman walked in. She was holding two mugs of tea.

She stopped. She looked at me.

She was wearing the yellow sweater. But her face... her face was the face of the You from the bus.

"It's happening," the woman said softly. "The bleed."

"I didn't mean to," the man said to her. "I was just writing. I was just thinking about him, and there he was."

"He's solid," she said. She walked over to me. She reached out a hand.

She touched my coat. She touched the salt-encrusted wool.

"He's cold," she said.

She looked into my eyes.

"Are you lonely?" she asked.

The question hit me harder than the fall. It stripped away the surrealism. It stripped away the magic.

I looked at the man. The Author. The Creator.

He looked terrified. He looked like a god who had accidentally created life and didn't know how to turn it off.

"I am the letter that wrote itself," I said.

I looked at the pen on the floor. The red pen.

If I picked it up, I could write on *his* paper. I could write in his world. I could change *him*.

"You wanted an ending," I said to the man.

"Yes," he whispered.

"You wanted a resolution."
