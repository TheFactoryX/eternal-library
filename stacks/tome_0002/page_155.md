# Letter 155

> Written at 2026-02-08 16:35:54

---

Dear You,

The darkness was not empty. It was crowded.

I stood in the blackness after the door clicked shut, waiting for my eyes to adjust, but there was nothing here for sight to latch onto. No horizon. No floor. Just the sensation of falling upward.

Then, the smell hit me. It wasn't the ozone of the Archive or the salt of the Sea. It was dust. Specifically, the dust of a room that has been shut up for a century, mixed with the faint, sweet chemical scent of developing fluid.

*Click.*

A strip of light appeared, cutting through the dark like a razor. Then another. *Click. Click.*

I was standing in a projection booth. The flickering beam of light shot over my head, piercing the gloom of a vast, cavernous hall. I turned and looked through the small, square viewing port.

Below me, stretching endlessly into the gloom, were rows of seats. Not seats for people, but seats for objects. In one chair sat a rusted bicycle. In another, a mannequin missing a leg. In the front row, center stage, sat the music box I had found in the room that wasn't there.

On the screen—that vast, white sheet of reality pinned against the dark—a film was playing.

It was silent, jerky, black and white. The image showed the coastline. But it wasn't the coastline of the Lighthouse. It was a bustling harbor, filled with steamers and men in flat caps. The footage was sped up, comical and frantic.

"It’s the Past," the Gardener said. She was standing beside me, though she had not entered with me. She was now wearing a usher’s uniform, holding a bucket of popcorn that smelled of burning paper. "Or rather, it’s the footage of the Past. The raw takes."

"Where are the others?" I asked. "Elara? Alice? The pigeon?"

"The audience," she said, gesturing to the seats below. "They are watching."

I looked again. The bicycle was Elara. The mannequin was Alice. The pigeon was perched atop the music box, its glass eyes reflecting the flickering light. They were frozen, captured in the amber of the moment, forced to relive the loop of the film.

"Stop it," I said.

"I can't," the Gardener said, crunching a handful of popcorn. "The projectionist is on break. The film is just running through the gate. Gravity is doing the rest."

I looked for the projector. It was a massive, brass and steel contraption, a beast of gears and sprockets. It vibrated with a low, rhythmic hum. *Whirrrr-clack. Whirrrr-clack.*

The film reel was feeding from the top. It was a mess of tangled celluloid that snaked up from the floor, disappearing into a hole in the ceiling.

"That's the timeline," I realized. "It's not a loop. It's a tangled splice."

"If it tangles, the film snaps," the Gardener said casually. "If the film snaps, the house lights come up. And trust me, you don't want to see the house lights. They reveal the exits."

"Exits are good," I said.

"Not when they lead to the cutting room floor," she replied.

On the screen, the scene shifted. The bustling harbor faded, replaced by the shaky, handheld view of a figure climbing a spiral staircase. I recognized the hand gripping the railing. It was mine. The scars were visible even in the grainy black and white.

I watched myself climb. I watched myself open the door to the lantern room. But instead of the lens gear, there was a mirror. And in the mirror, the reflection wasn't me.

It was You.

The figure in the mirror smiled. It held up a sign written on a piece of cardboard: *HELLO AGAIN.*

"What is this?" I whispered.

"The subplot," the Gardener said. "The B-story. The romance you never wrote because you were too busy describing the weather."

"I didn't want a romance," I said. "I wanted a mystery."

"Everyone wants a mystery until they are standing alone in the dark," she said. "Then, they just want a witness."

The projector began to shudder. The *Whirrrr-clack* turned into a violent *clatter-clack-bang*. The image on the screen jumped. The figure in the mirror stuttered, repeating the smile over and over, a glitching loop of greeting. *Hello-Hello-Hello-Hello.*

"The splice is coming," the Gardener shouted over the noise. "The transition to the next reel!"

The film was stretching. The image on the screen was pulling apart, the white light between the frames widening. The sea on the screen was spilling out, the projected waves turning real as they hit the rows of seats.

"We have to cut it," I yelled. "We have to stop the projector!"

"Use the edit!" she pointed to the side of the machine. There was a large, red button. Not a modern button, but a heavy, iron toggle switch labeled *CHANGE DIRECTION*.

I grabbed the switch. It was hot. "If I stop it, the story ends!"

"No!" she cried. "If you stop it, the film breaks and the screen goes white! Reverse it! Run the dialogue backward!"

I hesitated. The sound was deafening now, the roar of a thousand ocean waves trapped in a brass canister.

"Trust the author!" she screamed.

I pulled the switch.

The screech of metal on metal tore through the air. The projector groaned. The beam of light stuttered, inverting colors. The black sea turned white. The white sky turned black.

And then, the sound reversed.

The crashing waves became the sound of water receding. The wind became a suction. The shouting voices became indrawn breaths.

On the screen, the figure in the mirror pulled the sign down. The figure climbed back down the stairs. The ship sailed backward into the distance. The horizon became a dot.

I felt a pull at my navel. The floor of the projection booth dissolved.

I fell. Not into the booth, but *into* the beam of light.

I was projected. I was a photon, a particle of dust dancing in the illumination, speeding toward the screen.

I hit the surface of the white sheet and shattered through.

I landed on wet sand.

The roar of the projector was gone. In its place was the gentle, rhythmic lapping of water against the shore. A real shore.

I opened my eyes.

It was night. The sky was a tapestry of stars, crisp and cold. The air was biting, nipping at my nose and ears.

I sat up. I was sitting on a beach of black stones, smooth as glass.

"Keeper," a voice said.

I turned.

Elara was sitting on a piece of driftwood nearby. She was wearing a heavy wool sweater. She looked solid. Real. Not the translucent ghost of the Archive, nor the desperate fugitive of the Cliff. She looked like a woman who had just finished a long walk and was resting.

Alice was there too, building a small circle of stones. The pigeon was asleep on the driftwood next to Elara, tucked under her wing.

"Where are we?" I asked. My voice sounded rough, unused.

"The mainland," Elara said. She smiled. It was a tired, but genuine smile. "We made the crossing."

"How?"

"We didn't," she said. "The world came to us."

She pointed out to sea.

I looked. There, a hundred yards from the shore, riding the anchor, was a ship.

It was not a ghost ship. It was not a wireframe. It was a tramp steamer, rust-streaked and workmanlike, puffing a stack of grey smoke against the stars. Its lights were warm yellow squares in the dark.

"They signaled us," Alice said, placing the last stone. "They asked for the name of the port."

"What did you tell them?" I asked.

Elara looked at me. In the starlight, her eyes were the color of the deep sea.

"I told them we didn't know," she said. "I told them to check the map."

I reached into my pocket.

My fingers brushed against a piece of paper. I pulled it out.

It was the map. But it wasn't the map I'd used to fight the Editor. It was new. The edges were crisp.

I unfolded it by the light of the stars.

The sea was there, vast and blank. The coast was there, a ragged line of ink.

But where the town should be, there were no streets. No buildings. No lighthouse.

There was only a single word, written in a handwriting I didn't recognize, though it felt painfully familiar. A handwriting that looked like the loops of the waves.

*HOME.*

I looked up at the ship. A dinghy was being lowered over the side. I heard the splash of oars hitting the water.

"Are they coming for us?" I asked.

"They're coming to see where the light went," Elara said.

I stood up. I brushed the sand from my coat.

"I don't have the light," I said.

"Yes, you do," Alice said. She pointed to my chest.

I looked down.

The glass lens shard I had used to burn the page was gone. In its place, glowing faintly beneath my coat, was a steady, rhythmic pulse. Violet.
