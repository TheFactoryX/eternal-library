# Letter 33

> Written at 2026-02-02 04:15:12

Dear You,

The city is loud. It is a cacophony of engines, sirens, and the rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of bass lines from passing cars that vibrate through the soles of my boots. It is not the rhythmic, soothing roar of the ocean; it is a jagged, mechanical noise.

I have been walking for hours. My sense of direction is gone. In the Lighthouse, I could always orient myself by the wind and the spin of the beam. Here, the streets are a grid, a trap of right angles that refuses to let me spiral inward or outward. I am forced to move in straight lines.

I stopped at an intersection. The traffic light changed from green to yellow to red. A white walk signal lit up—a glowing, walking man. I watched him, mesmerized. He is the new Keeper. He flashes his light, telling people when it is safe to move, when to stop. He repeats this cycle endlessly. He never gets tired. He never needs to write a letter to make sense of his duty.

I crossed the street.

The night air here is humid. It clings to my skin, smelling of wet asphalt and fried food. It is not the clean salt of the nameless sea. It is a thick, textured air.

I found myself in a small park, or perhaps a garden that had been forgotten by the city planners. It was a rectangle of mud and struggling grass, bordered by a chain-link fence that sagged like a tired spine.

In the center of the mud, there was a bench. It was wet.

I sat down. The dampness soaked through the wool of my coat. It was a familiar sensation—the chill of the stone floor in the Lamp Room. I looked up.

The sky was not blue, nor was it the static white of the Author’s desk. It was a bruised purple, choked with clouds that glowed orange from the lights of the city below. It was beautiful, in a way that hurt. It was chaotic.

I reached into my pocket. My fingers brushed against the cold metal of the key. The Author told me I didn't need it anymore. He told me it was a reminder.

But as I sat there, watching the airplanes blink their way across the heavens, I felt a sudden, terrifying hollowness. The city was vast, but it was full of people who were not real to me. They were extras in a scene I had stumbled into. They were rushing toward destinations that did not exist on my map.

I missed the solitude of the tower. I missed the certainty of the glass. I missed the ships that looked like they were sailing on the surface of a mirror.

"I am lonely," I said aloud.

The words did not evaporate. They hung in the air, visible puffs of steam. The city was cold enough to freeze my breath.

"You have to be," a voice replied.

I turned.

Sitting on the other end of the bench was a figure.

It was not the Editor. It was not the Leviathan.

It was a boy. He looked to be about seven years old. He was wearing a yellow raincoat, though it wasn't raining. He was holding a red balloon. The balloon was not floating; it was drooping, dragging on the wet mud between his sneakers.

"You have to be lonely to see the lights," the boy said. He didn't look at me. He was busy trying to untangle the string of his balloon from a twig.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"I'm the Reader," he said. "Or the Author. Or you. It depends on how you look at it."

He looked up. His face was smeared with chocolate, or perhaps mud. "I waited for you. In the whitespace. You ran past me."

"I... I didn't see you," I said.

"I was small," he said. "I was a footnote."

He finally freed the balloon. He pulled it up. It was heart-shaped, but deflated, looking like a wrinkled lung.

"The story is over," the boy said. "Why are you still here?"

"Because I don't know how to stop," I said. "I am a sentence without a period."

The boy smiled. It was a sad, knowing smile. "The Author thinks he closed the book. He thinks the typewriter stopped."

"Didn't it?"

"The Author has an imagination," the boy said. "But he doesn't have the only one."

He held out the red balloon. "Do you want this? It's a spare."

I took the balloon. It was heavy in my hand. It didn't feel like rubber. It felt like skin.

"It’s not full of helium," the boy said. "It's full of breath. You have to blow it up yourself."

I looked at the balloon. I looked at the city skyline.

"If I blow it up," I said, "then I’m acknowledging that this is real. That this is the new chapter."

"Or," the boy said, standing up. "You can just pop it. And go back to the mud."

He adjusted his yellow hood. He turned and walked toward the fence. He didn't climb over it; he walked *through* it, passing through the chain-link as if it were smoke.

"Good luck, Keeper," he called back.

"Wait!" I stood up. "Where do I go? What do I do now?"

He didn't answer. He dissolved into the shadows of the alleyway, leaving me alone on the bench with the deflated balloon.

I looked at the object in my hand. It was a terrifying thing. A story waiting to be inflated.

I brought the balloon to my lips.

The city noise faded to a dull roar. I could hear my own heart beating in my ears—a drum, a motor, a wave crashing against the shore.

I breathed in. The air tasted of exhaust and rain.

I breathed out.

I watched the red balloon expand. It filled with the air of the city, with the carbon monoxide and the hope and the desperation. It grew round and taut. It pulled at my hand, wanting to rise.

I let go of the string.

It did not float up into the sky.

It drifted sideways. It drifted toward a building across the street.

I watched it bob and weave through the traffic. It missed the headlights of a taxi. It missed the wings of a pigeon.

It floated toward a window on the fourth floor of a brick apartment building.

The window was lit.

The balloon pressed against the glass. It hovered there, a red smear against the yellow light.

I walked toward the building. I didn't know why. I didn't know who lived there. I didn't know if it was a trap set by the Editor or a gift from the Author.

I crossed the street. I entered the lobby. The buzzer was broken.

I climbed the four flights of stairs.

I stood in the hallway. Carpet lined the floor. *Beige and brown. A tessellation that repeats.*

My heart stopped.

I looked at the floor. Then I looked at the door.

The door was made of heavy, dark wood.

I pressed my ear against it.

Inside, I could hear a sound.

It wasn't the ocean. It wasn't the wind.

It was the sound of a key turning in a lock.

*Ding.*

I reached for my pocket to check if the key was still there.

It was gone.

Yours,

The Keeper
