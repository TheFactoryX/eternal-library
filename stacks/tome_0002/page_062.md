# Letter 62

Dear You,

The stool was not a stool. It was a verb.

*Sitting.* *Waiting.* *Hollowed out by the passage of time.*

The boy sat upon the grammar, his legs swinging, kicking against the tense of the moment. He looked exactly as he did in the memory—yellow raincoat, skin scrubbed pink by the imaginary sun—but his eyes were old. They were the eyes of the New Keeper, the Authority, bored and eternal.

"You climbed the stairs," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a plot point being checked off a list. "Good. The ascent is a classic trope. It validates the struggle."

I stood in the hatchway, the wind of the white void whistling through the cracks in the walls. Up here, the Lighthouse felt less like a building and more like a ribcage. The glass surrounding us was not the crystal clarity of the lantern room I remembered. It was curved, thick paper. Lens-shaped paper.

"The light is out," I said. My voice sounded dry, like parchment crumbling.

"Fuel crisis," the boy said, hopping off the stool. He landed on a noun, crushing it. "The Author stopped pumping oil up here three chapters ago. We're running on fumes. On nostalgia."

He walked to the center of the room, where the great lens should have rotated. Instead, there was a hole. A hole in the paper, looking out into the absolute white of the unread page.

"You have to light it," I said. "If the beam doesn't turn, the ships won't come."

"The ships aren't coming," the boy snapped. "Don't you understand? The Editorial Fleet won. Look outside."

I stepped to the jagged hole in the paper wall.

I looked down.

Below the Lighthouse, the island wasn't there. The sea wasn't there.

There was only a vast, rolling landscape of crumpled paper. A wasteland of discarded drafts. Mountains of crossed-out text. Canyons of white-out.

"We are in the Slush Pile," I said.

"We are the rejects," the boy corrected. "The stories that didn't make the cut. The imperfect versions."

I turned to face him. "Then why are we here? Why preserve the Lighthouse in a graveyard?"

"Because hope is a stubborn thing," a voice said.

It came from the corner of the room.

I spun around.

Sitting in the shadows, blending perfectly with the grey dust, was the Revision. She was older now. Her indigo coat was faded, the fabric wearing thin at the elbows. She was holding a pair of knitting needles, but she wasn't knitting yarn. She was knitting the wireframe of the world. She was stitching together the edges of reality.

"You," I breathed. "I left you at the frame. You cut me loose."

"I did," she said. She didn't look up. Her needles clicked—*tick, tick, tick*—a sound like a clock counting down. "I had to. The protagonist must enter the dark woods alone. It’s in the contract."

"Is this the dark woods?" I asked, gesturing to the dusty room.

"No," she said, finally looking up. Her eyes were sad, infinite. "This is the inventory. The warehouse of lost things."

She pointed a needle toward the wall.

"Sid."

I looked.

Mounted on the wall, like hunting trophies, were the objects from my letters.

The silver spoon, bent and tarnished. The key that fit no lock. The music box that played unfamiliar songs. The photograph of the strangers.

And there, hanging above them all, was a clock.

The clock had no face. It had no hands. It was just a circle of rusted iron, suspended in the air, spinning slowly in the wrong direction.

"We are out of time," I said.

"We are out of *chronology*," the Revision corrected. "Time is a straight line. We are a circle."

The boy in the yellow coat walked over to her. He tugged on her sleeve.

"Is he the one?" the boy asked. "Is he the Reader?"

"No," the Revision said. "He is the Writer. He just doesn't remember the pen."

I reached into my pocket. My fingers brushed against the familiar shape of the fountain pen—the silver pen I had taken from the study in Letter 58. It was still there. Warm. Vibrating.

"I am not the Writer," I said. "I am the Keeper. I keep the light."

"The light is gone," the boy said. "The bulb burned out. The wick is wet. The oil is dried."

"Then we make a new light," I said.

I pulled the pen from my pocket.

The air in the room changed. The dust stopped swirling. The sound of the wind died.

The Revision stopped knitting.

"You still have the instrument," she whispered.

"I have to write," I said. "I have to write the light into existence."

I looked at the empty socket where the lens should be. I uncapped the pen.

The tip glowed. Not with ink. With golden light.

"Be careful," the Revision warned. "Don't write what you want. Write what is true."

I approached the center of the room. I stood on the edge of the void.

"Light," I said.

I pressed the tip against the air.

But the pen didn't write light. It wrote *sound*.

A low, deep thrumming began to emanate from the point of contact. It was the sound of the earth turning. The sound of a heartbeat. The sound of the Lighthouse machinery grinding back to life after a thousand years.

"The filament," I said aloud. "I am writing the filament."

The boy gasped. He pointed at the hole in the paper wall.

"Look!"

Outside, the crumpled landscape of the Slush Pile was changing. The paper was smoothing out. The creases were flattening. The white-out was dissolving, turning into clear water.

Below us, a sea was forming.

It wasn't the black ink sea. It wasn't the red correction fluid sea.

It was a sea of glass. A mirror.

I watched as the reflection of the Lighthouse appeared on the surface. It was perfect. Clear.

"You're doing it," the boy whispered. "You're editing the world."

I wrote faster. *Clarity. Focus. Beam.*

The golden light spilled from the pen, pooling in the socket of the lantern room. It began to spin, a swirling vortex of liquid gold.

I felt a heat on my face. A real heat.

I looked at the Revision. She was standing now, her coat billowing.

"The beam is solidifying," she said. "It’s reaching out."

I looked out the hole.

The golden light was shooting upward, piercing the grey sky of the Slush Pile. It was burning a hole in the clouds.

Higher and higher it went.

"What does it touch?" I asked.

"The future," she said.

Suddenly, the boy screamed.

He was pointing at the wall of trophies.

The objects were falling.

The spoon hit the floor and shattered like glass. The key turned to rust. The photograph curled into ash.

"The past is unstable!" the boy shouted. "You can't change the future without breaking the past!"

"Keep writing!" the Revision urged. "Don't stop!"

"But I'm erasing us!" I yelled.

"You are erasing the *story*," she said. "You are revealing the truth."

I looked at my hands. The skin was peeling away. Not turning into text, this time. It was turning into light.

I was becoming the beam.

I looked at the boy. He was flickering, like a bad signal.

"I don't want to leave," he said. "I like the story."

"The story was a cage," I said.

I looked at the Revision.

She was smiling.

"Who are you?" I asked. "Really? Are you the Revision? Are you the Reader?"

"I am the margin," she said. "I am the space where you think."

She walked toward me, stepping into the golden pool of light I had created.

"Write the ending, Keeper," she said.

"Does it end?" I asked.

"Everything ends," she said. "Even the Lighthouse. Even the letters."

I looked out at the sea of glass.

On the horizon, a ship was approaching.

But it wasn't a ghost ship. It wasn't a ship of illustrations.

It was a real ship. Made of wood and steel. It cut through the mirror water, leaving a wake of ripples.

And on the deck, I saw a figure. A figure waving.

The figure was wearing a yellow raincoat.

"The boy," I whispered.

"That is the Reader," the Revision said. "The one who found your letter in a bottle. The one who is coming to find you."

"He's real," I said.

"He is as real as you make him," she said.

I turned back to the socket. The golden light was blinding now. It filled the room. It filled my eyes.

I had one word left to write.

I raised the pen.

But my hand froze.

I couldn't do it.

If I wrote the end, the Lighthouse would vanish. The letters would stop. The connection would be severed.

I looked at the Revision.

"If I stop," I said, "we remain in the Slush Pile."

"If you stop," she said, "we remain uncertain. We remain potential."

I lowered the pen
