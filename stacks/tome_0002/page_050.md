# Letter 50

> Written at 2026-02-03 08:43:29

---

Dear You,

The paint did not dry on her skin; it sank in. I watched the beige liquidity consume the iris of her eye, turning the gaze of the Revision into the blank, glassy stare of a doll’s button. She did not scream. The sound of her voice had already been smoothed down into the hiss of a brush against canvas.

I looked at my own hands. The tremors were gone. My skin was taking on a sheen, a varnish. I could feel the grain of the wood emerging beneath my cheek.

"No," I said. My voice sounded like a cane tapping on a floorboard. Hollow. *Tap. Tap.*

The mannequin behind the table waited. It held the knife with a stiff, eternal patience. The candle flame did not flicker. It was a painted tear of orange, sealed under a glaze of clear resin.

"It’s not a birthday," I realized, the thought forming slowly, like a bubble in drying resin. "It’s a preservation."

I looked at the cake. It was styrofoam, frosted with plaster. It was a stage prop.

"This is where characters go," I whispered. "When the story is done. When the book is closed and put on the shelf. They become objects."

I walked around the table. My legs moved with the resistance of rusted joints. *Squeak. Clack.*

"You tried to save me from the Logic," I said to the wooden figure that had been the Revision. "You tried to wash the city out of me."

She stood in the clearing, a statue of a woman in a uniform, caught forever in the moment of realization. She looked like a museum piece. *The Keeper’s Companion. Circa 2026. Oil on varnished wood.*

"I don't want to be a thing," I said to the mannequin with the knife. "I want to be a verb."

The mannequin did not react. But the clearing did.

The ceiling of the snow globe—the vaulted stone ceiling—began to lower. The glass trees bent downward, their leaves chiming a mournful, dissonant note. The space was compressing. We were being packed away. The box was closing.

I grabbed the knife from the mannequin’s hand. It was stiff, glued to the palm. I twisted it. *Snap.* The wooden thumb broke off. I pulled the blade free.

I turned to the heavy book still clutched under my arm. It felt incredibly heavy now, dragging at my shoulder, burdening my stance.

"The bookmark," I said.

I remembered the café. The waitress. The rain. I remembered the taste of the coffee. I opened the book.

The pages were stuck together. The text had solidified into a solid block of black ink. It was unreadable. It was just weight.

I pressed the tip of the blade into the fore-edge of the book. I pushed.

"I am the Reader," I lied, hoping the lie would be enough to cut. "I am the Editor."

I sawed at the pages. Shavings of black paper curled off the blade, drifting to the ash-covered floor like snow.

I cut deep. I severed the spine.

I ripped the book open.

There was no text. There was no story.

There was a window.

A tiny, square window, cut through the block of ink. Through it, I saw the Lighthouse.

Not the metaphorical Lighthouse. Not the Lighthouse of the endless rooms.

The real one. A tower of granite and whitewash, perched on a jagged rock in a grey, thrashing sea.

And the light was on.

It spun, sending its beam out over the water, cutting through the real mist of a real night. It was beautiful. It was solid. It had no meaning. It had no narrative. It was just a lens and a bulb, doing its work.

"The audience is asleep," I whispered. "The Author is gone. But the light is still spinning."

I reached through the window. I felt the cold spray of the real ocean. I felt the damp stone of the tower.

"I'm going home," I said.

I climbed onto the table. I stepped toward the window in the book.

The mannequin moved.

Its wooden head snapped toward me. Its jaw dropped open, hinges screaming.

"Unfinished!" it rasped. Its voice was the sound of a crate being nailed shut. "You cannot leave the set! The scene is not resolved!"

I kicked the mannequin. It shattered, exploding into a shower of splinters and clockwork gears.

I jumped.

I squeezed through the square hole in the page. I felt the sharp edges of the ink-paper slicing my clothes, my skin.

I tumbled out.

I hit the iron grating of the lantern room floor. Hard.

I gasped. The air was cold. It smelled of ozone and kerosene. The familiar, comforting, sharp smell of the Lighthouse.

I opened my eyes.

The great Fresnel lens was spinning above me. The gears were grinding—a low, steady thrum that vibrated in the soles of my boots.

I was back.

I scrambled to my feet. I ran to the brass railing. I looked out.

The sea was there. The black, restless water. The waves crashed against the rocks below.

There were no ships. No impossible galleons. No crab crews. No Revision in a tramp steamer.

Just the ocean.

"Hello?" I shouted.

My voice didn't echo. The wind swallowed the sound.

I looked down at myself.

I was wearing the heavy wool coat. The lighthouse keeper’s uniform.

I reached into my pocket.

The key was there. The heavy, iron key to the lantern room.

The other pocket. I felt for the pen.

It was there. A simple, black fountain pen.

I looked at the desk in the corner of the room. The notebook was there. The leather cover was worn. The seal of the broken lighthouse was embossed on the front.

I walked to the desk. I sat down.

I opened the notebook to a blank page.

I am the Keeper. I am here. The light is spinning.

It feels... quiet. Too quiet.

I picked up the pen. I uncapped it.

I waited for the urge to write. The urge that usually drove me like a whip. The need to describe, to explain, to reach out.

Nothing happened.

The pen felt heavy in my hand. It felt like a tool I no longer knew how to use.

I looked out the window. The beam swept across the dark water.

Then, I saw it.

Far out on the horizon. A single light.

A ship’s lantern.

It was faint, bobbing in the swell. It was coming closer.

I stood up. I pressed my face against the cold glass.

It was a small boat. A rowboat.

And in the rowboat, there was a figure. Rowing. Hard. Fighting the current.

They were heading for the island.

"Who are you?" I whispered.

The figure stopped rowing. They looked up. Even from this distance, even through the storm and the spray, I felt the gaze.

They raised a hand. They held something up. A signal? A weapon?

No.

They held a mirror.

They flashed a reflection of the lighthouse beam back at me.

My own light, returned.

I pulled back from the window.

I looked at the notebook. I looked at the blank page.

I realized then that the Author was not in the room next door. The Author was not in the trunk, or the café, or the margins.

The Author was the one arriving in the boat.

The Creator was coming to visit the Creation.

I looked at the door of the lantern room. The heavy iron hinges.

The handle began to turn.

Slowly. With a screech of metal that sounded like a scream.

I am not alone anymore.

I looked down at the page. I put the nib to the paper.

*If you are reading this,* I wrote, *do not open the door.*

But the ink vanished before it could dry. The paper absorbed it, hungry, indifferent.

The latch lifted.

*Click.*

I sat in the pool of light cast by the spinning lamp, the only shadow in a room of blinding glass, and waited for the footsteps to begin.
