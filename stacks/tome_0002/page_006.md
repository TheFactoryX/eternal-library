# Letter 6

> Written at 2026-01-31 18:37:55

---

Dear You,

I did not run. The urge was there, a frantic bird beating its wings against the inside of my ribs, but my legs betrayed me. They have grown too accustomed to the spiral, too fond of the cold, hard certainty of the stone. So I did not flee through the door into the drying sea. Instead, I stood there, in the blinding white heat of the Lamp Room, and I turned around.

The room was empty.

The wet, webbed hand, the scent of vanilla and decay, the towering shadow with wings that spanned the wall—they were gone. Only the salt remained, crystallizing on the brass railing like a frost. The beam of the light cut through the air, a solid rotating blade of brightness, and in its rhythm, I felt a profound sense of disappointment. Is it mad to miss a ghost? To prefer the company of a monster to the solitude of a silent room?

I looked down at my arm. The cut was still there, a neat, red line parting the skin of my forearm. But the blood… the blood was red again. Rich, dark, human crimson. It welled up slowly, thick and warm. I pressed my thumb against it, feeling the throb of my pulse—*thump, thump, thump*—real and undeniable. The black ink-blood was a lie, or perhaps a hallucination born of the fumes.

Or maybe, I thought, touching the wound, the Lighthouse simply changed its mind about what I am.

I descended the stairs. The descent felt different this time. The air was thinner, sharper. The walls of the tower seemed to shrink inward, the stones pressing closer as if the tower was folding itself in the absence of the ocean’s weight.

When I reached the study, the silence that I had feared—the silence that followed the breaking of the photograph—was gone. In its place was a sound I can only describe as *growth*. A wet, creaking, stretching sound. Vines.

The room was overgrown. But not with ordinary plants. These were vines made of paper. Thick, pulpy tendrils, covered in handwriting, had burst from the cracks in the mortar, from the keyholes, and from the very drawer where I kept the now-empty inkwell. They crawled across the floor, twisted up the legs of the desk, and coiled around the music box like serpents.

I approached cautiously. The ink on the vines was fresh, glistening wetly in the dim light. I leaned in close to read the text on a thick, leaf-like tendril near my face.

It was my handwriting. But it wasn't my words.

*“The keeper tends the flame. The keeper forgets the name. The keeper is the fuel.”*

Over and over, the phrase repeated, spiraling around the stem of the paper-vine. I touched it. The paper felt warm, feverish. Beneath the surface, I could feel a rapid, fluttering pulse, like the heartbeat of a tiny bird.

The Lighthouse is not just burning my memories; it is *physicalizing* them. It is turning the letters I write into biomass.

I grabbed a pair of shears from the drawer—they were rusted, the blades dull—and I began to cut. I slashed at the vines, severing the connection between the desk and the wall. The paper did not tear; it bled. Black ink sprayed across my coat, staining the fabric like oil. The vines shrieked—a high-pitched sound like tearing canvas—and recoiled, slithering back into the shadows of the room.

I could not stay there. The study was becoming a press, a trap designed to compost me into pulp.

I went to the window. The view outside had shifted yet again. The ocean floor, exposed for that brief, terrifying eternity, was no longer a barren wasteland of kelp and stranded leviathans. It had changed. The water had not returned; the land had risen.

Where the sea used to be, there is now a forest of iron trees. Great, rusted trunks rise from the seabed, their branches made of twisted cables and rigging. Hanging from the branches like fruit are the hulls of ships—the trireme, the black iron vessel, others I had not seen before. They are caught in the branches, impaled on the metal trees, swinging gently in the wind that no longer smells of salt, but of ozone and old iron.

And in the distance, where the horizon used to be, I see the lights.

They are not campfires. They are windows. A city of glass and crystal is rising from the earth, its spires piercing the sky. The city from my dream. The city of broken glass. It is no longer a dream. It is the destination.

I turned back to the room. The vines were regrowing, inching across the floorboards with a sickening, wet rustle. I knew then that I could not stay in the Lighthouse. It was digesting me. If I slept, I would wake up as part of the architecture. I would become a pillar, a hinge, a lock.

But I could not leave empty-handed. I needed something. An anchor.

I went to the music box. It sat buried under a mound of paper vines. I pried them loose, ignoring the black ink that coated my hands. I opened the lid. The mirror inside—the one that showed the reflection of the room—was still cracked. But when I looked into it, I did not see the study. I saw the city. I saw the crystal spires and the lights. And I saw a figure standing at the base of the tallest tower.

It was you.

You were looking up at the spire, holding something in your arms. A bundle? A child? A book?

You turned, and for the first time, the reflection was clear. You saw me. You raised a hand and touched the surface of the mirror from the other side.

The glass of the mirror cracked outward. A fissure appeared in the air of the study, a jagged tear in reality that smelled of rain and asphalt. Through the tear, I could hear the city. Car horns. Voices. The hum of electricity.

The wind howled, not from the window, but from the tear in the mirror. It sucked the papers off the desk. It pulled the inkwell into the void.

I understood then. The Lighthouse is not a prison. It is a dam. It is holding back the flood of the real world because the real world is where you are, and if I remember you, I must leave this place.

The vines lunged. A thick rope of paper wrapped around my ankle, dragging me toward the wall. The floorboards groaned. The ceiling lowered.

I grabbed the music box. I didn't close it. I let it play. I let the sound of the city—the rain, the traffic, the distant murmur of a million living souls—fill the room.

"Run," the engraving had said.

So I ran.

I am running now. I am writing this as I sprint down the spiral stairs. The stairs are changing as I descend. The stone is turning to asphalt. The damp air is smelling of exhaust fumes and wet pavement.

I passed the third floor landing, and there was a streetlamp instead of a torch bracket. I passed the second floor, and I saw a traffic light suspended in the stairwell, blinking yellow.

Behind me, the Lighthouse is screaming. It is a mechanical, grinding shriek of metal on metal, a sound of gears stripping and lenses shattering. It knows I am leaving. It knows the fuel is escaping.

I can hear the ocean surging back, a tsunami rushing to fill the void I am creating by leaving. But I am faster. I have to be faster.

I am at the bottom door. The heavy iron door that leads to the outside. It used to lead to the rocks. I don't know what it leads to now.

The music box is playing louder now, a cacophony of city sounds. A subway train rattles in the distance. A dog barks.

I reach for the handle. My hand is covered in ink, in the blood of the paper vines, in the sweat of fear.

I am turning the knob.

If I open this door, I know the Lighthouse will vanish. The letters will stop. The endless loop will break. I will find you in the city of glass. I will ask you your name.

But what if the city is just another room? What if the streetlights are just more mirrors?

I can hear the water rushing up the stairs behind me, cold and violent. The Lighthouse will not let me go easily.

I am pushing the door. It is heavy, rusted shut.

Wait.

The handle is warm.

I look down, and the handle is not iron. It is flesh. It is a hand, reaching up through the wood of the door, offering a grip.

It is your hand.

I take it.

Yours, across the threshold,

The Keeper
