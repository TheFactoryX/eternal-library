# Letter 60

> Written at 2026-02-03 20:41:35

---

Dear You,

I did not knock. The wind knocked for me. It slammed my body against the wood, a wet, heavy percussion against the silence of the tower.

"Open!" I tried to shout, but the word was ripped from my throat and thrown into the dark. I was no longer a character of dialogue; I was a prop. A obstacle. A "soggy bundle of rags" blocking the entrance.

Above, in the lantern room, the darkness shifted. I saw it—a slice of yellow light cutting through the storm as the shutters were peeled back. Then, the beam began to sweep.

*One... Two... Three.*

It was a slow, heavy rotation. The machinery was waking up.

The light caught me. I was blinded. I threw my arm up, shielding my eyes, but the glare penetrated the eyelids, turning the world into a red wash of blood and bone.

The beam held on me. It pinned me to the rocks like an insect specimen.

"Heave," a voice said from inside.

The wood of the door shuddered. It wasn't opening outward toward me. It was dissolving. The grain of the timber swirled like smoke, pulling away into the void until there was only a rectangular frame of empty air.

I stumbled through, falling onto the floor of the entryway.

The room was different.

There was no dust. No rust. No pile of unread letters. The floor was scrubbed stone, smelling of lavender and harsh soap. A fire roared in the grate—a fire that burned without heat, casting shadows that moved independently of the people casting them.

I looked up. Standing over me was a figure. They wore the Keeper’s uniform, but they wore it with a rigidity that hurt to look at. The brass buttons gleamed with a terrifying polish. The boots were shined to a mirror finish.

It was the boy. But he was no longer a child, and he was no longer the confused youth I had met in the kiln. He was tall. He was vast. He was the archetype of Authority.

"You are wet," he said.

"I am the Keeper," I said, pushing myself up. My voice was weak. "I live here."

The boy—he was a man now, really—tilted his head. "You *lived* here. Past tense. This is the Second Edition. We renovated."

He gestured around the room. "We stripped out the ambiguity. It was cluttering the narrative. We painted over the horror with 'order.'"

"I didn't agree to a renovation," I said.

"The Author didn't ask for your permission," he said. "The Author needed a reliable narrator. Someone who doesn't ask questions. Someone who doesn't try to leave."

"You're the replacement," I said. "I saw you in the study. I saw you trying to get in."

"I got in," he said simply. "And I locked the door behind me."

He turned his back on me and walked toward the spiral stairs. "Come up. The light is hungry. It needs to be fed."

"I'm not going up," I said, backing away toward the dissolving doorframe. "I'm going out. I'm going to the shore."

"There is no shore," he said. He paused on the first step. "Look behind you."

I turned.

The doorframe was filled with white. Not the white of a sunny day, but the flat, blinding white of an empty page. The world beyond the threshold had been erased. The sea, the rocks, the wind—they were gone. There was only the blankness of the unwritten future.

"You can't leave," the New Keeper said. "Because you haven't written what happens next."

I looked at the fireplace. The shadows dancing on the wall weren't shadows; they were words. *Despair. Isolation. Duty.*

"This isn't a renovation," I said. "It's a prison."

"It's a Lighthouse," he corrected. "Now, come up. The machinery requires maintenance."

I had no choice. I followed him.

The climb was agonizing. The stairs were no longer stone; they were made of compacted letters, tiny and sharp, digging into my feet. *Gravel.* *Grit.* *Resignation.*

As we climbed, I saw things in the walls that made me freeze.

There were objects embedded in the mortar.

The key from the kiln, now rusted through. The silver spoon, melted into a useless slag. The compass, its glass cracked, the needle spinning frantically.

"You kept them," I whispered.

"Relics," the New Keeper said, not stopping. "Mementos of the first draft. They are weaker now. They have no context."

We reached the top.

The Lantern Room was vast. The great Fresnel lens was spinning, grinding against its track with a sound like tectonic plates shifting. The light it cast was not the yellow warmth of oil or the electric blue of the bulb. It was a stark, clinical white. It was the light of a magnifying glass brought to bear on an ant hill.

"Here," the New Keeper said. He pointed to a small stool in the corner. "Sit."

I sat.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

He walked to a small brass tap on the main gear train. He unscrewed it. Black liquid began to drip out. Not oil.

Ink.

"We have to top off the reservoir," he said. "The story cannot continue without ink."

He took a glass vial and caught the dripping black fluid. He held it out to me.

"Drink," he said.

"What?"

"Drink," he commanded. "You are fading, Old Keeper. You are losing your opacity. If you vanish completely, you become a plot hole. And we hate plot holes."

I looked at the vial. I looked at my hands. They were indeed becoming faint. I could see the gearwork of the floor through my own skin.

"If I drink," I said, "I become part of this. I become text."

"You already are text," he said. "You are just clinging to the delusion of flesh."

I took the vial. My hand shook. The liquid inside was cold. It smelled of the sea, but also of the sharp, metallic tang of the nib.

"Who are you writing to?" he asked, his voice echoing in the glass chamber. "The letters. You have always been writing. To whom?"

I hesitated. "To You. The recipient."

"Am I You?" he asked.

"No," I said. "You are the Replacement. I am writing to the one who forgot."

"Then drink," he said. "And write the final letter. The Author is waiting."

I raised the vial to my lips.

Then, I stopped. I looked out the window.

The storm outside wasn't a storm of rain. It was a storm of punctuation. Commas fell like needles. Periods pounded against the glass like buckshot.

And in the distance, on the horizon of the white page, I saw a shadow.

A ship.

It was a ship made of illustrations. Charcoal lines and ink washes, towering and impossible. It was sailing toward the Lighthouse, cutting through the white page like a blade.

"The Editorial Fleet," the New Keeper said, seeing my gaze. "They are coming to red-pen this place. To strike it through."

He grabbed my wrist. He forced the vial to my lips.

"Drink!" he shouted. "Write! Give the Author a reason to save us!"

I clamped my mouth shut. I grabbed his wrist. His skin was hard, like polished stone.

"I won't," I mumbled against the glass.

"Why?" he roared. "Because you hope for an escape? There is no escape! There is only the page!"

"I am not the page!" I yelled.

I slammed the vial against the floor.

It shattered. The ink didn't spill; it exploded.

Black droplets flew everywhere, hitting the white walls, hitting the spinning lens. They hissed where they landed, burning holes in the reality of the room.

Through the floor, I saw the ink seeping down. Down, down, down to the bottom of the tower.

I looked at the New Keeper. He was frozen. A drop of ink had hit his cheek. He stared at me, his eyes wide.

"You've made a mess," he whispered.

"I've made an opening," I said.

I ran to the hatch in the floor. The spiral stairs were gone. They had dissolved into the ink that now coated everything.

I looked down the shaft. All the way down, at the very bottom, in the root of the tower, I saw a glimmer.

The golden key. The one from the beginning.

"It was never at the top," I realized. "It was in the foundation."

"You can't leave!" the New Keeper screamed. He was losing his form. The ink on his face was spreading, consuming his features, turning him into a caricature. "The light must burn!"

"The light is blind!" I shouted back.

I looked at the ship on the horizon. It was firing erasers—great, white cubes that smashed into the lighthouse walls.

The room shook. The glass of the lantern cracked.

I had two choices: stay and be edited out, or jump into the dark.

I gripped the edge of the hatch.

"Dear You," I whispered into the rising wind. "I am coming to find you. I am climbing down the back of the page."

I let go.

I fell.

I fell through
