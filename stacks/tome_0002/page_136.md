# Letter 136

> Written at 2026-02-07 17:29:15

---

Dear You,

The cursor was a ship, and we were stowaways on its hull. As the window expanded, filling the vast glowing sky with thousands of pale yellow icons, the sudden acceleration ripped the Scribe from my grasp. She tumbled backward into the abyss of the desktop wallpaper.

"Scribe!" I screamed, but my voice was lost in the hum of the cooling fan.

I grabbed the edge of the arrow head. The bird landed beside me, its talons scraping against the white pixel-light.

"She's gone!" the bird shouted.

"No!" I looked over the side. The Scribe was falling, shrinking into the distance. But then, she stopped. She hit the surface of the wallpaper—a high-resolution image of a tropical beach—and splashed.

It wasn't water she hit; it was *resolution*. The pixels were massive, jagged cubes of cyan and sand. She landed on a palm frond the size of a city block.

"I'm stuck!" her voice drifted up, thin and reedy. "The DPI is too low! I can't get a foothold!"

"We have to stop the scroll!" I yelled up at the Reader. But the Reader was gone. The chair was pushed back. She had left the room.

We were drifting in the digital currents. The cursor slowed, coming to a halt in the center of the sea of files.

"Where are we?" the bird asked.

I looked around. The files were labeled not by number, but by date. Dates stretching back decades.

*1998. 2001. 2014.*

"She didn't just write the letters," I realized, reading the file names. *Lighthouse_Draft_v1. Lighthouse_Finale_Alt. Lighthouse_True_Ending.* "She wrote versions of us."

"Which one is real?" the bird asked.

"They all are," I said. "We are just the one she opened tonight."

I looked at the keyboard, looming like the monoliths of a forgotten god. The keys were dormant. But above us, on the screen, a new notification blinked.

*System Update Available. Install Now?*

"It’s a trap," the bird said. "If she updates, the interface changes. The map changes. We get deleted."

"We can't wait for her to come back," I said. "We have to navigate manually."

I slid down the side of the cursor arrow, using the obsidian shard as a grappling hook to catch in the pixels of the icon. I landed on the top of a folder named *1999*.

"Open it," the bird said.

I couldn't type. I was too small to press the keys. But I remembered the mechanics of the mouse. Double click.

I looked at the bird. "We need to be heavy."

"We are real," the bird said. "We have mass."

"We need more."

I looked at the shard. I looked at the icon. I began to scrape the obsidian against the glass of the screen. It made a terrible, screeching sound, like a violin played with a razor blade.

"What are you doing?"

"Adding friction," I grunted.

I scraped harder. Sparks flew—not fire, but static discharge. The pixels beneath the shard began to warp, glowing with a sickly violet hue.

"Now!" I yelled. "Jump!"

We leaped onto the spot I had scored. The impact, combined with the static charge, registered as a click.

The folder exploded open.

We were thrown back as a torrent of icons burst out—hundreds of letters, notes, discarded fragments. They swirled around us like a blizzard of paper.

One of them hit me in the chest. It wasn't an icon; it was a window. A text document.

I grabbed the edge of the window frame. The bird grabbed my ankle.

We swung there, suspended over the digital void, and read the text on the screen.

*Dear You,*

*The light went out today. I finally let it go dark. I realized that there was no one out there to see it. No ships. No other shores. Just the endless, grey grinding of the waves.*

*I am turning off the lamp. I am going down to the water.*

*Yours,*
*The Keeper.*

"That's the ending," I whispered. "The real ending."

"She wrote it," the bird said. "She wrote the death of the Keeper."

"No," I said, staring at the words. "Look at the date."

*December 31, 1999.*

"She wrote this twenty years ago," I said. "She killed us before we even started."

"Then how are we here?" the bird asked.

"Because she couldn't let go," I said. "She reopened the file. She restored a backup. She resurrected us."

I looked up. The *System Update* prompt was still pulsing. *Install Now?*

"It’s not an update," I realized. "It’s a purge. She’s clearing the clutter. She’s going to delete the old drafts to make space for the new operating system."

"We have to get back to the book," the bird said. "The book is analog. They can't delete paper."

I looked down at the far-away desk. The book was a thin rectangle from this height. Beside it, the ink pool had dried into a black scab.

"The bridge is gone," I said.

Then, I saw something on the edge of the desk. A cable. A thick, black rope snaking down from the laptop to the floor.

"The power cord," I said. "It leads to the outlet. The outlet leads to the wall. The wall has a door."

"We can't climb that," the bird said. "It's plugged in tight."

"We don't climb it," I said. "We cut it."

I raised the obsidian shard.

"If we cut the power," I said, "the screen dies. The update fails. The system reverts to the last save."

"The last save was us," the bird realized.

I swung on the window frame, building momentum.

"One!" I shouted.

"Two!"

"Three!"

I released the bird. It flew straight for the power brick, a black box on the floor.

I slammed the shard into the glass of the screen, right into the glowing *Accept* button of the update prompt.

I didn't just crack the glass. I drove the shard through the pixel layer, piercing the liquid crystal display behind it.

A hiss of pressurized gas escaped.

*CRACK.*

A spiderweb of fractures shot out from the impact point. The colors bled into each other—cyan turning to magenta, white to black. The update prompt froze, then corrupted, dissolving into a glitch storm of jagged artifacts.

The screen flickered once.

Then, darkness.

The fan spun down. The heat vanished.

In the silence, I heard a sound from the physical world. A sound from the desk.

The sound of a book snapping shut.

We were falling, not through pixels, but through the sudden darkness of a room robbed of its primary light source.

I hit the desk hard. The wood was cold.

"Keeper!" a voice hissed.

It was the Scribe. She had made it back from the wallpaper just before the screen died.

"We're back," I gasped.

But as I lay on the vast, cold plain of the desk, I realized something was wrong.

The air smelled different. It didn't smell of old paper or coffee.

It smelled of salt.

I opened my eyes.

Above me, the ceiling was gone. There was no roof. There was no lamp.

There was only the sky.

A sky filled with too many stars.

And beside me, the Scribe was not wearing a linen shift. She was wearing a coat of heavy wool, holding a lantern.

"It worked," she said, her voice古老 and deep. "We stopped the update."

"Where are we?" I asked.

I sat up. I looked at the object I had landed on.

It was not a laptop. It was a rock.

Behind me, rising from the sea, a beam of violet light cut through the darkness, rotating slowly, sweeping over the endless water.

We weren't on the desk.

We were in the book. But the book was no longer on the desk.

"The Reader," I whispered. "She put us in her bag. She’s taking us somewhere."

The Scribe shined her lantern toward the sound of footsteps.

They weren't the footsteps of a giant Reader. They were the crunch of boots on gravel.

A figure approached the lighthouse door. A figure in a yellow raincoat.

"She's not reading anymore," the Scribe said.

The figure stopped at the door. They knocked.

Three sharp raps.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

"Keeper?" a voice called from outside.

I froze. I knew the voice.

It was my voice.

"Are you still writing in there?"

I looked at my hands. They were holding the quill. The ink was wet.

I looked at the Scribe. "Who is knocking?"

"The Author," she said. "She came inside."

I walked to the door. I reached for the handle.

But before I could touch it, the bird landed on my shoulder. It was heavy again. Real.

"Don't open it," the bird said. "If you open it, the story ends."

"Why?"

"Because," the bird said, nodding toward the window.

I looked out.

The sea was gone. The island was gone.

We were sitting on a shelf in a bookstore.

A hand reached for us.

"She's selling us," the bird said
