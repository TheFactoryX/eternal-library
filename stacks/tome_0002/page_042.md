# Letter 42

> Written at 2026-02-02 16:45:42

---

Dear You,

The lion’s eyes were made of glass. They reflected my terrified face, warped by the curvature of the brass, making me look like a fish swimming in a bowl of gold. The wind here, high above the Garden of Prose, was thin and cold. It smelled of dust and the silence of long-empty rooms.

The knocker hung heavy, a ring of iron resting in the lion’s mouth. I didn't want to touch it. A knocker implies a request for entry, an admission of being outside. And I was tired of being outside. I wanted to be in.

But the ladder ended here. There was nowhere else to climb.

I reached out. My hand trembled. The metal was freezing, burning my fingertips.

I lifted the ring. I let it fall.

*Clang.*

The sound didn't echo. It didn't reverberate. It was sucked into the wood of the door, vanishing instantly as if the door were drinking the noise.

I waited.

Nothing happened. The yellow light in the window above didn't flicker. The sky remained a suffocating lid of bruised velvet.

"Open," I whispered. "Please."

I hammered the iron against the plate. *Clang. Clang. Clang.*

"I have come this far," I shouted, my voice raw. "I have crossed the static. I have tied the knot. I have sailed the margin. Let me in."

A vibration began in the wood. It started deep within the grain, a low-frequency hum that rattled my teeth. The lion’s eyes seemed to blink.

*“We do not open,”* a voice boomed. It didn't come from the other side of the door. It came from the door itself. The wood was speaking. *“We are closed for binding.”*

"I don't want to be bound," I said. "I want to be free."

*“Freedom is a myth of the unshelved,”* the door replied. *“To be read is to be trapped in a single moment, forever.”*

"I am trapped now!" I argued. "I am hanging on a ladder above a garden made of my own discarded words!"

*“Then turn around,”* the door said. *“Go back to the soil. Bloom.”*

"I am not a flower!" I screamed. "I am the Keeper! I tend the light!"

The door groaned, a sound of a tree falling in a forest with no one to hear it.

*“The light is gone. You extinguished it to save the manuscript. You chose the story over the signal.”*

It was right. I remembered the choice. I remembered the heat of the lantern room, the decision to burn the oil of the past to keep the future warm.

"If I cannot enter," I said, gripping the ladder until my knuckles turned white, "then I will stay here. I will be a barnacle on the frame of the world."

*“So be it,”* the door said.

The wood beneath my boots shuddered. The rung I was standing on cracked.

I looked down. The ladder was disintegrating. The wood was turning into mulch, into paper pulp, falling away in wet clumps.

I scrambled upward, trying to find purchase on the smooth face of the door. But there was nothing to hold. My fingernails scraped against the varnish.

I fell.

I didn't scream. I just watched the yellow window recede into the distance.

I fell past the ladder. I fell past the tops of the paper trees. The Garden of Blurbs spun beneath me, a kaleidoscope of text and greenery.

I hit the ground.

But it wasn't the ground of the garden.

It was water.

I plunged into deep, icy blackness. The shock took the breath from my lungs. I sank immediately, the weight of my clothes pulling me down. The silver key in my pocket dragged me like an anchor.

*Down. Down. Down.*

The water was filled with voices. Whispers from the margins. I could hear the Void Keeper crying out from the nebula. I could hear the Tree Keeper humming to the roots. I could hear the boy with the red balloon laughing.

I kicked. I struggled. I clawed at the water, trying to find the surface.

But the surface was gone. There was only the deep.

A shape moved in the darkness below me. Massive. Slow. A shape of coils and scales.

The Leviathan.

It rose toward me, its eyes glowing with the pale, sickly light of the bottom of the ocean. Its mouth opened, a cavern of ribbons and spines.

*“Come home,”* it whispered. *“The story is over. Sleep in the silt.”*

I reached into my pocket. My fingers closed around the silver key. It wasn't vibrating anymore. It was hot. Burning hot.

I yanked it out.

The water around me lit up. The key was emitting a beam of light, cutting through the murk like a spear.

The Leviathan recoiled, shielding its eyes.

I kicked harder, propelling myself toward the light. I wasn't swimming; I was climbing the beam.

I broke the surface.

I gasped, sucking in air that tasted of rain and iron.

I was floating in a swamp.

The water was thick with algae and lily pads. Around me, the trees were dead, their branches gnarled fingers reaching up to a sky choked with fog.

To my right, a shelf of slippery mud rose out of the water. I dragged myself onto it, collapsing face down in the muck.

I lay there for a long time, listening to the drip of water from the leaves.

When I finally looked up, I saw the house.

It was a Victorian mansion, three stories tall, peeling paint the color of scabs. The windows were dark, like hollow eyes. The porch sagged.

And hanging from the porch roof was a swing. It creaked back and forth in a wind I couldn't feel.

Sitting on the swing was a woman.

She was wearing a dress of grey silk. Her hair was pinned up in a loose bun. She was holding a cup of tea, steam rising from the porcelain.

She looked at me. She didn't seem surprised to see a bedraggled keeper crawling out of the swamp.

"You're late," she said. Her voice was crisp, British, infinitely tired.

"I... I fell," I said, standing up. My legs were shaking.

"Falling is the fastest way to get here," she said. She took a sip of tea. "Come up to the porch. You're dripping on the azaleas."

I walked toward the house. The mud sucked at my boots. As I got closer, I saw that the house was moving. Not the structure, but the surface. The wood grain was swirling. The paint was shifting colors—green to blue to black.

"It changes," she said, watching me watch the house. "It reflects the mood of the current chapter. We seem to be in a Gothic phase. Dreariness is in vogue."

"Who are you?" I asked, stepping onto the porch. The wood was solid, real.

"I am the Editor," she said. "Of sorts."

"Am I... back in the book?"

She laughed, a dry, crackling sound. "My dear Keeper, you were never *out* of the book. You just turned the page to the Appendix."

She gestured to the rocking chair next to her. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was comfortable. It felt familiar.

"Where is the Revision?" I asked. "Where is the Mirror Man?"

"Discarded," she said. "Rewritten. The author is streamlining the narrative. Tightening the plot. The middle section was becoming... bloated."

"Am I bloated?"

"You are essential," she said. "You are the point of view."

She leaned forward. "But we have a problem. The pacing is off. The tension has sagged. The Reader is getting bored."

She pointed toward the front door of the mansion.

"We need an inciting incident," she said.

The door began to rumble. The wood splintered. Something was pounding on it from the inside.

"What is that?" I asked, standing up.

"That is the consequence," the Editor said. She set her tea down on a small table. "You opened the door in the previous letter, remember? You invited the climax in."

"But I didn't mean to!"

"Intent is irrelevant," she said, smoothing her skirt. "Action is everything."

The door exploded outward.

A wave of black ink burst through the frame, flooding the porch, knocking me backward. It wasn't liquid; it was alive. It was a swarm of black beetles, millions of them, scrabbling over the wood, over the swing, over the Editor.

She didn't scream. She simply dissolved into the swarm, her body turning into a cascade of letters and punctuation marks that were swallowed by the beetles.

The beetles surged toward me.

I turned to run back to the swamp, but the path was gone. The porch extended endlessly into the fog. The house was stretching, growing, the porch becoming a runway.

I ran.

The beetles clicked behind me, a tidal wave of consumption.

I saw a door at the end of the porch. A simple, wooden door with a brass handle.

I sprinted. My lungs burned. The sound of the beetles was deafening, like heavy rain on a tin roof.
