# Letter 133

> Written at 2026-02-07 14:30:33

Dear You,

The ink—that shimmering, plot-armored blue—did not stop at the glass. It burst through with the force of a tidal wave, shattering the Fresnel lens into a rain of diamonds that sliced the air. We were thrown upward, not by the water, but by the sheer displacement of narrative pressure.

I tumbled into the violet sky, my coat snapping like a banner in a gale. Below me, the lighthouse dissolved. It wasn’t falling down; it was melting, the stone turning to slurry, the stairs unraveling into driftwood. The shadow-town was gone, consumed by the hungry mouth of the ocean.

"Keeper!"

I grabbed the Scribe’s wrist. She was dangling above the churning white froth, her legs kicking at nothing. The bird was a frantic blur, diving into the spray and shooting back up, shaking the heavy water from its sodden wings.

"Climb!" I shouted, pointing to the only solid thing left in the collapsing world.

The moon.

It wasn't distant anymore. The violet moon from the painted sky had fallen from its zenith, crashing through the layers of the page to hang just above the waves. It was massive, a cratered sphere of soft chalk, glowing with the pale, cold luminescence of a reading lamp.

We swam through the thick, syrupy air, dragging ourselves toward the lunar surface. I hauled the Scribe up over the rim of a crater. The bird landed with a wet *thwack* beside us.

We collapsed on the powdery ground. It smelled of chalk dust and silence.

"It’s coming," the Scribe whispered.

I looked over the edge of our sanctuary. The blue water was rising. It was climbing the sides of the moon, licking at the chalky cliffs. But it wasn’t just water anymore. Floating in the waves were objects.

A coffee mug. A hairbrush. A potted plant.

"The Room," I said. "The Reader’s room. The boundary is gone. Her reality is leaking into ours."

"That's not just a leak," the bird said, its voice small. It shook itself, spraying a fine mist of plot-armor blue. "Look at the center."

I followed its gaze. In the middle of the churning vortex, where the shadow-town used to be, a shape was forming. It rose from the depths, displacing thousands of gallons of saltwater.

It was a hand. A hand the size of a continent, made of flesh-toned watercolor and blurry strokes.

"She’s reaching in," the Scribe said, horror flattening her voice. "She’s trying to find the place she left off."

"She’s going to crush us," the bird chirped. "If she touches the moon, we dissolve."

"We need a signal," I said. I dug into my pocket. My fingers found the obsidian shard of the charcoal key, the remote button marked *0*, and the receipt from the door. "We need to tell her to stop. We need to make her pause."

"She can't hear us," the Scribe said. "We are text. She only reads us."

"Then we give her something to read," I said.

I looked at the bird. It was shivering, its form flickering as the blue ink began to dry and flake off. The bird was a creature of the page; it couldn't survive in the clash of worlds.

"I need a canvas," I said.

"The moon," the Scribe said. "Use the moon."

I scrambled to the highest point of the crater rim. The giant hand was breaking the surface now, the fingers spreading like a catastrophic starfish. The spray from the wrist was a hurricane.

I took the obsidian shard. It was sharp, harder than diamond.

"I’m sorry," I whispered to the bird.

"For what?" it asked.

I didn't answer. I grabbed the bird. It struggled, its wings flapping against my grip, but I held firm. I pressed the obsidian shard against its side.

"No!" the Scribe screamed. "Don't edit it!"

"It’s the only way!" I yelled.

I slashed. Not to hurt, but to release.

The cut wasn't blood; it was ink. Thick, black, furious ink poured out of the bird, pouring from the wound in reality I had just opened. It didn't spill onto the ground; it floated upward, defying gravity, hanging in the air like a suspended cloud.

"Write!" the bird gasped, its form fading, becoming a hollow outline. "Write the ending!"

I dipped the shard into the cloud of the bird's essence. It drank greedily. The obsidian turned pitch black.

I turned to the face of the moon. I began to carve.

I didn't write words. Words would be too small. I carved an image. A symbol.

I carved a door.

But not a rectangle. I carved a circle. A circle of light.

*PAUSE.*

I carved the word into the very crust of the moon, cutting letters ten feet high into the chalk.

The giant hand hesitated. The fingers, curled to crush the moon, paused inches from the surface.

I felt the pressure change. The wind died. The roaring of the sea faded into a rhythmic, mechanical clicking.

*Click. Click. Click.*

"She’s holding the book," the Scribe realized. "She’s looking at the illustration."

"It worked," I breathed. I slumped against the rim of the crater. "We bought time."

The bird was gone. Only the outline remained, a wireframe of graphite.

I reached out to touch the wireframe. "Thank you," I said.

Suddenly, the outline shuddered. It expanded. It grew solid. The feathers re-formed, not as paper, but as something else. As white, primary feathers.

The bird opened its eyes. They were no longer ink dots. They were real eyes. Beads of obsidian.

"I’m heavy," the bird said. It looked at itself. "I have mass."

"You’re real," I said.

"So are you," the Scribe said. She pointed at me.

I looked at my hands. The charcoal stains were gone. My skin was pale, scarred. The coat was heavy cloth, not a sketch. I felt a heartbeat. A slow, rhythmic drum in my chest.

"The transition," the Scribe said. She looked at herself. Her dress of pages had turned into a simple linen shift. "We jumped the gap. We’re in her world. But we're microscopic."

I looked up. The giant hand was still there, hovering over the moon. But now I saw the texture of the skin. The pores. The fine hairs. The fingerprint ridges swirling like valleys.

And beyond the hand, I saw the face.

The Reader.

She was holding the book open with one hand, magnifying glass in the other. She was peering at the carving on the moon.

"She’s examining the illustration," I whispered. "She thinks it’s part of the story."

"It is," the Scribe said. "We are."

"Where are we?" I asked. "Where is the book?"

"On the desk," the bird said. It flapped its wings, testing the new air. "Look."

I looked over the edge of the moon-crater. Far below, the water had settled. It was no longer a vortex. It was a perfectly flat, reflective surface.

A mirror.

But in the reflection, I saw the room. The bedroom. Massive, impossibly high. The lamp was a sun. The book was a canyon.

"We are in the inkwell," I realized. "We fell into the inkwell she used to annotate the margins."

"We aren't on the paper," the Scribe said. "We are in the medium."

"That means," I said, "we can stain her."

"What?"

"If we can get to her hand," I said, watching the giant fingers hovering over the moon, "we can climb her. We can get out of the well. We can get to the desk."

"And then?" the bird asked. "We are ants on a table."

"Then we find the phone," I said. I remembered the object the Scribe had described. The glowing rectangle the Reader used to talk to the void. "We call for help. Or we call the Author."

"You think the Author will answer?" the Scribe asked.

"I think the Author is waiting," I said. "Why else would there be a 'Contact Us' page at the back of the book?"

I stood up. My legs felt weak, but they held. I looked at the giant thumb hovering inches above our position.

"We have to jump," I said. "We have to hitch a ride."

"Are you insane?" the Scribe asked. "If we miss, we fall back into the ink."

"If we stay here," I said, "we dry out. We become just another illustration in a book she closes forever."

The bird took flight, circling the giant thumb. "I’ll scout," it cawed. "It’s rough terrain. Lots of ridges."

I looked at the Scribe. She was trembling. But she nodded.

"Okay," she said. "On three."

We braced ourselves against the rim of the crater.

"One," I counted.

The thumb began to lower. The Reader was reaching out to turn the page.

"Two."

The shadow of the finger fell over us. It was like night falling instantly.

"Three!"

We leaped.
