*SNAP.*

The sound was deafening, like a mountain range breaking in half. The fingers closed around the tower. I felt the crushing pressure of the grip, the immense, unthinking force of a god tired of his toys.

I was pinned against the glass, my face pressed to the cold pane. Through the distortion, I saw the coffee mug tip. A brown tsunami spilled over the ceramic edge, flooding the Persian rug. I saw a dark shape swept away in the current—the Captain, reduced to a ragdoll of feathers and ink, washed away into the deep shag of the weave.

She was gone. Washed away like a bad draft.

The Lighthouse—and I inside it—was lifted into the air. The sensation was stomach-turning. The g-force pinned me to the glass. We were rising, rushing past the towering stacks of books, past the stalagmites of pencils.

We stopped.

A face loomed into view. The Dreamer.

He was vast. His pores were craters. His eyes were bloodshot lakes, staring *through* the paperweight, looking for the source of the tiny, irritating light that had stabbed his sleep.

He held the Lighthouse up to his ear. He shook it.

I tumbled inside the glass sphere, battered against the lens. He was listening. Checking to see if the rattle had a heartbeat.

"Throw it," I whispered, my voice swallowed by the thickness of the glass. "Please, throw it back."

He didn't throw it.

He reached for a drawer.

*NO.*

The desk surface dropped away. We plunged into the shadows of the desk cavity. It was a tomb of forgotten things. Rubber bands, dried-up pens, paperclips shaped like skeletons.

His hand opened.

The Lighthouse fell. It tumbled through the darkness, bouncing off a hard rubber floor. I was thrown violently against the glass. The impact cracked the pane of the Lamp Room. A fissure opened, letting in the stale, dark air of the drawer.

Silence followed.

I lay on the floor of the sphere, gasping. The light from the switch was still burning, that tiny, defiant word *Remember* glowing in the gloom. It illuminated the cracks in the glass, turning them into rivers of gold.

"He put us away," I said. The realization was a cold weight in my gut. "He put us back in the box."

"The dark," the First Draft’s voice whispered. He was outside, somewhere in the darkness of the drawer. "It smells like neglect here, Elias. The smell of things unfinished."

I crawled to the crack in the glass. "Are you there?"

"I am here," he said. "But the Captain... I felt her go. The story just... let go of her."

"We have to get out," I said. "If we stay here, we rust."

I looked around the Lamp Room. The tiny bulb was fading. The filament—the word *Remember*—was burning out. The Dreamer was forgetting again. Going back to sleep.

I needed a new spark.

I reached into my pocket. My hand brushed the cold ivory of the Colon key. It was useless here. Grammar couldn't break us out of a wooden prison.

I needed something visceral. Something that could make a sound.

I took out the photograph.

The woman with the fish. The memory of the sun.

I looked at the image. In the dying light of the filament, the ink on the photograph seemed to shift. The woman was laughing, but her eyes were terrified. She wasn't holding a fish anymore.

She was holding a rope.

I looked closer at the background of the photo. The pier. The water.

It wasn't a pier.

It was the edge of the drawer. And the water was the shadow under the desk.

"It's a map," I realized. "It’s not a memory. It’s a schematic."

The woman in the photo was pointing. Not at the camera, but *out* of the frame. Her finger pointed toward the hinge of the Lighthouse door.

I scrambled to the tiny door. It was barely an inch high. I pried at the hinge.

It popped.

The door didn't open outward. It fell *in*, revealing a hollow space inside the brickwork of the paperweight.

Inside the hollow space was a roll of paper. A scroll. The original blueprint? No. It was too thick.

I pulled it out. It unspooled across the floor.

It wasn't a blueprint.

It was a list. A long, columned list of crossed-out names.

*The Queen of the Salt. The Clockmaker's Daughter. The Boy Who Lit the Sun.*

And, halfway down: *The Keeper.*

I stared at my name. It was crossed out in red ink.

"I was deleted," I whispered. "Before I even began."

"Not deleted," the Old Man’s voice came from the crack, closer now. "Revised. You are the Second Draft, Elias. The version made after the tragic accident."

"What accident?"

"The one where he stopped dreaming," the Old Man said. "The one where the coffee went cold and he decided to grow up. You are the ghost of the hobby he abandoned."

I looked at the light. The filament had burned down to a single, glowing curve. The letter 'C'.

"We don't die in the drawer," I said, a sudden, fierce resolve hardening my voice. "We die when the ink dries up."

I grabbed the list. I crumpled it in my hand.

"I am not a red line," I shouted. "I am the ink!"

I pressed the crumpled paper against the dying filament. The paper was dry. It caught instantly.

Fire.

A tiny, controlled flame. It ate the list. It ate the crossed-out names.

The flame grew. It tasted like nostalgia. It smelled like burning hair and autumn leaves.

The smoke from the fire began to fill the glass sphere. It was thick, grey, and sweet. It pressed against the crack in the glass.

It pushed out.

The smoke leaked into the drawer, forming a dense, low-hanging fog.

"Do you smell that?" the Old Man whispered. "It smells like a signal fire."

"If we can't break the glass," I yelled over the crackle of the burning paper, "then we make the air too heavy to hold us!"

I fed more of the list into the flame. *The Captain. The Reader.* They all burned.

The glass began to vibrate. The smoke inside was pressurizing the sphere. The cracks were spreading.

*CRACK.*

A sound like a pistol shot. A shard of glass the size of a dinner plate fell away, slicing into the darkness of the drawer.

I stood in the opening. The air of the drawer rushed in, cold and smelling of cedar. I was on the roof of the paperweight, standing on the glass lens.

Below me, in the gloom, I saw the Old Man. He was standing on top of a rubber band, signaling with his cane.

"To the back!" he shouted. "The back of the desk! Where the light gets in!"

I looked.

Behind the wall of the drawer, miles away in the scale of the drawer, was a sliver of blinding white light. It was the gap where the drawer didn't close all the way. The gap to the real world.

"Jump!" I cried.

I leaped from the roof of the Lighthouse. I didn't fall toward the floor of the drawer. I fell into the smoke.

The smoke was buoyant. It caught me. It carried me.

I was flying on the fumes of the burned past.

I soared over the landscape of office supplies. I saw the paperclips like iron forests. I saw the erasers like pink boulders.

I headed for the light.

The Old Man was right beside me, surfing a current of smoke on his cane.

"We are the vapor!" he laughed. "We are the essence!"

We reached the gap. The blinding white rectangle.

We passed through the crack.

And emerged onto the vast, wooden plain of the desktop.

But the world had changed.

The coffee cup was empty. The chair was pushed in.

And the window was open.

The morning sun was streaming in, hitting the desk with a force that felt like solid gold. It illuminated the dust motes dancing in the air.

They weren't dust motes.

They were people. Tiny, translucent people. Dancing. Fading.

"The others," the Old Man whispered. "The ones he finished."

I looked at the window. The screen was down. But there was a tear in the mesh. A hole big enough for a man of smoke and ink to slip through.

Outside, I could see the world. The Real world.

A garden. Green grass. A tree with a tire swing. A cat sleeping on the porch.

It was beautiful. It was solid.

"There," I pointed. "That's the way out."

But the Old Man grabbed my arm. His grip was tenuous, like holding a handful of fog.

"Look at the paper, Elias," he said.

I looked down at the desk surface.

Under the place where the drawer had closed, the Dreamer had left a sheet of paper.

On it, he had written a single sentence. Fresh ink. Still glistening.
