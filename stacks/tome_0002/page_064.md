# Letter 64

> Written at 2026-02-04 02:24:26

---

Dear You,

I wrote the words, but the ink did not dry. It remained wet, glossy, and reflective on the page. It pooled in the loops of the 'y' in "You," creating tiny, dark wells.

I am in the room below the keyhole. The air is colder here than it was in the lantern room, sharper. It smells of ozone and old paper. The silence is not empty; it is heavy, pressurized, like the air before a thunderstorm.

I have been sitting in the wooden chair for what feels like a long time. I have not moved. I am afraid that if I shift my weight, the friction will disturb the delicate equilibrium of this place.

Through the floorboards, I can hear the scratching.

It is a frantic, irregular sound. *Scritch. Scritch. Pause. Long scrape.*

It is the Author.

I can see them through the cracks in the floorboards—just a glimpse of a lamp, a stack of books, a hand tapping a pen against a chin. They are sitting directly below me, in the room with the blue walls and the yellow flower. They are trying to write.

They are trying to write *me*. Or perhaps, they are trying to write *around* me.

I looked at the key in the door. It has fully merged with the wood now. The keyhole is gone. The door is just a solid slab of timber, bound in iron bands that look like the rings of a tree.

"Locked," I whispered.

The scratching below stopped.

"I know you're there," the Author’s voice drifted up, muffled by the floor. It sounded tired. "I can feel you. A heaviness in the prose. A sentence that won't resolve."

I didn't answer. I held my breath.

"I put you in the tower," the voice continued. "I put you in the storm. I gave you the light. Why won't you shine?"

"Because I am not the light," I said, my voice barely audible. "I am the shadow it casts."

The Author sighed. I saw the dust motes dance in the beam of their lamp as they shifted position.

"Fine," the voice said, hardening. "If the protagonist won't act, the environment must react."

Suddenly, the room began to tilt.

It was a subtle sensation at first, like the tilt of a ship on a swell. But then, gravity shifted. The chair slid across the stone floor. The open notebook on my lap—the one I had just written "Dear You" in—slid off and hit the wall with a slap.

I grabbed the arms of the chair to steady myself.

Outside, beyond the stone walls, I heard a roar. Not the roar of the sea, but the roar of the page turning.

"The climax," I realized. "They are rushing the ending."

The room tilted further. The floor became a wall. The ceiling became a floor.

I tumbled out of the chair. I slid across the stone, crashing into the door. The impact jarred my shoulder.

I looked up. The door was now above me, set in the ceiling like a hatch.

"They want a resolution," I said aloud. "They want a dramatic exit."

I scrambled to find purchase. The stones were slippery. The wet ink from my notebook had spilled on the floor, creating slick black patches.

"They want me to fall," I realized. "They want me to fall into the dark."

But I didn't fall. Because the Author had underestimated one thing.

I was no longer falling *through* the story. I was standing *still* while the story moved around me.

I stood up. I braced my feet against the iron bands of the door. I pushed myself upward, defying the tilt.

"You can't rush me!" I shouted down through the floorboards. "I am the Reader's patience! I am the time it takes to turn the page!"

The scratching below became a violent scribbling.

*Scritch-scratch-scratch-scratch.*

The room began to spin. The stone walls blurred into a grey vortex. The wind howled, tearing at my clothes.

I saw objects flying past me—spoons, keys, clocks, letters—all being sucked into the vortex of the climax.

"I am not a prop!" I screamed.

I reached into my pocket. My fingers closed around the only thing that was left.

The silver pen.

I held it up.

The room stopped spinning.

The vortex froze.

The silver pen began to hum. It was vibrating with a frequency that made my teeth ache.

I looked at the nib.

It was glowing red.

"What have you done?" I whispered.

I looked at the door above me. The iron bands were glowing red-hot.

The Author spoke again, but the voice was different now. It was amplified, distorted. It was coming from the pen.

"I didn't write that," the voice said.

"Then who did?" I asked.

"You did," the voice said. "You are writing back."

I looked at the pen in my hand. I wasn't squeezing it. I wasn't forcing it. But ink was flowing from the nib.

It wasn't dripping down. It was floating up.

It was drawing a line in the air. A straight, vertical line, rising toward the door.

"A ladder," I said.

The ink solidified into a black, shimmering rope. It latched onto the iron bands of the door.

I climbed.

I climbed out of the gravity well, up the rope of ink, until I reached the hatch.

I pushed.

The door didn't budge.

"It's locked from the other side," I realized.

I looked down. The spinning room below was gone. The floorboards were gone.

There was only white.

A blinding, suffocating white that was rising up the ink rope like a flood.

The Editor had found the gap.

"Cut it!" a voice boomed from the white. "Sever the connection!"

I scrambled up the rope. The white heat was at my heels.

I reached the top. I pounded on the door.

"Let me in!"

"I can't!" the Author's voice cried from the other side. "The narrative structure is collapsing! The door is a metaphor! You can't open a metaphor!"

"The metaphor is breaking!" I yelled.

The white foam touched my boot.

I felt a sensation of terrible cold. The cold of cancellation. The cold of the book being closed and returned to the shelf.

I looked for a weapon. A key. A spell.

I had nothing.

Except the rope.

I looked at the black ink line tethering me to the door. It was the only real thing in the universe.

"The ink," I said.

I grabbed the rope with both hands. I didn't pull myself up.

I pulled.

I pulled the rope *down*.

I ripped the ink away from the door.

"What are you doing?" the Author screamed.

"I'm unwriting the anchor!" I shouted.

The rope came free. The door above slammed shut, sealing the gap.

The white flood surged up, swallowing the space where the door had been.

I fell.

I tumbled into the white, clutching the black ink rope.

I fell forever.

Then, I hit the ground.

It was soft.

I opened my eyes.

I was lying on a carpet. A beige, wall-to-wall carpet.

I sat up. I was in a hallway.

The walls were painted a soft, calming blue.

There were abstract paintings on the walls.

And on a small table against the wall, there was a vase. And in the vase, there was a single, yellow flower.

I stood up. My legs were weak. I looked at my hands. They were solid. They were trembling.

I was on the other side.

I was in the room with the blue walls.

"Hello?" I called out.

My voice didn't echo. It was absorbed by the drywall and the carpet.

I walked down the hallway.

I passed a bathroom. I glanced in. The mirror was normal. Just a glass rectangle reflecting a tired person in a Keeper’s uniform.

I kept walking.

I reached the living room.

There was a fireplace. A sofa. A television.

And a desk.

The desk was cluttered with papers. Stacks of manuscripts. Reference books.

And a laptop computer. The screen was glowing.

I walked to the desk.

I looked at the screen.

There was a document open.

It was titled: "THE LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER'S LETTERS - FINAL DRAFT."

I read the text on the screen.

It was my life. My letters. All sixty-three of them. Scrolling down, down, down into the digital abyss.

I reached the end of the document.

The cursor was blinking at the bottom of page 64.

*blink... blink... blink...*

I looked at the keyboard.

I reached out. My hand hovered over the keys.

"If I type," I whispered, "I change the story."

"No," a voice said.

I spun around.

The old person with the cane was standing in the doorway. They were holding a cup of tea.

"You don't type," they said. "You read."

They pointed to the screen.

"I am not the Author," they said. "I am the Muse. I am the memory. I am the one who forgot."

"Who are you writing to?" I asked.

"I'm not writing," they said. "I'm remembering. And sometimes, remembering hurts."
