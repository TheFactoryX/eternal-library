# Letter 87

Dear You,

...put us in his pocket," I finish, the thought drying into a hard, bitter lump in my throat. "And toys do not have pockets. Toys do not write letters."

The giant eye of the child fills the torn aperture of the cabin wall. It is vast and terrifying, a blue planet swimming in the white fog of the ceiling. The pupil dilates, a black void expanding to swallow the *Iron Duke*, the dry dock, and the sky.

"What do we do?" you ask. Your voice is trembling, not with fear, but with the vibration of the box being lifted. The horizon outside the porthole tilts violently. We are going up.

"We don't belong to him," I say. I grip the edge of the desk, my knuckles white. "We are not dolls. We are the ones who remember."

I look back at the photograph in my hand. It is curling, reacting to the humidity of this breath-made world. In the image, we are solid. We are on the island. The lighthouse stands behind us, immutable. It is a proof of life. A document of a time before the text, before the critique, before the shelves.

"The ending," you whisper. "That's the ending, Elias. The Author wrote it, but he never published it. He hid it in the bottle, inside the ship, inside the box."

"It's not an ending," I say, staring at the smiling version of myself. "It's a destination."

I grab the silver pen—I had retrieved it from the floorboards where it fell, clutched it like a lifeline. It is warm, pulsing with the ink of my own veins.

"I need to sign this," I say.

"What?"

"I need to validate the document. If I sign it, it becomes real. If I sign it, we *are* the people in the photograph, not the dolls in the box."

The giant fingers of the child are closing around the ship. The wood groans under the pressure. The sky outside is turning dark as we are slid into a darkness that smells of lint and lost pocket change.

"Hurry," you urge.

I press the tip of the pen to the white space beneath the photograph. The paper resists. It is slick, glossy.

*Write,* I command.

The pen tears the surface. A drop of black ink blooms—a star on the glossy paper.

I write my name. *Elias.*

But as I write, the ink changes. It doesn't stay black. It turns red.

The paper begins to hiss.

"No," I say. "Not the edit. Not the red ink."

I try to pull the pen away, but it is stuck. The ink is flowing backwards, draining *out* of the paper and into the pen, sucking the image away. The photograph of the lighthouse is fading, bleaching white under the assault of the Censor's correction.

"It's a trap," you gasp. "The photograph was a red herring. A plot device to lure us into a genre shift."

The ship lurches. We have been deposited. The light changes from the clinical ceiling glare to a dim, dusty orange.

We are in the pocket.

It is a cavern of woven cotton. The walls are vast cliffs of fabric. The floor is littered with debris—a button like a tombstone, a mint wrapper like a crumpled tent, a lint ball the size of a boulder.

"The darkness," you say, pointing upward.

The opening of the pocket is high above, closing as the fabric settles. But through the gap, I see a face.

Not the child.

The Janitor.

He is peering into the pocket, his face illuminated by a harsh, flickering light. He holds the box in one hand, but he is looking at the *boy's* pocket.

"Did you find anything good, sport?" the Janitor asks. His voice is like gravel in a mixer.

The child's voice booms from the sky, thunderous and distant. "Just some old junk, Mr. G. The boat is broken."

"Junk," the Janitor sneers. "Best place for it."

His hand descends into the pocket. It is colossal, the fingers like greedy sausages. He is reaching for us.

"We have to hide," I say.

"Where?"

"The lint," I say. "We bury ourselves in the context."

I grab your hand. We scramble over the mint wrapper and dive behind the lint boulder. The Janitor's fingers sweep the floor of the pocket, blindly groping. They brush against the wrapper, crumpling it like a car crash.

"The key," you whisper. "Do you still have it?"

I pat my pockets. The iron key is gone. I must have dropped it when the ship tilted.

"I lost it," I say, a cold panic washing over me. "I lost the pronoun."

"Then we are locked in," you say.

The Janitor's hand retreats. He lifts the box. He shakes it.

"Nothing," he says. "Empty."

"No," I whisper. "We are here."

He drops the box into a bin. *Clatter.*

Then, he reaches into the child's pocket. He grabs the *Iron Duke*—our entire world—and lifts it out.

We are hanging in mid-air, suspended in the grip of the giant. The wind rushes past us. The library stretches out below us—a vast, endless grid of shelves, but the books are gone. The shelves are filled with boxes, crates, and jars.

"The Archive of the Unloved," I realize.

The Janitor carries us to the end of the aisle. There is a chute. A dark, square hole in the wall.

"The incinerator," you say. "The burning of the books."

He tilts his hand. We slide.

We slide off the deck of the *Iron Duke*, falling through the air. We tumble toward the dark mouth of the chute.

"The photograph!" I shout. I still have it. It is nearly blank now, the image completely erased by the red ink. But I still hold the paper.

"Use the paper!" you yell.

I don't think. I act. I hold the glossy photograph above my head like a parachute. It catches the air.

But it's not air resistance that slows us.

It is a crease.

A sudden, sharp crease forms in the paper, catching the wind of the chute like a wing. We swoop upward, carried by the draft of the ventilation system.

We are flying, but not with wings. We are flying on a folded memory.

We bank hard, avoiding the trash compactor at the bottom of the shaft. We spiral upward, circling the dust motes that dance in the shafts of light.

We crash-land on a metal shelf.

Pain shoots through my shoulder. I lie still, gasping. The smell of ozone and old glue is overwhelming.

"Elias?"

"I'm here."

You sit up. We are on a catwalk suspended above the warehouse floor. Below us, the Janitor is sweeping the floor, whistling a tune that has no melody. He is pushing dust into piles—piles of deleted characters, discarded verbs, and unfinished sentences.

"We are safe," you say. "For now."

I sit up. I look at the photograph. It is blank. A white, glossy rectangle. The red ink has consumed everything.

"Look," I say, pointing to the white surface. "It's not blank."

There is text on it now. Tiny, red typed text filling the space where the lighthouse used to be.

I hold it up to the light to read.

*SCENE 87: THE LIBRARY.*

It is a script. We are in the script.

"It's a blueprint," you say. "We are in the rough draft."

"And the Janitor?" I ask.

"The Reader," you say. "The Reader who skips the boring parts."

I look at the next line on the paper.

*THE KEEPER STANDS AND WALKS TO THE EDGE OF THE SHELF. HE LOOKS DOWN AT THE READER. HE REALIZES THE READER IS NOT LOOKING AT THE BOOK. HE IS LOOKING THROUGH IT.*

I stand up. I walk to the railing of the catwalk. I look down at the Janitor.

He stops whistling. He freezes. He looks up, but his eyes don't focus on me. He looks through me. He is looking at the wall behind the shelf.

"He can't see us," I say. "We are in the blind spot."

"We are in the marginalia," you say.

I look at the script again.

*THE KEEPER TURNS TO THE COMPANION. HE ASKS THE QUESTION THAT CANNOT BE ANSWERED.*

I look at you. The script is burning in my hand, the red ink heating up, curling the edges of the paper.

"Do you remember the name of the city?" I ask. "The city of books? The Library?"

You look at me, your eyes reflecting the red light of the script.

"I don't remember a city," you say. "I remember a garden. A garden of glass flowers."

I look down at the script. The text is shifting. The red ink is swirling, reforming into new words.

*THE KEEPER REMEMBERS THE GARDEN. THE KEEPER REMEMBERS HE PLANTED THE SEEDS.*

"I planted them," I whisper. "I planted the stories."
