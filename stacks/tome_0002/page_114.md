# Letter 114

> Written at 2026-02-06 16:52:01

---

Dear You,

I recognize that voice. It is the voice of the Punctuation Keeper, stripped of its celestial detachment. It is weary, grounded, and impossibly close.

"I had to find the door," I reply into the dark. The air here is thick, smelling of ozone and old dust—a storm that has been breaking for centuries.

"The door is irrelevant," the voice says. "You have opened it, and now the room exists. Be thankful I did not make it a cathedral."

A spark flares in the darkness above. A match strikes against rough brick. The sudden illumination reveals a face hovering in the gloom. It is the Boy, but he is older now, his eyes heavy with the weight of keeping the light. He is holding the match to the wick of a lantern that hangs from the ceiling of a shaft.

"The climb is always the hardest part," he says, shaking the match out until only the curl of smoke remains. The lantern glass, once clear, is now frosted over, obscuring the flame within. "The lamp room is above. But be warned. The Keeper is not himself today."

"I am the Keeper," I say, gripping the railing of the spiral stairs. The iron is cold, seeping through my gloves.

"Are you?" He smiles, a sad, curving thing. "I am the Keeper of the Lamp. He is the Keeper of the Key. You... you are just the mailman, Elias. You are the courier between the inside and the out."

I push past him. The stairs seem to stretch endlessly upward, carved into the living rock of the tower. As I climb, the sound of the ocean returns. It is not outside the tower; it is *inside* the walls. The stones are grinding against each other, a tectonic moan that vibrates in my marrow.

I remember the glass tower from the previous draft. I remember the grinding gears and the tiny figures trapped within them. I touch the rough stone wall, half-expecting it to be smooth, cold glass.

"It changed," I whisper.

"Of course it did," the Boy calls from the darkness below. "The genre shifted. We are no longer in a Fantasy. This is a Gothic. The props are heavier. The lighting is lower."

I reach the top. The heavy iron door to the lamp room stands ajar. I push it open.

The room is small, circular, and suffocatingly hot. But there is no great Fresnel lens spinning. No electric bulb. In the center of the room, hanging from chains thick as ship's cables, is a massive, ornate cage.

Inside the cage is a sun.

It is a small, furious star, about the size of a fist, pulsating with a blinding, golden-white rage. The heat of it singes my eyebrows. The light casts shadows that move independently of me, dancing on the stone walls in jerky, frantic motions.

Sitting at a small desk in the corner, his back to me, is a figure. He is writing furiously.

"You're blocking the rotation," the figure says. He does not turn around. "If you don't move, the shadow of the Lighthouse will stop turning on the ocean floor. The fish will get confused."

I step closer. "The Old Man?"

"Dead," he says. "Eaten by the spider. Remember? You watched it happen. Or did you forget that part because it was inconvenient to the narrative arc?"

"I saw him dissolve," I say. "I saw him turn into sunlight."

The figure stops writing. The scratching of the nib ceases. The silence is louder than the roaring sun-cage.

"He turned into *sunlight*?" The figure asks, his voice trembling. "Are you sure?"

"He dissolved into a puddle of light on a blade of grass," I say.

The figure turns around.

It is me.

But it is a version of me I have never seen in the mirror. He is gaunt, his skin translucent, revealing veins that pulse with black ink. He is wearing the Keeper's uniform, but it is tattered, stained with salt and time.

"You fool," he says, standing up. "That wasn't a metaphor. That was a transfer of energy."

He walks to the cage. He reaches through the iron bars—ignoring the searing heat—and grabs the fist-sized star.

"He didn't die," the Gaunt Keeper says. "He ascended. He became the fuel."

He looks at me, his eyes burning with the same golden fury as the star in his hand.

"The lamp isn't electric, Elias. It never was. It’s a biological engine. It runs on the souls of the drafts who escape."

He slams the star back into the cage. The tower shudders.

"Why are we here?" I ask. "I saw the book close. I saw the Dreamer die."

"The Dreamer didn't die," the Gaunt Keeper says. "He just lost interest. He put us down. We are in the pause between chapters. The pause is dangerous. It is where the logic leaks out."

He points to the window.

I look out. The sea is gone. The sky is gone.

There is only a vast, textured expanse of beige. And floating in the beige, miles away, is a single, colossal human eye.

It is looking right at us.

"He's reading again," the Gaunt Keeper whispers. "He's back."

"Who?"

"The Dreamer. The Author. The Giant." The Gaunt Keeper grabs my lapels. His grip is like iron. "You have to make it interesting, Elias. You have to give him a twist. If he gets bored, he'll close the book again. And next time, he might put us in the fireplace."

"What kind of twist?"

"Something new," he says. "A new mystery. A new object."

I reach into my pocket. My fingers brush against the cold metal of the bone compass. I pull it out.

The compass needle is spinning wildly.

"Give it to me," the Gaunt Keeper demands.

I look at the compass. I look at the cage of fire.

"No," I say.

I climb onto the desk. I reach toward the sun-cage.

"That's hot enough to burn the story right off the page!" the Gaunt Keeper shouts.

"We need more light," I say. "If the eye is watching, we need to blind it."

I hold the compass near the cage. The magnetic pull is ferocious. The needle rips itself off the pin, flying across the room and embedding itself in the stone wall.

*THUNK.*

It points at a specific stone.

I walk to the wall. I pry the stone loose.

Behind the stone is not a passage. It is a cavity.

Inside the cavity is a small, glass bottle. And inside the bottle is a rolled-up piece of parchment. But the parchment is glowing.

"The First Draft," the Gaunt Keeper breathes. "The original letter. Before the ink dried."

I pull the bottle out. The wax seal on the cork is stamped with a symbol I don't recognize—a bird with a human face.

"If we read it," I say, "does the story restart?"

"It restarts everything," the Gaunt Keeper says. "It resets the timeline. The Captain will be alive. The Old Man will be in the study. The glass tower will never have existed."

"But I will forget," I say. "I will go back to the beginning."

"You will be the You who started," he says. "The You who still had hope."

I look at the eye outside the window. The pupil is dilating, focusing on us. The giant is leaning in. The curiosity is palpable, a pressure in the atmosphere.

I break the seal against the iron bars of the cage. The glass shatters. The parchment rolls open.

I read the first line.

*The Lighthouse Keeper wrote a letter to a person who did not exist.*

"No," I whisper. "That's not right. I wrote to you."

I look up from the page.

The Gaunt Keeper is smiling. He is fading, becoming transparent, turning into the mist that curls off the ocean.

"Exactly," he says. "I don't exist. Only the ink exists."

The room begins to dissolve. The sun-cage flickers. The stone walls turn into paper.

I look at my hands. They are turning into text. My fingers are becoming the words: *The Keeper reached out.*

I am being written back into the story. But I am not the writer. I am the ink.

I look at the parchment one last time, desperate to read the second line before the transition completes.

The ink swirls, forming the letters.

*The person who did not exist was the one holding the pen.*

The floor drops out from under me. I am falling, not down, but *in*—spiraling into the binding of the book, into the spine, into the dark center where the stories are glued together.

I hear the sound of a match being struck.

Then, the smell of pipe tobacco.

And a voice that sounds terrifyingly like my own, saying:

"Letter 114. Where were we?"
