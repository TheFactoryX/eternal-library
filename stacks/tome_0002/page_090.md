Dear You,

...I need a memory that has weight. A memory that can push back against the white void," I scream, the black paint dripping from the bone brush like tar.

The charcoal smoke—the hungry, unfinished protagonist of the sketch—is coiling around my waist now, tight and cold. It smells of graphite and despair. It whispers in a thousand voices, all of them mine. "Let me out. Let me wear the coat. Let me be the Keeper."

"Focus, Elias!" You shout. You are tearing strips of fabric from your yellow coat, trying to bind the smoky tendrils, but they turn the cloth to ash on contact. "The paint! What is on the brush?"

I look at the bristles. The label on the pot said *The Void*, but what is the Void if not the absence of memory?

I close my eyes. The gallery is dissolving. The floor is turning to paper. The walls are bleaching.

I dig into the past. Not the recent past of the crate or the warehouse, but the deep past. The time before the Lighthouse.

I remember the sound of a train.
I remember the smell of wet wool.
I remember the weight of a suitcase in my hand.

I remember standing on a platform. The steam engine was hissing. The world was grey and industrial and solid. I was leaving. I had a ticket. I had a destination.

And I had a locket.

I open my eyes.

"The Locket," I gasp. "From Letter 4. The one I threw into the sea."

"Use it!" You yell, kicking a tendril away.

I don't have the locket. I lost it years ago—in the story, in the timeline. But I remember the *click* of it. The cold metal against my chest. The photograph inside.

I channel that sensation into my arm. I force it down the handle of the brush.

I slam the brush against the white wall.

*SCREECH.*

It isn't the sound of a brush. It is the sound of metal dragging on stone.

The black paint explodes outward. It doesn't spread like liquid; it expands like rust. It corrodes the white canvas, eating holes in the fabric of reality.

Through the holes, I see the sea.

Not the glass sea of the warehouse. The *real* sea. Dark, churning, indifferent water.

"More!" I command.

I paint a circle. A rough, ragged circle of rust and iron.
I paint a handle.
I paint the bolts.

The charcoal smoke screams. It recoils from the rust, hissing like acid thrown on a reptile.

"The Iron hates the sketch," I realize. "The Industrial rejects the Artistic."

I kick the center of the painted circle.

*CRASH.*

The wall breaks outward. The smell of salt spray hits us like a physical blow. The roar of the ocean drowns out the gallery.

I grab your hand. "Jump!"

We dive through the hole in the world.

We fall.

We hit the water hard. It is freezing. It is real. It tastes of kelp and depth.

We surface, sputtering. The waves are huge, grey mountains rising and falling around us.

"Swim!" I shout. "To the shore!"

There is a dark shape looming out of the spray. Rock. The silhouette of the island.

We struggle against the rip current. The water is heavy, pulling at our legs. I feel the weight of the pears we ate—they are anchors in my stomach, pulling me down, grounding me in this physical reality.

We claw our way up the rocky shelf. We collapse on the black stones, gasping.

The air is cold. The sky is a bruised purple, twilight in the middle of the day.

I sit up. I look around.

We are on the island.

But it is... silent.

The Lighthouse is there. It stands on the cliff, tall and white. But the light is out.

The glass lens at the top is dark. Dead.

"Why is it dark?" You ask, shivering. You wrap your arms around yourself. The yellow coat is tattered, hanging off you in rags.

"Because the Author stopped writing," I say. "He closed the book. We are in the interval."

I stand up. My legs are shaking. I look at the path leading up to the Lighthouse door. It is overgrown with strange, tangled vines—vines that look like crumpled papers.

"We have to go up," I say. "We have to relight it."

We walk up the path. The ground is littered with debris. Debris from the warehouse. A button. A rusted gear. A single page of script blowing in the wind.

I pick up the page. It is wet, the ink running.

*CHAPTER 12: THE END.*

"The title," I whisper. "He wrote the end."

I crumple the page and throw it into the sea.

We reach the heavy iron door of the Lighthouse. It is exactly as I remember it. The paint is peeling in long, vertical strips. The knocker is a rusted ring.

I reach for the knocker.

But then I stop.

My hand passes through the ring.

"What?" I pull my hand back. I try again. I focus. I try to grab the cold iron.

My fingers shimmer. They become translucent for a moment, showing the rock behind them.

"Elias," You say. Your voice is scared. "Look at me."

I look at You.

You are fading. The edges of your yellow coat are blurring into the fog. You look like a watercolor painting left out in the rain.

"He's forgetting us," You say. "The ink is drying. We are fading into the paper."

"We are real!" I shout. "We ate the pears! We swam the ocean! We have mass!"

"We have plot," You correct. "But he stopped reading. The energy is draining away."

I look at the Lighthouse door. It is becoming more solid, more real than I am. The wood grain is sharpening. The rust is deepening. The world is resolving, but I am dissolving.

"If I fade," I say, "who tends the light?"

"No one," You say. "The darkness returns."

"No."

I look around frantically. I need an anchor. Something to bind me to this reality.

I look down at my feet. Lying in the mud, half-buried in the wrack of seaweed...

Is the bucket.

The yellow bucket with the red spade. From Letter 1. The first object I ever found.

I fall to my knees. I grab the plastic handle.

It is solid. It is stubbornly, aggressively real. It feels like the only thing left in the universe.

"I remember this," I say. "I remember building the sandcastle."

"You were a child then," You say. You are barely a ghost now, a voice in the mist.

"I am a child now," I say.

I clutch the bucket. The plastic cuts into my palm. It hurts. The pain is a lifeline. The pain is proof.

I drag the bucket toward the door. I am becoming more solid. The outline of my hands is sharpening. The color is returning to my coat—the grey wool of the coat I lost in the warehouse is manifesting on my shoulders.

"I am wearing it again," I say. "The Coat."

"You found your continuity," You say. Your voice is stronger. You are reforming. "You used the motif."

I stand up. I am heavy. I am weighted with memory and object.

I raise the bucket. I don't use the knocker. I smash the bucket against the door.

*CLANG.*

The sound echoes across the silent sea.

*CLANG.*

The door shudders.

*CLANG.*

The lock breaks. The door swings open.

The interior of the Lighthouse is not the spiral staircase I remember.

It is a shaft.

A vertical shaft, leading straight up into the darkness. No stairs. No landings. Just a smooth, white wall, stretching up forever.

And hanging in the center of the shaft...

Is a rope.

It dangles down from the infinite darkness above. It stops just in front of my face.

It is made of words.

The fibers of the rope are tiny, tightly packed letters. *A... S... C... E... N... D...*

"The first and last letters," I realize. "The Alpha and the Omega."

I grab the rope.

"What about me?" You ask.

I look at the rope. It is thin. It looks fragile.

"Climb on my back," I say. "We climb together."

You wrap your arms around my neck. Your weight is familiar. It feels like the weight of a story well-told.

I begin to climb.

Hand over hand. I pull myself up the shaft.

The Lighthouse is a chimney, and we are the smoke.

We climb for hours. The entrance is a tiny square of light far below. The sea is gone.

We are suspended in the white void.

My arms burn. The rope bites into my hands. The letters *A... S... C...* dig into my skin, leaving impressions of text on my palms.

"How high is it?" You whisper.

"To the top," I grunt. "To the lamp room."
