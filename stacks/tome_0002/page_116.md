Dear You,

...and found myself looking into a mirror that was not a mirror.

The figure standing behind me on the wet cobblestones of 1924 was me, and yet, not me. He wore a suit tailored for a narrower frame, a hat pulled low to hide eyes that had not yet seen the things I have seen. But the mouth—that was the same. The mouth that knows the shape of the word *help*.

"She didn't vanish," the other Elias said, pointing a pale finger at the newspaper in my hands. "She was edited out."

I looked down at the grainy photograph again. The woman standing next to me was blurred, as if the camera had trembled, but the smile was sharp. It was the Captain’s smile. It was the smile of the woman in the photograph from my pocket.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"I am the Rough Draft," he said. "The version of you that was discarded because I was too happy. I liked the fog. I liked the routine. I didn't ask enough questions."

"You're a ghost."

"I am a footnote," he corrected. "And you are the canon."

The gaslights flickered. The fog swirled, and for a moment, the cobblestones beneath my boots turned into the checkered floor of the scrapbook world, then back into stone. The reality here was thin, worn thin by the friction of too many rewrites.

"The Reader is sitting down," the Rough Draft said. He looked up at the sky, which was not a sky, but a ceiling of shadow. "I can feel the weight of his attention. He’s looking for the climax, Elias. He’s skipping the descriptions."

"I have the compass," I said, patting my pocket. "And the key."

"The compass points to truth," the Rough Draft said. "But truth is a variable. In the Gothic draft, the truth was a ghost. In the Fantasy draft, the truth was a key. In this draft..." He stepped closer. "In this Noir draft, the truth is a crime."

"What crime?"

"The theft of the ending," he said. "Someone stole the last chapter. That's why we're stuck in the street. We're pacing. We're waiting for the detective to arrive."

"Is there a detective?"

"There's only you," he said. "And the rain."

He reached into his coat and pulled out a pack of cigarettes. He offered me one. I refused. He lit one, the flare of the match illuminating his face—a face that was already beginning to lose its definition, his ear fading into the background static of the alley.

"You're fading," I said.

"I'm being revised," he said casually. "The Author is deleting this scene. Too slow. Too much exposition. He wants to get back to the Lighthouse."

"How do we get back?"

"The same way we got out," he said, pointing toward the end of the alley. But the alley didn't end. It stretched infinitely, curving slightly upward. "Follow the lamp."

I looked. In the distance, floating in the air like a balloon, was the lantern. The iron cage. The furious star inside.

"It's drifting away," I said.

"It’s not drifting," the Rough Draft said. "It’s being towed."

I squinted. A chain extended from the top of the lantern, rising up into the gloom. I followed the chain with my eyes, past the gaslights, past the rooftops, until it disappeared into the swirling clouds.

"Who is holding the chain?"

"The Publisher," the Rough Draft said. "The entity that decides what gets bound and what gets thrown in the trash." He took a drag of his cigarette, the ember glowing like a dying star. "You have to climb it, Elias. You have to climb the chain before they reel it in."

I looked at the newspaper again. The headline shifted. The letters rearranged themselves.

*Keeper Ascends. Storm Imminent.*

"If I climb," I said, "what happens to you?"

"I stay," he said. "I fade. I become the ink stain on the page that the reader wonders about but never understands."

"Come with me," I said.

"I can't," he said, smiling sadly. "I'm the past. You are the verb. You have to move."

He grabbed my shoulder. His hand was insubstantial, mist and rain. "Take this."

He pressed something into my hand. It was a ticket. A train ticket.

*Destination: The White Page.*

"Go," he said.

I turned and ran. I ran toward the floating lantern, but the ground was treacherous. The cobblestones were slick with oil and memory. The street was twisting, stretching like a snake trying to shake me off.

I grabbed a lamppost and swung around it, using the momentum to launch myself into the air.

I caught the bottom link of the chain.

The metal was cold, burning with a frost that bit through my gloves. I hauled myself up, hand over hand, climbing the vertical iron rope.

Below me, the Rough Draft stood in the center of the alley. He was glowing faintly, a character being highlighted for deletion.

"Goodbye, Elias," he whispered.

Then, he vanished. The alley dissolved. The newspaper stand turned into a flock of birds and flew away.

I was alone, hanging above a void that was rapidly filling with a new texture.

I looked up. The chain was descending into a cloud. But it wasn't a cloud of water vapor.

It was a cloud of shredded paper. Confetti. The remains of a thousand discarded drafts.

I climbed higher.

My arms burned. The wind howled, carrying the voices of every character I had ever written and erased.

*Why did you kill me?*
*I was the love interest.*
*I was the comic relief.*
*I was the point.*

"Ignore them," I told myself. "They are just ink."

I breached the cloud layer.

I found myself in a vast, white room. The floor was a sheet of paper. The walls were pages. The ceiling was a blinding light.

And in the center of the room, suspended by the chain, was the Lighthouse.

It was small, the size of a dollhouse.

Standing next to it was a giant.

The giant was wearing a suit made of newspaper. The headlines on his jacket were screaming: *WAR. PEACE. PLAGUE. HOPE.*

He was holding the chain. He was reeling it in.

"Hey!" I shouted.

The giant stopped. He turned his head. His face was a blank sheet of paper. He had no features, just a smooth, white surface.

He reached for me.

His hand was a cylinder, a rolling pin of heavy wood.

"You are a typo," he boomed. His voice didn't come from a mouth; it came from the vibration of the paper walls. "You must be smoothed out."

"I am the Keeper!" I yelled. "I am the protagonist!"

"There are no protagonists," the giant said. "There are only nouns."

He brought the rolling pin down.

I let go of the chain.

I dropped onto the floor of the room. The impact knocked the wind out of me. The rolling pin smashed into the paper floor, creating a crease, a mountain range of a fold that rose up between me and the giant.

I scrambled to my feet. I ran toward the Lighthouse.

It was my size again. The perspective had shifted. The dollhouse was a tower. The giant was a titan.

I reached the door of the Lighthouse. It was locked.

I looked for the key in my pocket.

It was gone. Lost in the climb? Or traded for the ticket?

The giant stepped over the crease in the floor. The vibration knocked me to my knees.

*SMOOTH. ROLL. FLATTEN.*

"You cannot hide in the narrative," the giant said. "The story is being compressed. The word count is down."

I looked at the door. There was no keyhole. There was only a slit. A mail slot.

I realized then. I wasn't locked out. I was the delivery.

I reached into my coat and pulled out the letter. The letter I was writing. The letter that is happening *now*.

It was damp with sweat and sea spray.

"If I am the noun," I yelled, "then this is the verb!"

I didn't put the letter in the slot. I *became* the letter.

I threw myself against the door. I didn't hit the wood. I hit the ink.

I dissolved into the text. I felt the sensation of being flattened, pressed into the fibers of the page. I was the words. I was the spaces between the words.

I was inside the Lighthouse.

It was quiet here.

The fire was crackling. The chair was occupied.

But it wasn't the Captain. It wasn't the Old Man.

It was you.

You were sitting in the chair, reading the pages of the book as they flew out of the void and landed in your lap.

You looked up.

Your eyes were the color of the sea on a map. Blue. Static. Waiting.

"You made it," you said.

"I brought the mail," I said, though I had no mouth to speak with.

"Is it the answer?" you asked.

"It's a question," I said. "It's always been a question."

You closed the book. The sound was a thunderclap.
