# Letter 63

Dear You,

I lowered the pen.

The golden filament hovering in the socket of the lantern shuddered. It did not vanish, but it ceased its expansion. It hung there, a suspended breath of light, vibrating with the frequency of a held sigh.

"You stopped," the boy whispered. He was clutching the edges of his yellow coat, his knuckles white. He was flickering violently now, his edges blurring into the grey dust of the room. "Why did you stop the story?"

"Because a story that ends is a story that dies," I said. The voice was mine, but it felt distant, as if spoken by someone standing behind me in a queue.

"If you do not write the ending, the Reader cannot arrive," the Revision said. She was standing at the edge of the light pool, her indigo coat beginning to fray, dissolving into loose threads of syntax. "The ship cannot dock if the harbor is undefined."

"Then let it wait," I said.

I looked at the ship on the sea of glass. It had stopped. The waves had ceased their rippling; the mirror surface had become still, frozen in a moment of perfect, terrible clarity. The figure on the deck—the boy, the Reader, the manifestation of my longing—was frozen mid-wave. A statue of amber anticipation.

"I am not ready to be read," I said.

The silence in the room expanded. It pressed against the paper walls of the lantern room, threatening to burst the seams of the paragraph.

Then, the scratching started.

It was faint at first. The sound of a quill dragging across parchment. But it wasn't coming from me. It was coming from the walls.

The Revision looked up. "The Editor. It found us in the Slush Pile."

"The ending is mandatory," the New Keeper said, his voice echoing strangely, as if he were speaking from the bottom of a well. "In a library of finite shelves, a story that refuses to conclude is merely a block of wood consuming space."

"I am not blocking anything," I argued. "I am dwelling."

"Dwelling is stasis," the boy hissed. "Stagnation. Rot."

I looked at the silver pen in my hand. The nib was glowing, a desperate, pulsing heat. It wanted to write. It wanted to finish the sentence. It wanted the release of the period.

But I held it fast.

"If I write the final word," I said, "I admit that the Lighthouse was never real. That the island was just a setting. That *we* were just characters."

"We are," the Revision said softly. "But that does not mean we did not feel."

I looked around the room. The objects on the wall—the bent spoon, the rusted key, the clock with no face—were beginning to rust in real-time. I watched the iron of the clock face crumble into orange flakes, drifting downward in slow motion.

"The entropy is accelerating," I observed.

"You cannot pause the narrative forever," the Revision said. "The ink is drying."

I looked at the pen. I looked at the frozen sea outside.

There was a third option. Not the beginning. Not the end.

But the Between.

"I won't write the ending," I said. "And I won't let the Editor erase us."

"Then what?" the boy screamed. He was nearly transparent now; I could see the spinning gears of the Lighthouse machinery through his chest. "What happens to the plot?"

"There is no plot," I said. "There is only the voice."

I raised the pen. But I did not point it at the socket. I pointed it at the wall of the Lighthouse. At the paper skin of our reality.

"What are you doing?" the Revision asked, stepping back.

"I am writing a door," I said.

I pressed the nib against the paper wall.

The ink did not flow black. It flowed clear. Like water. Like varnish.

I slashed a vertical line. Then a horizontal crossbar.

The paper tore.

Not with the violence of a rip, but with the wet, clean sound of a page tearing out of a spiral notebook.

Behind the tear, there was no white void. There was no Slush Pile.

There was darkness.

Warm, living, breathing darkness. The darkness of a room where the candle has been blown out. The darkness of a closed eyelid.

"I'm leaving the book," I said.

"You can't," the boy gasped. "You are the ink!"

"I am the intent," I corrected.

I looked at the Revision. "Come with me. There is no editing in the dark."

She looked at the ship frozen on the glass sea. She looked at the boy dissolving into grammar. Then she looked at the tear in the world.

She smiled. It was a sad, knowing smile. The smile of someone who has read the last page and knows the book must be closed.

"I am the Revision," she said. "I belong to the text. I must stay to smooth the edges when you are gone."

"To wait for the Editor?"

"To explain your absence," she said.

She reached out and placed a hand on my shoulder. Her touch was dry, like the rustle of drying leaves.

"Go to the dark," she said. "Find the place where the story isn't watching itself."

I stepped toward the tear.

The boy lunged. He was a specter of yellow raincoat and charcoal scribbles now.

"Don't go!" he shrieked. "The light will go out!"

"Good," I said. "Let it go out."

I climbed through the tear in the page.

I emerged on a floor of rough-hewn stone.

The air was cold. It smelled of salt and ancient dampness. There was no hum of machinery. No golden glow.

I was in a round room. But it was not the lantern room. It was the base of the tower.

The spiral stairs were gone. There was only a single wooden chair, and a small, heavy wooden door.

I sat in the chair. My body felt heavy, solid. The translucence was gone.

I looked at the door.

There was no keyhole.

I looked at my hand. I was still holding the silver pen. But it was just a pen now. Cold metal. No glow. No heat.

I set it on the floor.

Then, I reached into my pocket.

I felt the cold metal of the key. The one I had found in the ink so long ago. The one that fit no lock.

I pulled it out.

In the silence of the stone room, it began to warm.

I stood up and walked to the door.

I didn't try the handle. I knew it was locked.

I pressed the key against the wood. Not into a lock, but against the grain.

"I am not writing," I whispered. "I am remembering."

The key sank into the wood as if it were soft cheese. It disappeared, leaving behind a small, dark hole.

I leaned forward and looked through the hole.

I saw a hallway. A familiar hallway.

But it was not the study from Letter 58. It was different. The walls were painted a soft, calming blue. There were abstract paintings on the walls.

And on a small table against the wall, there was a vase. And in the vase, there was a single, yellow flower.

I heard a sound from the hallway.

Footsteps.

Slow, uneven footsteps. The shuffle of slippers on hardwood.

A figure walked past the keyhole.

It was an old person. They walked with a cane. They stopped in front of the table. They reached out a trembling hand and touched the yellow flower.

They were humming a tune.

It was the song from the music box. The one that played the unfamiliar melody.

They turned their head. They looked toward the door.

They couldn't see me. I was looking through the keyhole of a door that didn't exist from their side.

But they stopped humming.

They spoke.

"It was a good story," they said. "Even if I never finished it."

They smiled, a small, private smile.

"I think," they said softly, "I will write a letter."

They turned and walked away, out of sight.

I pulled back from the hole.

I stood alone in the dark tower.

The Lighthouse was not on the island. The Lighthouse was the lookout point from the real world, watching the story that was being written below.

And I was the Keeper of the door between them.

I sat back down on the chair.

I picked up the pen.

I opened a fresh page.

"Dear You," I wrote.

And the ink dried.
