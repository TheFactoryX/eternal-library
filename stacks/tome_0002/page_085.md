# Letter 85

> Written at 2026-02-05 04:51:07

---

Dear You,

...into a space that defies the laws of perspective, even the warped laws of this narrative.

I expected to see the interior of a moving van. I expected to see the dust motes of a garage or the sterile fluorescent hum of a bookstore warehouse. But as I pressed my face against the rough splinter of the gap, the darkness was ripped away by a blinding, blurring velocity.

We were not in a vehicle. We were flying.

Or rather, the world was falling away from us.

I saw a landscape of corrugated cardboard blur past, vast mountains of brown paper rising and falling like waves. But the perspective was wrong. The flutes of the cardboard—the wavy inner layer—were as tall as redwoods. We were hurtling through the microscopic canyons of a packing material.

"Hold on!" I shouted, though there was nothing to hold onto but the vibrating floor.

The crate slammed.

*CRUNCH.*

The sound was deafening, a thunderclap of fiber compression. We had stopped. The sudden halt threw me against the wall of books. The silver pen flew from my pocket, skittering across the floorboards and vanishing through a crack.

"No!" I lunged for it, but it was gone.

"We have to go," You said. You were already at the tiny blue door, your hand on the brass lion knocker.

"Go where?" I asked, staring at the space where the pen had fallen. "It was my link. My tether to the text."

"It was a tool," You said. "Now, we are the ink."

You pushed the tiny door open. But this time, the warm amber light of the Archives was gone. This time, there was only a glare. A harsh, unyielding white.

We stepped through.

The smell hit me first. Not of ozone or old paper. Of bleach. Of sterility. Of lemon disinfectant.

I opened my eyes.

We were standing in a hallway. The floor was checkered linoleum, white and black. The walls were painted a stark, clinical white. Overhead, long tubes of fluorescent light hummed with the sound of angry hornets.

It was a school. Or an office. A place of order. A place of lines.

"The Real World," I whispered. The name felt heavy on my tongue, a forbidden word.

"Not the world," You said, looking around with wary eyes. "The waiting room."

We began to walk. Our footsteps squeaked on the polished floor. To our left, there were doors. Hundreds of them, all identical. Some had frosted glass windows. Some were solid wood. Some had numbers, some had names.

We stopped at a door marked *CONFERENCE ROOM B*.

Inside, I saw a long table. Around the table sat a dozen people in grey suits. They were featureless, their faces smooth like unformed clay. They were staring at a projection screen on the wall.

I looked at the screen.

It was the island.

It was being dissected. A laser pointer—controlled by one of the faceless figures—circled the Lighthouse.

"Structural integrity is failing," a voice droned from within the room. "The metaphor is collapsing. The protagonist is becoming self-aware. Recommendation: Pulping."

"They're talking about us," I said, backing away.

"They are the Committee," You said. "The Editors of Reality. They decide what stays and what gets filed under 'Fantasy'."

"We can't let them see us," I said. "If they see us, they'll rationalize us. They'll turn us into a case study."

We moved faster. The hallway seemed to stretch endlessly. But then, I saw something that made me stop.

A table.

It was set against the wall, isolated. On it sat a single object.

My coat.

The heavy, wool coat I had worn in Letter 12. The coat I had discarded in the whitespace. It was hanging there, looking heavy and solid, covered in the dust of the page.

And beside it...

The key.

The iron key. The one I had used to lock the trapdoor. The one I had left in the fire.

"It's here," I said, reaching out. My hand brushed the rough wool. It felt real. Colder than the air of the hallway.

"Don't touch it," You warned.

"Why not? It's mine. I remember it."

"Exactly," You said. "You remember it. That makes it a memory. And in this place, memories are artifacts. Evidence."

I pulled my hand back. "If I take it, I become a character again."

"If you leave it," You said, "you become a ghost."

Suddenly, the lights flickered. The humming of the tubes rose in pitch, becoming a scream.

A door opened down the hall. A door marked *JANITOR*.

A figure stepped out.

He was tall, wearing blue coveralls stained with ink. He carried a mop that dripped a dark, viscous fluid. He pushed a bucket on wheels that squeaked—a lonely, rhythmic sound.

He wasn't faceless like the Committee. He had a face. A tired, drooping face. A face that looked like it had been erased and redrawn a thousand times.

He stopped. He looked right at us.

"Hey," he grunted. "You kids aren't supposed to be here. The reading hour is over."

He pointed the mop at us. The dripping fluid hit the floor. Where it touched the linoleum, the white checkerboard turned into black ink. The floor began to dissolve.

"Run," You said.

We ran.

We ran past the Conference Room, past the water fountain that gurgled with a sound like rushing waves. We ran until the hallway ended.

A dead end.

But there was a window.

I stopped. I looked out.

The glass was thick, reinforced wire mesh embedded in the panes. Through it, I saw the sky.

It was grey. A flat, uniform grey. But below the sky, I saw them.

The Ships.

Dozens of them.

But they weren't on the water. They were in the sky. They were anchored to the grey clouds by massive chains.

I saw the *Iron Duke*, its hull rusty. I saw the glass vessel from Letter 30. I saw a ship made of human bones.

"The Parking Lot," I realized. "This is where the stories go when they aren't being read. They dry-dock them in the sky."

The Janitor's footsteps squeaked behind us. *Squeak. Squeak. Squeak.* He was taking his time. He knew we had nowhere to go.

"The window," You said. "It's the only way out."

"It's wire mesh," I said. "We can't break it."

"We don't have to break it," You said. "We have to describe it."

"What?"

"The pen is gone," You said. "But we are the ink now. You are Elias. You are a noun. A noun can be a hammer."

You looked at me. Your eyes were fierce.

"Tell me what the glass is," You commanded. "Define it."

I looked at the window. I thought of the cold, the barrier, the separation.

"It's brittle," I said. "It's a frozen liquid. It's a hesitation."

The Janitor was getting closer. I could smell the bleach and the old coffee on his breath.

"Louder," You urged.

"It's thin!" I shouted. "It's a lie! It's just sand that forgot it was sand!"

*CRACK.*

A fissure appeared in the center of the wire mesh glass. A spiderweb of fractures blooming outward.

The Janitor stopped. He tilted his head.

"Oops," he said.

"Again!" You shouted.

"It's breaking!" I yelled. "It's surrendering!"

*SHATTER.*

The glass exploded outward. The wind rushed in—not the air-conditioned draft of the hallway, but the raw, salt gale of the open sky.

The pressure differential sucked us toward the hole.

"Jump!" You said.

I looked at the table as I passed it. I looked at the coat. I looked at the key.

I grabbed them.

I grabbed the heavy wool coat and the cold iron key. I shoved them into my arms. The weight of them—the weight of the past, the weight of the object—anchored me.

"No!" The Janitor lunged, his mop handle striking the empty air where I had been.

We flew out the window.

We fell.

We didn't fall toward the ground. We fell *up* into the grey sky.

The wind howled. The chains of the ships clattered around us.

I wrapped the coat around us. It billowed out, catching the updraft, becoming a sail.

"Where are we going?" I screamed over the wind.

You pointed down. "To the dry dock! To the fleet!"

We landed hard on the deck of the *Iron Duke*.

The wood was splintered and grey. The sails were tattered rags. The ship was grounded on a bed of clouds, anchored by chains that stretched up into the infinite grey.

I stood up, gasping for air. You were beside me, shaking the dust from your yellow coat.

The ship was empty. Not a soul on board. The wheel was spinning lazily in the wind.

"Where is the crew?" I asked.
