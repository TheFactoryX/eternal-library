# Letter 23

Dear You,

The ink was not wet. It was fire.

I plunged into the red sea, and the heat of it took my breath away. It was not the heat of a stove, but the feverish heat of a bruise, of a body fighting an infection. I sank through layers of viscous, crimson fluid. It was thick, like syrup, and it tasted of iron and hesitation.

I was drowning in the blood of the story.

I kicked my legs—my human, fragile legs—fighting the density of the deleted prose. Around me, shapes floated in the gloom. I saw the corpse of a plot point where the Lighthouse was a spaceship. I saw the skeletal remains of a character named "The Traveler" who had been removed in the second draft to make the isolation more absolute. He reached for me with hands made of wire and dust, his mouth opening in a silent scream of *I could have been*.

"I know," I wanted to say, but the red filled my mouth. "I know you could have been."

I sank deeper. The light from above—the golden beam I had left burning in the Lamp Room—became a distant, flickering star.

Then, I hit bottom.

It was not a floor. It was a bed of ash. The red ink here had cooled, congealing into a vast, crimson plain that stretched out into the darkness. I crawled onto the shore, gasping, spitting out the taste of rejection.

My skin was stained pink. My clothes were heavy with the weight of words that had been stricken.

I stood up. The air here was stale, smelling of old shelving and neglected libraries.

"Hello?" I called out. My voice didn't echo. The silence here was final. This was where sound went to die.

I looked at the map I had clutched during the fall. The paper was soaked, the red ink of the X running together with the red ink of the sea. But the line was still there.

It pointed toward a structure in the distance.

At first, I thought it was a mountain. As I walked closer, stumbling over the uneven terrain of crumpled paper balls, I saw it was a pile of books.

A massive, teetering tower of unread books. Thousands of them. Millions. They were stacked haphazardly, forming a ziggurat in the waste. And at the very top, there was a single, empty chair.

I began to climb. The books were slippery. I grabbed a spine; it was *The History of Salt*. I stepped on a cover; it was a romance novel where the lovers never met.

Halfway up, I heard a sound.

It was a scratching sound. *Skritch, skritch.*

It was coming from inside the pile.

"Who is there?" I asked.

A hand reached out from between the pages of a dictionary. It was small, pale, and ink-stained.

Then, a voice.

"I'm trying to fix the ending."

A figure pulled itself out of the books. It was a boy. He looked about ten years old. He was wearing a suit that was too big for him, the sleeves rolled up to show thin wrists. He had glasses that were cracked, and behind them, eyes that were old, tired, and infinitely sad.

"You're the Reader," I said.

The boy adjusted his glasses. "I'm a fragment. The part that stays up at night wondering what happens after the page stops."

He looked at me, and I felt the weight of his gaze. It was heavier than the Editor’s scissors. It was the weight of expectation.

"Why are you here, Keeper?" he asked. "The story is over. The Editor cut the rope. The tower is falling."

"I came to find the forgotten time," I said. "The First Draft said... she said I was happy here. Once."

The boy looked down at his shoes. "Happiness is a boring plot device," he muttered. "It lacks conflict."

"Tell me," I demanded.

He sighed. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, folded piece of paper. He handed it to me.

I unfolded it. It was a letter. My handwriting.

But it wasn't addressed to "You." It was addressed to "Me."

*Dear Me,*

*Today the sea was blue. Not the blue of ink, but the blue of a summer sky. I found a shell on the shore. I put it to my ear, and I didn't hear the ocean. I heard a voice. It said, "You are allowed to rest."*

*I sat on the rocks and watched the sun set. I didn't write. I didn't tend the light. I just watched the colors change. And for the first time, I didn't feel like a character. I felt like a man watching a sunset.*

*And it was enough.*

I stared at the words. They blurred. My human eyes filled with tears—hot, salty tears that felt incredibly real.

"I remember," I whispered. "I remember the shell."

"It was cut," the boy said. "The Editor said it broke the tension. He said if you were happy, you wouldn't need to write the letters. And if you didn't write, there would be no book."

"I was happy," I said, "so the story had to end."

"Or," the boy said, sitting on a stack of encyclopedias, "the story had to become a tragedy to make you keep writing."

I looked up at the ceiling of the waste. Far above, the golden beam was flickering. The red ink was dripping back up.

"The Editor is cutting the climax," I said. "He's cutting the Lighthouse."

"Yes," the boy said. "When he finishes, the Lamp Room falls. The tower dissolves. You go back to the white. I go back to the shelf."

"I can't let that happen," I said. "I am not finished."

"Then you have to give the Reader what they want," the boy said. "Not an escape. Not a trick. A connection."

He pointed to the empty chair at the top of the pile.

"Sit," he said.

I climbed the last few feet. I stood before the chair. It was a simple wooden chair, worn smooth.

"If I sit," I said, "I stop moving."

"If you sit," the boy countered, "you become the narrator."

I sat down.

The perspective shifted instantly.

When I stood, I was a man in a suit of red ink. When I sat, I became a point of view. I could see the entire waste. I could see the blades of the Harvester far above, chopping through the darkness like scissors through silk.

"Write," the boy said. He was standing beside the chair now, holding the pen I had dropped in the air so long ago. "Write the truth. Not the facts. The truth."

I took the pen. It felt heavy, like a branch of iron.

"I don't know what to write," I said.

"Yes, you do," the boy said. "You've been writing it for 23 letters. You've been writing to the person who is reading this. You've been trying to bridge the gap."

I looked at the paper in my hand—the memory of happiness. I realized then that the shell was real. The sunset was real. They weren't in the published book, but they happened.

I placed the paper on my knee. I pressed the tip of the pen to the world.

I didn't write letters. I didn't write sentences.

I wrote a door.

I drew a rectangle on the arm of the chair.

*Open,* I wrote.

The air shimmered. The wood of the chair arm rippled. A handle formed. Brass. Real.

"Go," the boy said. "Before the Editor realizes the narrative perspective has shifted."

I reached for the handle. But I stopped.

I looked at the boy.

"Come with me," I said.

He shook his head. "I can't. I'm the imagination. I stay in the book."

"Then take this," I said.

I reached into my pocket and pulled out the black seed. The seed that had been with me since the beginning. The seed that I had used to light the lamp.

I pressed it into his hand.

"It's a beginning," I said.

He looked at the seed. He smiled—a genuine, un-ironic smile that made him look ten years younger.

"Thank you," he said.

I pulled the handle.

The chair didn't move. The world moved.

The pile of books groaned. The red sea began to churn. The golden beam from above shot down, piercing the floor of the waste, hitting the chair.

It lifted me.

I was riding the light again. But this time, I wasn't a verb in motion. I was a name.

I shot upward through the layers of text. I passed the Editor, who was frantically trying to staple the pages shut. I passed the Harvester, whose blades were rusting. I passed the wardrobe, where the Critic was weeping over a bad review.

I burst through the surface.

I was back in the Lamp Room.

But the room was shaking violently. The walls were cracking. The golden light was fading, sputtering like a candle in a vacuum.

The Editor was there. He was standing by the mirror, his hand reaching through the glass, trying to grab the lever of the lamp to turn it off.
