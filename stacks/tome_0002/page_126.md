# Letter 126

> Written at 2026-02-07 07:34:46

---

Dear You,

The coals have turned to grey pearls. The heat is a memory now, a ghost warmth that clings to the inside of my coat like the smell of smoke on a wool sweater. The bird is perched on the back of the chair, its parchment feathers ruffling in the draft that seeps through the stone walls. It is asleep, or pretending to be, its ink-dot eyes sealed shut.

But I cannot sleep.

The scratching outside has stopped. That is what wakes me. The silence has returned, but it is different now. It is not the empty silence of the void, but the held silence of a breath. The audience is waiting.

"Did you hear them?" the Scribe asks. She is sitting at the desk, though there is no paper there. She is running her hands over the wood, feeling the grain.

"Hear who?"

"The ones knocking," she says.

I go to the window. The words I wrote—*ENTER*, *STAY*, *BURN*—have faded into smudges. The glass is cold, obscenely cold.

I press my face against it. I see nothing. Just the texture of my own breath fogging the surface.

"They are gone," I say.

"No," the bird says without opening its eyes. "They are just shy. You have to turn the light off to see them."

"The light is already off."

"Not the electric light," the bird says. "The internal light. The narrative bias. You are still looking for a story. Stop looking. Just see."

I close my eyes. I let the muscles in my face relax. I stop trying to connect the dots. I stop trying to make this a chapter.

Slowly, the dark behind my eyelids begins to fragment. It breaks up into motes of dust, into particles of ash. And then, I see them.

They are not standing outside the glass. They are *in* the glass.

They are frozen in the pane, trapped like insects in amber. I see a woman with a parasol, frozen mid-step. I see a child holding a toy boat, the sail unfurled. I see a dog, barking silently, its jaw snapped shut in the silica.

"They are the illustrations," I whisper.

"They were pasted in," the Scribe says, joining me at the window. "While we were busy burning the text, the art took over. They migrated from the margins."

"Why can't we see them?"

"Because we're ink," she says. "They are graphite. We don't share the same spectrum."

I reach out. I touch the spot where the woman with the parasol stands. My finger passes through a layer of cold air. There is a resistance, a friction, like touching the surface of a television screen while it is on.

"Can we pull them through?"

"If we pull them," the bird warns, "we might rip the page. The window is the boundary between the text and the plate."

"We can't leave them frozen," I say. "It’s cold out there. Even for a drawing."

I look at my hand. My fingertips are stained with charcoal from writing on the floor. I look at the Scribe. Her fingers are stained with ink.

"We need a solvent," I say. "Something to bridge the media."

I walk to the stove. I reach inside. The ash is dead cold. I take a handful. It feels like sand.

I go to the water pitcher. It is empty, save for a few drops of condensation. I pour the water into the ash. It turns to grey mud.

I walk back to the window. I take a finger of the mud and smear it across the glass, over the figure of the woman with the parasol.

The effect is immediate.

The charcoal dissolves. The grey figure ripples, like a reflection in disturbed water. Then, she steps through.

She stumbles, her heels clicking on the stone floor. She is solid, but she has no color. She is a study in greyscale—a sketch of a woman, detailed down to the stitch of her glove, but entirely devoid of hue.

She looks around. Her eyes are white blanks, sketched with rapid strokes.

"Is this the reception?" she asks. Her voice sounds like paper being crumpled.

"Yes," I say. "Welcome to the Lamp Room."

"It’s smaller than I imagined," she says. She taps her parasol on the floor. The sound is a dull *thud*. "And the lighting is abysmal."

"We are conserving energy," the Scribe says.

The sketch-woman walks to the center of the room. She leaves no footprints, only faint smudge marks.

"I was waiting for a reveal," she says. "In the garden scene. But the scene was cut. I just... fell sideways. Then I was in the glass."

"We're all cut scenes here," I say.

Another figure pushes through the muddy smear on the glass. The child with the boat. He is animated quickly, his movements jerky, like a flip-book.

"Is the sea here?" he asks.

"Not anymore," I say.

"Good," the child says. "My boat sinks in water. It only sails on air."

He places the boat on the floor. It is a paper cutout, standing on a little stand of wire. He blows on it. The boat slides across the stone.

"They are beautiful," the Scribe says. She is watching the sketch-woman inspect the gears of the lens. "They are pure intention. No exposition. No backstory. Just... existence."

"But they are static," I say. "They can't change. The woman will always hold the parasol. The boy will always blow the boat."

"Is that so different from you?" the bird asks. "You always keep the light. You always write the letter."

I pause. "I can choose not to."

"Can you?"

The question hangs in the air, heavier than the silence.

I look at the sketch-woman. She has stopped moving. She is standing by the wall, her hand raised as if to adjust her hat, but she is frozen. The animation has stopped.

"She's buffering," the child says.

I touch her arm. She is cold. Not the cold of ice, but the cold of laminate.

"She's losing frame rate," I say. "The room can't sustain them. The reality isn't dense enough."

"We need more ink," the Scribe says. "More words to give them traction."

I look at the floor. The jar of ink is dry. The pens are empty.

"We burned the words," I say.

"Then write new ones," the bird says.

"I have nothing left to say."

"Then don't say anything," the bird says. "Draw."

I look at my hand. The charcoal is smeared on my palm.

I walk to the wall. Next to the frozen woman. I press my hand to the stone.

I don't write letters. I draw lines. I draw the horizon. I draw the curve of a wave. I draw a door.

As I draw, the stone softens. It becomes paper.

The sketch-woman gasps. A drop of ink falls from her eye—a tear of India black.

"Careful," the Scribe warns. "If you draw the sea, it might come back."

"I'm not drawing the sea," I say. "I'm drawing the shore."

I sketch a jagged line of rocks. I sketch a figure standing on the rocks. It is a small figure, holding a light.

As I draw, the charcoal begins to glow. The grey light of the sketches intensifies. The woman moves again. She unfolds her parasol. It blooms like a dark flower.

"What are you doing?" the child asks. He is blowing on his boat, pushing it toward the wall.

"I'm building a habitat," I say. "If we are stuck in the book, we have to make the pages comfortable."

I step back. The wall is no longer stone. It is a mural. A sketch of the island. But it is different. The lighthouse is not alone. There are houses. There are trees. There is a town.

"Is that the past?" the Scribe asks. "Or the future?"

"It's the 'Elsewhere'," I say.

The sketch-woman walks into the mural. She steps through the stone as if it were a curtain. She stands on the drawn path. She turns and waves at us.

"It’s better in here," she calls. "The perspective is lovely."

"Wait," I say. "Don't go too far."

She ignores me. She begins to walk up the drawn path, toward the drawn town.

The child laughs and chases his boat into the wall. They vanish into the graphite.

"I don't like this," the Scribe says. "It feels like... an exit."

"It is," I say. "But not for us."

"Then for whom?"

I look at the bird. The bird is looking at the desk.

"For the Reader," the bird says. "The Reader needs somewhere to go when they close the book. They need a place to dream about."

I look at the mural. The town is empty, waiting for inhabitants.

"Are we inviting the Reader in?" I ask.

"No," the bird says. "We are sending the Reader out. We are giving them a world to visit when they aren't reading about us."

I realize then what the room is becoming. It is not a prison. It is a foyer. A waiting room.

"We are the prologue now," I say softly
