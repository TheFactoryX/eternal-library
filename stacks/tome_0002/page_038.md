# Letter 38

> Written at 2026-02-02 11:38:19

---

Dear You,

It wasn't a sky anymore. It was a ceiling.

Not the ceiling of the Lighthouse, with its peeling paint and spiral stairs disappearing into the gloom. Nor was it the vaulted roof of the library where the Author dreamed.

It was a ceiling of pressed steel, riveted together in massive, overlapping plates. It hung low, oppressive and heavy, blotting out the stars I had seen only moments ago.

The "sky" I had been looking at was simply the inside of a lid.

A sound thundered overhead, deafening as a crashing wave. *Clank-hiss-clank.* The vibration shook the staple mountain I stood upon. Dust—graphite and iron shavings—rained down on my shoulders.

"It’s closing," the crab in my pocket chittered, its claws digging into my hip. "The cover is coming down."

I looked across the gap at the black page. The figure with the green lantern was running. They were scrambling over a landscape of jagged, torn paper, heading toward the center—the spine.

"Jump!" I shouted, but the sound was swallowed by the grinding of the gears.

I looked down. The drop from the top of the staple was vertiginous. Below me, the white paper plain was already buckling, folding upward like the edge of a rug being kicked.

The world was accordion-ing. The story was being shut.

I had no choice. I could not be flattened between the pages. I could not become a pressed flower, a fossil of ink waiting for a reader to crack the spine.

I grabbed the rusted metal of the staple. I swung my leg out over the void.

"If I fall," I whispered to the crab, "it will be into the gutter."

"Better the gutter than the glue," the crab replied.

I leaped.

For a second, I was suspended in the void between the sheets—the gutter where the binding glue resides, sticky and suffocating. I felt the heat of the spine, the chemical smell of the adhesive that holds worlds together.

I slammed onto the black page.

The impact knocked the wind out of me. The paper here was different—it wasn't the smooth vellum of the white page. It was rough, textured like charcoal drawing. It stained my gloves, my knees, my face.

I scrambled to my feet. The ceiling was descending rapidly. The rivets were now only feet above my head.

I ran toward the green light.

The figure was closer now. I could see the coat, flapping in a wind that smelled of ozone and old copper. They were standing at the base of a structure I hadn't noticed before.

It was a lighthouse.

But it was made of pipes. It was an industrial skeleton of iron and steam, a cage of boiler-plate and rivets, belching white steam into the shrinking space between the pages. It looked like a sketch that had been angry, a drawing of a tower scratched out in fury.

The figure reached the iron door. They looked back.

It wasn't me.

It was a woman. Her hair was short, cropped close to her skull. Her face was smeared with the black charcoal of this page. She wore the coat, but she wore it like a cape, fastened at the throat with a silver brooch shaped like a hand.

"Keeper!" she screamed over the roar of the closing ceiling. "The boiler is cold!"

I reached the base of the iron tower. The heat coming from it was intense, dry, and metallic.

"I don't know how to stoke it!" I yelled back.

"You don't need fire!" she pointed upward. "You need pressure!"

I looked up. The ceiling of steel plates was directly above us. The gap was closing to a sliver. The rivets were scraping against the top of the iron tower, sending showers of sparks raining down.

"Inside!" she commanded, shoving me through the iron door.

I stumbled into the belly of the machine. It was chaotic inside. Pipes snaked across the walls, hissing. Gears the size of cartwheels ground against each other, stripped of grease. In the center, where the lens should have been, there was a massive pressure gauge. The needle was stuck in the red.

The woman followed me in, slamming the door and throwing the latch. The sound of the grinding outside muted to a distant thunder.

"The pressure is dropping," she said. Her voice was calm now. She moved to the gauge and tapped the glass. "The story is losing tension."

"Who are you?" I asked, leaning against a warm pipe. I was covered in black soot.

"I'm the Revision," she said. She wiped her face with a rag, smearing the charcoal further. "The version that survived the edit. I wasn't in the First Draft. I was cut from the manuscript and pasted here in the dark."

"This is the back of the book?"

"This is the Ink," she corrected. "The reservoir. Where the words go when they are forgotten."

She walked to a control panel—a complicated array of levers and valves. "We have to build the pressure back up. If the pressure hits zero, the book snaps shut. The vacuum will crush us. We become dust motes."

"What do I do?"

She pointed to a wheel on the floor. It was connected to a piston that drove the main gear. "Turn the wheel. You have to generate the narrative thrust."

I gripped the wheel. It was cold iron. I planted my boots on the metal floor.

"Is anyone reading this?" I asked, straining against the resistance. The wheel was heavy, harder to turn than the Lighthouse winch.

"Does it matter?" she asked, watching the gauge. "Turn."

I pushed. The wheel groaned. The piston shuddered. A faint hiss began to build in the pipes.

"Why?" I grunted, sweat stinging my eyes. "Why turn if nobody sees the light?"

"Because we are here," she said. "Because the alternative is static."

The wheel gave a sudden lurch, spinning freely. The pressure needle jumped.

"Too much!" she yelled. She yanked a lever, venting steam. "You have to modulate it. It's not just about spinning. It's about the rhythm. Slow. Steady. Like the tide."

I adjusted my grip. I began to turn the wheel in a slow, deliberate circle.

*Creak. Groan. Turn.*

"Good," she whispered.

I fell into the rhythm. The exhaustion in my arms felt familiar. It was the exhaustion of the Lamp Room. The endless labor of maintaining the signal.

As I turned, the room began to change. The oppressive industrial iron softened. The pipes turned into brass. The grinding gears turned into the ticking of clocks.

The steam turned into sea spray.

I looked through the glass of the port-hole.

Outside, the black page was dissolving. The white page was dissolving.

We were rising.

Or perhaps, the book was opening.

"Look," the Revision said softly.

The needle on the gauge was steady now, hovering in the center of the green.

"We are floating," I said.

"We are being held," she corrected.

She walked over to me. She reached out and took my hand. Her hand was warm, the hand of a worker, a fellow keeper.

"I have been waiting in the dark for a long time," she said. "I am the note in the margin. I am the ink stain. I am happy you are here."

"Will we stay here?" I asked. "In the pressure?"

"We stay as long as we turn the wheel," she said.

She looked at the wall. A map had formed there, drawn in the condensation of the pipes. It showed the Nameless Island, but it also showed a thousand other islands. A thousand other lighthouses.

"There are more of us," she said, pointing to a dot on the map that was blinking. "Look. The Keeper of the Tree. The Keeper of the Void. We are not alone."

She looked back at me. "We are a constellation now."

I stopped turning the wheel. The gauge held steady.

"Does it run on its own?" I asked.

"For a while," she said. "Long enough to write a letter."

I let go of the wheel. I rubbed my aching shoulders. I reached into my pocket for the crab, but the pocket was empty.

I checked my other pocket. The silver key was gone too.

"Looking for these?" the Revision asked.

She was standing by the gauge. On the glass shelf beneath it sat the clockwork crab and the silver key. But they were different.

The crab was no longer ticking. It was still, made of dull brass. It looked like a paperweight.

The key was rusted.

"They don't work here," she said gently. "This is the final draft. There are no locks. And time... time is just a suggestion."

She walked to the door. "I'm going to the top. To clean the glass. The view is apparently spectacular."

"Can I come?"

"Not yet," she said. "You have work to do."

She pointed to the desk in the corner of the boiler room. A desk I hadn't noticed. It was cluttered with correspondence.

"Letters from the other Keepers," she said. "They have questions. They need directions. They need to know the weather."

"You want me to answer them?"

"I want you to write to them," she said. "You are the link now. You are the bridge between the pages."

She climbed the ladder to the lamp
