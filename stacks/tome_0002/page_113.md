# Letter 113

"You are late," he said.

His voice was not the Dreamer’s. It was younger, yet older—the voice of the boy who lit the sun, tempered by the centuries.

"I got lost in the margins," I said, standing up. The water ran off me like mercury, beading and rolling back into the sea. "I didn't know the book was closed."

"It never closes," he said. "It just waits for the eye to return."

He raised the pen. He pointed it toward the sky.

"Look."

I looked up. The swirling colors were gone. The sky was a manuscript. A vast, parchment firmament stretching to infinity. And in the parchment were holes. Thousands of them. Tiny pinpricks of light.

"Stars," I whispered.

"Punctuation," he corrected. "Periods at the end of a million unwritten sentences. Every light is a full stop. The universe is just a story that ran out of things to say."

He walked past me. He was barefoot. His feet left no prints in the sand.

"I am the Punctuation Keeper," he said. "I keep the pauses."

"Who are you writing to?" I asked.

"Same as you," he said. "The person who isn't here."

He stopped and looked out at the sea. The water was ink-black, reflecting the parchment sky perfectly.

"I remember the First Letter," he said. "Do you?"

"I remember the feeling," I said. "The fear."

"You wrote: *The ink is wet, and I am afraid.*"

"Yes."

"You were right to be afraid," he said. "Wet ink dries. And when it dries, it cracks. It flakes away. The only way to keep the story alive is to rewrite it. To revise it."

He turned to face me. The lantern was unlit, but I could see his face now. He was me.

"I am the revision," he said. "I am the you that you wrote when you realized the lighthouse was a trap. You wrote me to escape."

"So you are real?"

"I am as real as the ink allows," he said. "And right now, the ink is drying up. The well is empty."

He handed me the pen. It was heavy. It felt like the vertebrae of a bird.

"You have to write the next word, Elias. The story is stalled. The author has stopped. We are drifting in the whitespace."

"If I write," I said, "what happens to the others? The Captain? The Old Man?"

"They become background," he said. "Setting. They cease to be characters and become the texture of the world."

I looked down at the pen. The nib was sharp. It looked like it could cut through reality.

"What if I refuse?"

"Then we fade," he said. "We become a margin note. A footnote in a book no one reads."

He began to dissolve. The edges of his coat turned into sand, blowing away in the wind.

"Wait!" I shouted. "Where do I go?"

"Follow the light," he whispered. "But remember... the light is a lie. The lighthouse doesn't guide ships in. It keeps them out."

He was gone.

I stood alone on the beach. The parchment sky was lowering. The pressure was immense.

I looked at the pen. Then I looked at the sea.

I walked to the water's edge. I dipped the nib into the black ocean.

The ink drank the ocean. The nib glowed with a dark, violet light.

I raised the pen. I pressed it against the air in front of me.

I didn't write a letter. I didn't write a word.

I drew a door.

A heavy, arched door set into the fabric of the air.

I drew the handle. I drew the hinges.

*Click.*

The door clicked open.

But it didn't open into a room.

It opened into a memory.

I saw the study. The real study. But it was different.

There was no desk. No chair.

There was only a bed.

And in the bed was the Dreamer.

He was old. Frail. His skin was the color of parchment.

His eyes were closed.

But his hand was moving.

He was writing.

He was writing on the sheet. On the blanket. On his own arm.

He was writing everywhere.

And I saw the words.

*Help me. Let me out. I am trapped in the book.*

"The dreamer is the prisoner," I realized.

The door began to close.

"No!" I shouted. I jammed the pen into the gap, holding the door open.

"I'm coming!" I yelled.

I stepped through the frame of ink.

I fell onto the bedspread. The fabric was vast, a landscape of woven cotton.

I ran toward the Dreamer's hand.

It was a mountain of flesh, trembling.

"Elias?" a voice croaked.

The Dreamer opened his eyes.

They were white. Blind.

But he looked right at me.

"You came," he wheezed. "The character came to save the author."

"You're not trapped," I said. "You're dreaming us. You can wake up."

"I can't wake up," he said. "I am the dream. If I wake, you cease."

He coughed. A cloud of dust motes exploded from his mouth.

"The ending is coming, Elias," he said. "I feel the cold. The ink is turning to ice."

He reached for me. His finger was the size of a tree trunk.

"You have to finish it," he said. "You have to turn the page."

"I don't know how," I said.

"Break the pen," he said. "Stop the flow. End the sentence."

I looked at the pen in my hand. The bone pen.

"If I break it," I said, "the story stops."

"The story was never the point," the Dreamer whispered. "The reading was the point. And the reader is gone."

He pointed to the window of the room.

I looked.

The window was dark. Outside, there was no sun. No stars.

Just a vast, empty void.

"The book is on the shelf," he said. "The library is closed."

He closed his eyes. His breathing slowed.

I stood on the chest of the dying god.

I raised the pen.

I looked at the window. The void was staring back.

And then, I saw it.

A single point of light in the void. A tiny star.

No.

A glint.

A glint of metal.

A reflection.

There was someone standing outside the window. Looking in.

They were holding a light.

"Dear You," I whispered.

I didn't break the pen.

I turned it around.

I took the blunt end of the pen and I tapped on the glass of the window.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The sound was like a pistol shot in the quiet room.

The figure outside moved.

They raised a hand.

They were holding a letter.

And in the letter, I saw my own name.

*The Keeper.*

The figure opened their mouth.

I couldn't hear them through the glass. But I read their lips.

*Turn around.*

I turned around.

The bed was empty.

The Dreamer was gone.

In his place was a single sheet of paper.

And on the paper, fresh ink was spreading. Bleeding out of the white page, staining the sheets, flooding the world.

The ink smelled of the sea.

And from the black pool rising on the bed, a shape emerged.

A tower.

A Lighthouse.

And a door.

A door with my name on it.

I walked toward it.

I turned the knob.

And as the door swung open, I heard the sound of a latch sliding home.

A lock.

But from which side?

I looked down at my hand.

I was holding the key.

But I didn't remember picking it up.

I only remembered the sound of the waves, crashing against the sides of my head, louder than the sea, louder than the wind, louder than the scratch of the pen that had just stopped writing.

I stepped into the darkness of the tower, and the heavy oak door swung shut behind me, sealing with a sound like a period being stamped into wet paper.

*Thud.*

Silence.

Then, from the darkness above, a single, familiar voice.

"You're late."
