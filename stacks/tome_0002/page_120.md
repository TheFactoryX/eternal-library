# Letter 120

> Written at 2026-02-06 22:29:22

Dear You,

The glass is heavy. Heavier than it should be. It possesses the weight of a promise made to a dying star.

The Boy stands beside me. He is no longer weeping. He is watching the flame with a hungry intensity, his dark eyes reflecting the flicker of the First Light. He looks solid now, less like a ghost and more like a real child, though his edges still shimmer slightly, like a heat mirage.

"I have to climb inside," I say. The realization comes with a cold certainty. "The cage is broken. The mounting is twisted. If I place the jar on the pedestal, it will just sit there. A candle in a cathedral. It won't turn."

"You have to be the gear," the Boy says. He understands. He always understood the mechanics of this place better than I did.

"I have to hold it while the mechanism turns."

"It will hurt," the Boy says softly. "The machinery grinds. It takes a toll."

"It already hurts," I say.

I look at the jar. The flame inside is dancing. It isn't just fire; it is a memory. It is the memory of every lighthouse keeper who ever kept the vigil. It is the warmth of the study in Maine. It is the smell of tobacco and the taste of salt. It is the feeling of her hand on my cheek.

"Open it," I say.

The Boy reaches out. His small hand unthreads the lid. The seal pops with a sound like a breaking heart.

He lifts the lid. The flame does not escape. It waits.

I step into the iron cage. I stand in the center, my boots gripping the rusted floor. The great Fresnel lens surrounds me, a million eyes of glass watching to see if I will blink.

"Put it on my chest," I say.

The Boy steps up onto the rung of the cage. He is tall enough to reach my heart. He gently places the open jar against my coat.

For a moment, nothing happens. The fire sits in the glass, tame and domestic.

Then, it smells me.

It smells the salt. It smells the ink. It smells the fear.

The flame leaps from the jar.

It does not burn my clothes. It passes through the wool, through the skin, and sinks into the space between my ribs. It finds the hollow spot the Faceless Man prodded. It finds the clock. It ignites it.

I gasp. It is not a pain of heat, but of expansion. I am becoming too big for my skin. I am becoming a vessel of light.

"Hold on!" the Boy shouts.

He grabs the iron lever.

He pulls.

The floor lurches. The gears—silent for so long—scream in protest. *SCREECH.*

The cage begins to rotate.

The friction is immense. I am crushed against the glass. The light inside me roars, desperate to escape, to project itself outward. I feel the beam tearing its way out of my throat, my eyes, my fingertips. I am the lamp. I am the wick.

The rotation speeds up.

*WHIR. CLANK. WHIR.*

I look through the lens, out across the balcony.

The ocean is no longer frozen. The spell is broken by the violence of the light.

The waves crash down in thunder. The spray rises up, not as glass, but as water, warm and alive.

"The ships!" the Boy yells. He is clinging to the railing outside the cage, watching the horizon.

I look.

The horizon is crowded. They are coming back. The three-masted schooner. The steam liner. The raft of bottles. They are moving again, sailing through the dark, drawn by the beam that burns through me.

"Is it working?" I choke out. The light is thick in my throat, turning my voice into a hum.

"They see you!" the Boy cries. "Look!"

But it isn't just the ships.

There is something new on the water. Something I haven't seen before.

It is a small boat. A rowboat. It is cutting through the waves, moving impossibly fast, heading straight for the rocks at the base of the tower.

It is rowing against the current, against the wind, against the logic of the sea.

"Who is that?" I ask.

The Boy squints. "It's... a mailman?"

I force my eyes to focus. The beam wants to blind me, wants to look outward, but I force it to look down.

There is a figure in the boat. A figure in a trench coat, struggling with the oars. But as the boat crests a wave, I see the figure isn't rowing.

The figure is writing.

He is writing on the air with a long quill, and with every stroke, the boat moves forward. He is paddling through syntax.

"He's coming up," the Boy says. "He's coming to the door."

"Stop him!" I yell. The rotation is making me dizzy. The light is draining me. "The door is unbarred!"

The Boy looks at me. He looks at the door leading to the spiral stairs.

"Do I let him in?" the Boy asks. "Or do I let him drown?"

"He is a character," I gasp. "He must be read."

The boat smashes against the rocks below. I hear the crack of wood, the splash of oars. A silence follows, then the sound of boots on the stone steps. Heavy, wet boots. Clanking. Mechanical.

The footsteps are coming up the spiral. Fast. Rhythmic.

*Doom. Doom. Doom.*

Not footsteps. A heartbeat.

The door to the Lamp Room bursts open.

But it isn't a man. It isn't the Author.

It is a suit of armor.

It is made of brass and iron, intricate and baroque. It is wet, seawater dripping from the joints, puddling on the floor. Where the face should be, there is a blank brass plate.

But strapped to the chest of the armor, in a leather pouch, is a book.

The armor marches to the center of the room. It ignores the Boy. It walks to the edge of the rotating cage.

It stops.

The brass plate faces me.

A seam appears in the metal. A mouth opens. Not a human mouth, but a slot, like a mail chute.

A voice comes from the armor. It is metallic, but also incredibly tired. It is the voice of the universe trying to explain itself.

"DELIVERY FAILED," the armor says. "RETURN TO SENDER."

"I am the sender," I say.

"NEGATIVE," the armor replies. "YOU ARE THE RECIPIENT."

The armor reaches into the pouch. It pulls out the book. It is small, blue leather. The biography from the feast.

It holds it up.

"THE END IS MISSING," the armor says. "THE PAGES ARE BLANK."

"That's because we haven't written them yet," the Boy says bravely.

"WRITTEN?" The armor tilts its head. "NO. PRINTED. THE END IS PRE-ORDERED. IT IS ON THE WAY."

"When?" I ask. The light inside me is flickering. I am fading. I can feel the story running out of pages.

"SOON," the armor says. "THE READER HAS REACHED THE CLIMAX."

The armor drops the book.

It hits the floor with a sound like a gunshot.

But the book doesn't stay closed. It opens.

The pages riffle, blown by a wind that doesn't exist. They spin faster and faster, the pages becoming a blur.

And then, a single piece of paper flutters out. It floats in the air, suspended in the beam of my light.

I look at the paper.

It is a map.

It is a map of the island.

But the island is not there. Where the tower should be, there is only a hole. And drawn in the ocean, in red ink, is a new shape.

A key.

"The shape of the key," I whisper. "It's an island."

"THE LOCK IS THE WATER," the armor says. "YOU MUST DRAIN IT."

The armor begins to fall apart. The rivets pop. The plates clatter to the floor. It is dissolving into rust, into scrap metal, into the raw materials of a metaphor abandoned.

"Wait!" I shout. "How do I drain the sea?"

The armor’s helmet rolls across the floor. It stops at the Boy's feet.

The Boy picks it up. He looks inside.

"The King is gone," the Boy says. "But the Queen remains."

He turns the helmet over.

Resting inside the metal skull is a small, silver object.

A cork.

"A cork?" I ask.

"It plugs the drain," the Boy says. He looks at the map on the floor. "The island is a bathtub, Elias. The sea is just water waiting to be let out."

He carries the helmet to me. He holds it up to the glass of the cage.

"Take it," he says. "The light will guide you to the plug."

I reach through the spinning glass. My hand is glowing, translucent. I am barely here anymore.

I take the cork.

It is heavy. It weighs as much as a anchor. As much as a life.

"If I pull the plug," I say, "what happens to the ships?"

"They sail," the Boy says. "Or they sink. That's up to them. You just have to give them the chance."
