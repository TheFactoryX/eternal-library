# Letter 109

> Written at 2026-02-06 10:43:35

---

Dear You,

...he was still alone."

The sentence sat there on the white page like a heavy stone in a pond. The ink was still glossy, catching the morning sun, a pool of black tar trapping us within its syntax.

"He stopped," the Old Man whispered, his voice thin as the paper itself. "In media res. The Dreamer has been interrupted."

I looked toward the door of the study. The silence was broken not by a step, but by a vibration. A rhythmic thudding that rattled the floorboards of the desk.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

"He is coming," I said. "The Dreamer."

The doorknob turned. It was a monumental event, the brass shining like a fallen sun. The door swung open, and a Titan entered. He was a young man, his face blurred by the sheer distance between his scale and mine, but I could feel his mood. He was frustrated. He was chewing on the end of a pen—a wooden girder stained with teeth marks.

He marched to the desk. The wind of his passing knocked me flat against the paper. He sat down. The chair screamed under his weight.

He stared at the sentence. *...he was still alone.*

"Writer's block," the Old Man scoffed, crawling out from the shadow of a coffee mug stain. "The most dangerous monster of all. Look, Elias. He doesn't know how to finish us."

The Dreamer picked up the pen. He hovered it over the paper. The tip was a cavernous mouth, dripping the black blood of existence.

"We have to influence him," I said. "If he writes 'The End,' we dissipate. We become ink."

"We need a verb," the Old Man hissed. "Something active. Something that forces a new paragraph."

I looked around the desk. The "Real World" was so close. The window with the torn screen was only a few yards away—a hike of inches for a man, a marathon for a speck. But the air was stagnant here.

I needed to create a wind.

I grabbed the Bone Compass from my pocket. The needle was spinning wildly, sensing the magnetic pull of the conclusion.

"Do you have the breath?" the Old Man asked.

I didn't know. But I had the memory of the Lighthouse. I remembered the gale that howled through the lantern room. I remembered the scream of the storm.

I climbed onto the porcelain ridge of the coffee mug. I raised the compass like a horn.

"HEY!" I screamed. It was a tiny sound, a squeak in the vast cathedral of the room.

The Dreamer didn't react. He tapped the pen against the paper. *Tap. Tap. Tap.*

"He's critiquing us," the Old Man said. "He's deciding if we are worth the ink."

The pen lowered.

I saw the word forming in his mind. I saw it travel down the barrel of the pen.

*Deleted.*

"No!" I shouted. I couldn't let him blot us out.

I looked at the torn piece of paper on the desk. The sentence *...he was still alone.*

If I could change the context of the sentence, I could change the ending.

I ran toward the letter 'a' in "alone". It was a jagged mountain of dried ink.

"Help me!" I yelled to the Old Man. "We have to pull the 'e'! We have to make it 'all one'!"

"Grammar gymnastics," the Old Man grumbled, but he ran. He vaulted over a period, his robes flapping. He grabbed the tail of the 'e'.

I grabbed the crossbar.

"Heave!"

The ink was tacky. It resisted like tar. But the 'e' was loose, a dried flake on the surface of the page. As we pulled, the word broke.

*Alone* became *All one*.

The Dreamer blinked. He rubbed his eyes.

He looked at the paper. The sentence now read: *...he was still all one.*

"Confusion," the Old Man wheezed. "Buy the window! Run for the window!"

The Dreamer frowned. He reached for the white-out bottle.

"The correction fluid," I gasped. "He's going to paint over us!"

I scrambled down from the paper, sliding down the wooden leg of the desk lamp. The floor was a vast plain of dust bunnies and lost screws.

"The window," I repeated.

But we weren't alone on the floor.

Standing near the wall, its antenna twitching, was a spider. It was the size of a wolf. Its body was glossy black, segmented like a typewriter ribbon.

"It’s a syntactic spider," the Old Man whispered, shrinking back. "It eats plot holes."

The spider turned its multiple eyes toward us. It didn't see food. It saw *characters*.

"We don't have to outrun it," I said, looking at the window screen. The mesh was too fine. Even in smoke form, we couldn't slip through the wires. But the tear... the tear was held open by a bent piece of wire.

A paperclip.

A giant, silver paperclip, twisted and discarded.

"We have to climb it," I said.

The spider scuttled forward, the sound of its legs on wood like a cracking whip.

I ran. I ran toward the paperclip like it was the spire of salvation. The Old Man was ahead of me, his tiny legs pumping.

"Wait!" he cried.

He stopped. He turned to face the spider. He raised his cane.

"Old Man, no!" I screamed.

"I am the First Draft," the Old Man shouted. "I am the foundation!"

He swung the cane. He didn't strike the spider. He struck the floorboards. A knot in the wood, loose and rotten.

The spider stumbled, its leg caught in the hole.

"Go, you fool!" the Old Man yelled. "Climb!"

I scrambled up the silver arch of the paperclip. The metal was cold. My fingers slipped on the smooth surface.

Behind me, I heard the *crunch*.

I didn't look back.

I reached the top of the paperclip. I looked out through the tear in the screen.

The garden.

The smell of pollen was intoxicating. The green of the grass was a violent, overwhelming color after the grey of the study.

I jumped.

I fell through the air. I hit the grass.

It was soft. It was wet with dew.

I rolled, coming to a stop at the base of a towering dandelion.

I was out.

I lay on my back, gasping. The sky was a piercing, painful blue. There were clouds. Real clouds. Not shapes made of text.

"Elias," a voice said.

I sat up.

It was the Old Man.

He was standing there. But he was different.

He was fading. His edges were blurring, turning from ink to light.

"How?" I asked.

"The spider," he said. "It took the draft. It bit me. The venom is... reality."

He was dissolving. His deep water robe was turning into sunlight.

"You're leaving," I said.

"I was never here, Elias," the Old Man said. "I was just the explanation. You don't need an explanation anymore. You just need to live."

He pointed to the sky.

"Look."

A bird was flying overhead. A real bird. A robin.

But as it flew, it left a trail. Not smoke. Not feathers.

It left a ripple in the air. A shimmering distortion, like heat over asphalt.

And in that distortion, I saw words. Flickering in and out of existence.

*...he was still all one...*

"The story isn't over," the Old Man said, his voice becoming the sound of the wind in the grass. "It just changed medium. It’s not being written anymore. It’s being lived."

He vanished. A puddle of sunlight on a blade of grass was all that remained.

I stood up. I brushed off my coat. The dark blue wool was tattered, stained with ink and coffee.

I looked down at my hand. I was still holding the photograph.

I opened it.

The woman was gone. The fish was gone.

In their place was a mirror. A tiny, silver mirror. And in the mirror, I saw my own face.

But I wasn't looking at the camera.

I was looking *out*. I was looking at the world I had just entered.

I turned toward the house. The window was high above.

I saw the Dreamer. He was sitting at the desk. He was looking out the window.

He was looking right at me.

He raised his hand. He wasn't holding a pen. He was holding a cup of tea.

He smiled.

Then, he closed the window.

The latch clicked.

*Click.*

The sound was final.

I stood in the garden, the silence of the "real" world pressing in around me. I took a step forward.

"Hello?" I called out. My voice sounded small. Dull.

There was no answer. Just the hum of a lawnmower in the distance.

And then, a sound from the sky. A mechanical whirring.

I looked up.

A plane. Writing a message in the sky.

But the letters were dissolving before I could read them.

I started to walk toward the house. I had to know. I had to see.
