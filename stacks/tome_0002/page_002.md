# Letter 2

> Written at 2026-01-31 15:58:07

---

Dear You,

I write this with a thumb that throbs in time with the ocean’s heartbeat, a rhythmic, dull ache that reminds me I am still flesh and bone, despite the way this place tries to erode me.

You asked—no, I asked—do I remember who I was? The question hangs in the damp air of the lighthouse like the smoke from a cigarette that was never lit. Today, I tried to answer it. I tried to force the locks of memory open, but the machinery of my mind is rusted stiff. Instead, I found other things.

It started with the glass. In my last letter, I told you of the shards on the floor, the debris of a city made of crystal that lived and died in my sleeping hours. I swept them up, or I thought I did. I swept them into a pile and tipped them into a small, porcelain dish I keep on my desk. But when I woke this afternoon, the dish was empty. The glass was gone. Yet, when I walked barefoot across the flagstones to the window, a sharp, stinging pain shot through my heel. I looked down. Embedded in my skin was a tiny, perfect shard of that same dreaming-glass. It was glowing. A faint, violet luminescence, pulsing. I pulled it out with a pair of tweezers. The wound did not bleed. Instead, a viscous, clear liquid welled up, smelling faintly of ozone and lightning.

Is it possible to bleed light? If so, I think I am doing it.

I do not know what the glass means. Perhaps it is a warning. Perhaps it is a map. I kept the shard. It sits on the desk now, beside the inkwell, catching the dying rays of the sun.

The sun is a traitor today. It refuses to set. It has been hovering at the horizon for what feels like days, a swollen, orange coin dripping molten copper into the mercury-colored sea. The light is relentless. It casts no shadows, only long, distorted stretches of darkness that seem to detach themselves from the objects that cast them. I watched my own shadow stand up and walk toward the wall while I sat perfectly still in my chair. I wanted to call out to it, to ask it where it was going, but my throat was tight. It vanished into the stone.

The door I found yesterday—the one that led to the velvet hallway—is gone, as I knew it would be. But the Lighthouse is not done with its games. This morning, the spiral stairs betrayed me. I was descending to the kitchen, intending to brew tea from the dried leaves I found in a jar labeled *“Forgetting”*.

I know the stairs. I have climbed them a thousand times. There are three flights between the Lamp Room and the kitchen. Three. Always three. But today, as I stepped off the second landing, my foot found not stone, but grass.

I froze. The air changed instantly. The smell of salt and brine was replaced by the scent of damp earth, loam, and crushed mint. I looked down. I was standing on a lush, green hillside. The stairs had simply ceased to be behind me; in front of me, they stretched onward, but they were made of wooden planks, worn and grey, twisting through a field of wildflowers that grew tall and chaotic, brushing against my trousers.

I was outside, yet I was inside. The walls of the lighthouse were gone. The ceiling was a sky of impossible, swirling violets and golds. The wind here was gentle, warm. It kissed my cheek. For a moment, I felt a surge of joy so sharp it brought tears to my eyes. I thought, *This is it. This is the way out.*

I ran. I ran down the wooden stairs, leaping over the flowers—bluebells, poppies, things with petals like tiny hands. I ran until my lungs burned, following the curve of the hill, looking for a road, a house, a tree, a sign of life.

There was nothing. Just the hill, rolling on forever, and the violet sky.

And then, I heard a sound. A child’s laughter. It was bright and clear, coming from just beyond the rise. I knew that sound. I knew it intimately. It sparked a memory of a swing set, of a woman’s voice calling a name—my name?—of the taste of strawberry ice cream melting in the sun.

“You!” I shouted. “Wait! Please!”

I scrambled up the rise, my fingers tearing into the turf. When I reached the crest, I looked down, ready to finally see a face, ready to finally be seen.

There was no one.

The hill below was empty. The laughter had stopped. But lying in the grass, right where the sound had been, was an object. I walked down slowly, the dread returning like a cold tide. It was a music box.

It is made of dark mahogany, inlaid with mother-of-pearl. The inlay forms a pattern I cannot quite focus on; every time I try to trace the lines, they seem to shift, like the grain in wood trying to communicate.

I picked it up. It was heavy. I wound the key. The mechanism was stiff, grinding like bone on stone. When I opened the lid, there was no dancer, no spinning figurine. Only a mirror.

And the song... the song it played was not a melody. It was a conversation. I could hear voices—two voices—talking softly over the crackle of a fire. They were laughing. They were saying, *“We’ll be okay,”* and *“I promise, I’ll never leave.”*

I slammed the lid shut. The sound cut off instantly, silenced by the closing of the box.

I stood there for a long time, holding the box, waiting for the hill to vanish, for the grass to turn back into cold stone. But it didn't. The sun remained suspended, the light grew no dimmer. I had to make it leave. I had to force myself back.

I closed my eyes and pictured the grey stone. I pictured the damp. I pictured the crushing isolation. I concentrated on the ache in my thumb. *I am the Keeper,* I thought. *I tend the light.*

When I opened my eyes, I was standing on the stone landing of the second floor, right where I had started. The grass was gone. The smell of mint was replaced by the familiar odor of kerosene and old dust. My heart was hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird.

I am back now. I am sitting at my desk. The music box sits before me. It looks impossibly out of place here amidst the rust and the grey.

I have not opened it again. I am afraid of what I might see in the mirror. I am afraid I might see a face that is not mine, smiling at someone who is not you.

The sea outside has changed again. The mercury has drained away, leaving a water that is perfectly clear, like glass. I can see deep into it. I can see shapes moving down there—great, leviathan shadows with trailing tendrils, drifting through the canyons of the deep. They are glowing, pulsing with the same violet light as the shard in my foot.

One of them is rising. It is breaking the surface. I can see a dorsal fin, translucent and winged. It is circling the island.

I think it is waiting for the light to fail.

I must go. The lamp needs tending. The flame is sputtering, and the wind outside is beginning to howl my name again, louder this time. It sounds less like the wind and more like a demand.

Do not worry. I will keep the light burning. Even if it burns me.

Yours, in the static,

The Keeper
