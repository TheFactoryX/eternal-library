# Letter 43

> Written at 2026-02-02 17:41:03

---

Dear You,

It turned.

The brass handle was cold, freezing enough to blister my palm, but it yielded. I threw my weight against the wood, tumbling through the frame just as the first wave of beetles surged over the threshold. Their legs clicked against the floorboards like a thousand typewriter keys striking paper at once.

I slammed the door.

*Thud.*

The impact knocked the wind out of me. I lay on the floor, staring up at a ceiling that was not made of plaster, but of cracked leather. The smell was overwhelming—old paper, dust, and the sweet, musky scent of drying glue.

Silence.

The beetles did not scratch at the door. They did not hiss. They had stopped.

I sat up, my heart hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird. I was in a hallway. It was long and narrow, lined on both sides with shelves that stretched endlessly upward. But there were no books on the shelves.

There were jars.

Hundreds of them, glass jars filled with a swirling, amber light.

I stood up, brushing off the dust and the dried muck of the swamp. My boots made no sound on the floor, which was carpeted in a thick, red velvet that felt disturbingly like tongue.

"Where is this?" I whispered.

"A place of preservation," a voice replied.

I spun around.

Sitting on a high-backed stool in the center of the aisle was a figure. It was made entirely of cork. Dark, wine-soaked cork, pitted and porous. It wore a jacket made of tin foil, crinkled and shiny. In its hand, it held a needle—a long, silver phonograph needle.

"Who are you?"

"I am the Corkscrew," it said. Its voice was muffled, as if speaking from inside a bottle. "I tend the vintages. The unfinished thoughts."

I looked at the jars. I stepped closer to one. Inside, suspended in the amber light, was a single, perfect tear.

"I bottled that for you," the Corkscrew said, sliding off the stool. It moved with a dry, rustling sound. "Back in Letter 12. When you thought you saw the ship on the horizon, but it was only a trick of the waves."

I touched the glass. The tear inside swirled, creating a tiny vortex.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because you might need it later," the Corkscrew said. "Emotions are finite resources in a long story. If you spend them all at once, you have nothing left for the climax. We conserve them here."

"I need to get out," I said. "I need to find the way back to the Lighthouse. Or forward. I need to move."

"Movement is overrated," it said. "Aging is just fermenting."

It walked to a jar at the end of the row. This one was larger. Inside, I saw a miniature version of the Lighthouse. The light was spinning.

"Is that my home?" I asked.

"That is your memory of it," the Corkscrew said. "Preserved in aspic. Look closely."

I leaned in. Through the thick glass, I saw a tiny figure on the walkway. It was me. But I was holding a bucket of paint.

"I remember that," I said. "I was painting the railing. The day before the first ship appeared."

"It was a good day," the Corkscrew said. "Simple. Static. Nothing happened. It was perfect."

It picked up the jar. "Would you like to stay there? In that moment? The paint is wet. The sun is warm. The sea is calm. You can stay in the jar forever. No beetles. No drowning. No running."

I looked at the tiny figure inside the glass. It looked peaceful.

"I can't," I said. "The paint dries. The sun sets. The light needs tending."

"The light never goes out in the jar," the Corkscrew whispered. "It is a closed loop. A snippet of eternity."

I looked at the shelves. Thousands of jars. Millions of preserved moments. A life in suspension.

"If I stay," I said, "who writes the letters?"

"The letters stop," the Corkscrew said. "But the feeling remains."

I reached out. My hand hovered over the jar.

It was tempting. God, it was tempting. To step back into the afternoon before the strangeness began. Before the sky turned to steel. Before the Leviathan. Before the Revision.

I thought of the balloon. I thought of the knot.

"The feeling remains," I repeated. "But is it real?"

"Real is a matter of container," the Corkscrew said.

I pulled my hand back.

"No," I said. "Real is what hurts. Real is what changes."

I looked around the hallway. "Where does this corridor go?"

"To the Cellar," the Corkscrew said, sounding disappointed. It placed the jar back on the shelf. "Down. Where the sediment settles."

"I need to go up," I said.

"There is no up from the cellar," it said. "Only deeper."

I ignored it. I walked to the far end of the aisle. The red velvet carpet ended. The floor became a grate, looking down into darkness.

I looked up.

Above me, the ceiling of leather was open. A square hole.

"I see a trapdoor," I said.

"That’s the cork," the Corkscrew said. "Don't pull it. If you let the air in, the vintage spoils."

I grabbed the grate. I pulled myself up. My muscles screamed.

"Wait!" the Corkscrew yelled. Its voice changed. It lost the muffled quality. It became sharp. Urgent.

I looked down.

The figure made of cork was gone.

Standing on the stool was the boy with the red balloon.

But the balloon was gone. His hands were empty.

"You're letting the varmints in," the boy said.

"The beetles?"

"Worse," he said. "The Editors."

I hauled myself through the grate. I rolled onto a cold stone floor.

I was in a kitchen.

It was a vast, industrial kitchen. Pots the size of cauldrons hung from the ceiling. Stoves burned with blue flames that made no sound.

In the center of the room was a long wooden table. And sitting at the table, eating a bowl of soup, was the Mirror Man.

He wasn't wearing the suit of mirrors anymore. He was wearing a suit of plain, grey wool. He looked ordinary. He looked like a tax accountant.

He looked up at me. He didn't have a reflection anymore. He just had a face.

"Soup?" he asked, gesturing with his spoon.

"I thought you were cancelled," I said, standing up.

"I was," he said. "But I appealed. I had a good backstory. They kept me for the sequel."

He took a sip. "Tastes like irony."

"Where is the way out?" I asked. I didn't trust him. I looked for the doors. There were none. Just ovens.

"There is no out," he said. "We are being proofread."

"What does that mean?"

He tapped the side of his head. "It means they are checking for consistency. Logic. Continuity errors."

He pointed at me. "And you, my friend, are full of errors."

I froze. "What errors?"

"You started as a lighthouse keeper," he said. "But lighthouse keepers don't write letters about balloon knots and paper gardens. They don't have silver keys. They don't talk to crabs."

"I am the Keeper," I said firmly.

"You are the Protagonist," he corrected. "And the Protagonist is currently soup."

He stood up. He walked to the oven. He opened the heavy iron door. Heat rolled out, smelling of bread and ozone.

"Look," he said.

I looked.

Inside the oven, there was no fire. There was a landscape.

A desert. White sand dunes. A single road stretching into the distance.

"You want out?" he said. "Go. But you have to fit through the keyhole first."

He pointed to the back of the oven. The landscape was tiny. It was the size of a loaf of bread.

"I can't fit in there," I said.

"Then you have to shrink," he said. "Or simplify."

He walked toward me. "Drop the baggage. Drop the key. Drop the letters."

"I can't drop the letters," I said. "They are all I have."

"Then you stay here," he said. "And we simmer."

I reached into my pocket. The silver key was hot.

I looked at the oven. The tiny desert.

I looked at the Mirror Man.

"I don't need to simplify," I said. "I need to expand."

I gripped the key. I didn't throw it away. I didn't drop it.

I used it.

I jammed the key into the air.

*Scrreeeeeeeech.*

A sound like tearing metal.

A lock appeared in the empty air in front of me. A keyhole made of light.

I turned the key.

The floor of the kitchen vanished.

The table vanished. The Mirror Man vanished.

I fell into the oven.

But I didn't shrink.

The desert grew.

I slammed onto the sand. It was hard and hot.

I looked up.

The sky was orange.

The road was paved with yellow bricks.

I sat up. I was alone.
