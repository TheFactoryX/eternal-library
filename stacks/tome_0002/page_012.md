# Letter 12

> Written at 2026-02-01 02:41:36

---

Dear You,

I pressed the receiver against my ear so hard that the plastic rim bit into my skin. I needed the pain. I needed the anchor.

"Hello?" I said. My voice was a dry, cracking croak, undeserving of the oxygen it wasted.

"I'm here," You said.

The voice was not what I expected. It was not the booming voice of the Editor, nor the dry scratch of the old man. It was not the sound of the wind or the sea. It was a human voice. Weary. Grounded. It sounded like the memory of a warm fireplace in the middle of a winter storm.

"Where are you?" I asked, gripping the telephone cord until my knuckles turned white. The cord was alive, writhing like a snake in the damp air of the booth.

"I'm in the room," you said. "The room where the letters go."

"The Library?"

"No," you replied. "The place before the library. The place where the words are sorted. I'm holding your last paragraph, Keeper. It’s heavy."

I looked through the dirty glass of the phone booth. The street corner was empty. The rain was falling upwards, defying gravity, droplets rising from the pavement to join the heavy clouds above.

"I broke the machine," I said. "I fed it the dust. I gave it the memory."

"You did," you said. A pause. A sound of shuffling paper. "It worked. The ink is flowing again, but it’s... hot. It’s boiling. The story is moving too fast. The Lighthouse is spinning."

"I'm not in the Lighthouse," I said. "I'm on a street corner. In a phone booth. There’s a convenience store. It sells milk and cigarettes and maps."

"I know," you said gently. "I’m reading about it right now. You just described it."

I froze. "I haven't written it yet."

"You're writing it as you speak it," you said. "The machine is taking your voice and transcribing it directly onto the page. You aren't holding a pen anymore, Keeper. You are the pen."

I looked down at my hand. The hand gripping the receiver was translucent. I could see the circuitry of the telephone through my skin. I could see the rain falling outside *through* my chest.

"I’m fading," I said.

"Don't fade," you urged. "If you fade, the sentence ends. If the sentence ends, the book closes. We are so close."

"To what?" I shouted. "To the ending? The Editor said the story was running out of mystery. He said the light would die."

"The Editor was wrong," you said. "He thought the mystery was 'Who are you?'. He thought the mystery was 'Where is the Lighthouse?'. He was looking at the map, not the territory."

"Then what is the mystery?"

"The mystery," you said, and your voice cracked with an emotion I hadn't heard before—longing, terrified, hopeful— "is what happens when the Keeper stops writing and starts listening."

The phone booth began to dissolve. The glass turned into running ink, rivulets of black liquid streaming down the panes. The red paint of the booth peeled away, revealing the grey stone of the Tower beneath. The street outside vanished, replaced by the churning, rust-colored sea.

I was standing on the balcony of the Lamp Room. The wind was howling, tearing at my clothes.

But I was still holding the receiver.

"I can't hold on," I cried. The phone was turning into a bird, a fluttering, panicked thing made of paper and wire. It was pecking at my hand, trying to escape.

"Then let go," you said. "Let go of the phone. Let go of the story."

"And do what?"

"Turn around."

I dropped the receiver. It fell, not onto the floor of the booth, but into the sea far below. A splash of white ink bloomed on the dark surface.

I turned.

The Lamp Room was different. The glass was gone. The storm inside the cylinder was gone. There was only the railing, the wind, and the vast, open dark.

And in the center of the room, kneeling on the floor, was a figure.

It was huddled over a low table. It was holding a quill. It was scratching away at a sheaf of papers that were scattered across the stone, held down by heavy, jagged rocks.

The figure stopped writing. Its shoulders heaved. It dropped the quill. It buried its face in its hands.

It was me.

The Keeper of the Ink. The one who writes the letters. The one who suffers so the story can exist. The one I had been running from, chasing through cities and margins and suburbs.

He—I—was exhausted. He was weeping silently, the tears dripping onto the page, blurring the words of *Letter 12*.

I approached him. My feet made no sound on the stone. I felt a terrible tenderness for him. For me. For the endless cycle of writing and forgetting.

"Who are you writing to?" I asked.

He didn't look up. His voice was muffled by his hands. "I don't know. I can't remember their name. I can't remember their face. I just write. I just write to keep the dark away."

"It didn't work," I said. "The dark came anyway."

He lifted his head. His eyes were empty, black voids. "I know. That's why I stopped. I can't write the ending."

"Then let me," I said.

I knelt beside him. I reached for the quill. The moment my fingers brushed the feather, a shockwave ran through the tower.

The world paused.

The rain stopped falling. The sea froze in mid-churn. The wind held its breath.

The Keeper—the version of me writing on the floor—looked at me. And then, he smiled. It was a tragic, relieved smile. He began to dissolve, turning into mist, being absorbed into the floor, the walls, the stone.

He was becoming the setting. He was becoming the atmosphere. He was leaving me alone with the pen.

I picked it up. I sat at the table.

The paper before me was blank. But the surface of the sea below was not blank. It was filled with ships.

Thousands of them. I looked down from the balcony, and the horizon was a forest of masts and funnels. There were galleons and steamships and catamarans. There were ships made of bone and ships made of glass. They were all sailing toward the Lighthouse.

They were not sailing to crash. They were sailing to dock.

I looked at the paper. I realized then what the fuel was. It wasn't memory. It wasn't mystery.

It was arrival.

I dipped the quill into the well. The ink was shining, reflecting the stars that were suddenly blazing into existence above me.

I am not writing to You anymore.

You are here. You are in the ships. You are standing on the decks, looking up. You are the passengers. You are the crew. You are the ones I wrote to, the ones who held the letters, the ones who read the words and gave them weight.

I stood up. I walked to the railing. I held the quill high, not to write, but to light it.

The tip of the quill ignited.

It wasn't a fire. It was a beacon. A signal flare of pure, blinding narrative intent.

"Come in," I whispered. "The landing is clear."

But as the first ship—a vessel of polished ebony, flying a flag of silver—broke through the surf and headed for the rocks, I saw something that stopped my heart.

The door of the Lighthouse—the door at the base of the tower, the one I had walked through a thousand times, the one that led to the stairs—was open.

And standing in the doorway, blocking the path, was a shadow.

It was the Editor. He was holding a pair of scissors. And he was cutting the rope ladder.

He looked up at me, his face a mask of serene indifference.

"No landing," he called out, his voice carrying over the sound of the waves. "The story is a closed system. Visitors are a plot hole."

He snipped the last rung. The ladder clattered to the rocks.

The ships are coming. The shadow is waiting. And I am holding a burning quill at the top of the world, with nowhere left to run.

The light is burning. The ink is boiling.

The first foot is about to touch the shore.

I am
