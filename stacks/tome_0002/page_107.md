*CLINK.*

The vibration rattled my bones. The glass walls of my prison hummed.

I pushed myself up from the bottom of the bottle. My movements were sluggish, weighted by the thickness of the air inside. There was no air here, only the stale exhalation of a memory. I looked at my hands. They were translucent. I could see the wood grain of the desk through my own palms.

I was fading. The "Reality" the Reader promised was not solidity; it was the realization of being small.

"Elias!"

The voice was faint, carried on the draft that leaked through the seal of the cork. It wasn't the Reader. It wasn't the Captain.

It was the Old Man. The First Draft.

I pressed my face against the glass. Outside, resting on the vast, flat expanse of the desk, was the paperweight. The Lighthouse. It was barely taller than the bottle, a tiny, detailed monument. Standing at its base, no larger than an ant, was a figure in a deep water robe.

"The eye is closing!" the First Draft shouted, though I heard him as if he were speaking directly into my thoughts. "The Dreamer is losing focus! If we fall into the coffee, we dissolve!"

I looked up. The Eye—the colossal, bloodshot eye of the god who dreamed me—was indeed fluttering. The eyelids were vast, fleshy curtains scraping against the sky of the ceiling. The light in the room was dimming, shifting from the harsh white of attention to the blurry orange of dusk.

"He is falling asleep," I realized. "The story is pausing."

"If he sleeps, the dream recedes," the First Draft yelled. "The coffee gets cold! You have to pop the cork!"

"I have no leverage!" I screamed back. I beat my fists against the glass. It was futile. I was a ghost in a jar.

"You have the weapon!"

The weapon.

I reached into my pocket. My fingers closed around the cold, dense shape of the leaden box from the Keep. I had forgotten it was there. I pried the lid open with a thumbnail.

Inside, the ivory Colon key sat on its bed of black velvet.

"A semantic breaching charge," the Reader had called it. But I wasn't trying to breach a plot anymore. I was trying to breach the physics of a bottle.

I gripped the key between my thumb and forefinger. It felt heavy, heavier than the box, heavier than the Old Man’s cane. It had the weight of grammar. It had the weight of *connection*.

I looked for a seam in the glass. There was none. The bottle was smooth, perfect, blown by a master glassblower in a city I would never visit.

"You don't attack the glass," I whispered to myself. "You attack the seal."

The cork. It was just organic matter. Just compressed bark.

I raised the key. I didn't strike. I didn't have the room for a swing.

I pressed the tip of the ivory key against the inner side of the cork.

"Separate," I hissed. I pushed. I pushed with the weight of every unwritten word, with the gravity of the unspoken love for the woman in the photograph.

The key flared. A sudden, blinding heat.

The cork didn't pop. It didn't fly off.

It *sublimated*.

It turned from solid wood into a cloud of red dust, instantly, violently. The pressure difference equalized in a fraction of a second.

*WHOOOOSH.*

I was blasted out of the bottle neck, riding a jet of pressurized air. I tumbled into the vastness of the desk, rolling across the polished oak which felt like a frozen lake. I skidded to a stop inches from the precipice of the desk's edge.

Below me was the carpet. The Persian rug. From this height, the intricate patterns looked like a sprawling, red jungle. I could get lost in those fibers. I could be eaten by the moths.

I scrambled to my feet. The air out here smelled of old paper and dust. It was the smell of a library that hadn't been visited in decades.

"Elias!"

The First Draft was standing by the tiny Lighthouse. He was waving his cane.

"Up here!" he pointed. "The Lamp Room!"

I looked at the paperweight. It was exquisite. Every brick, every window of the Lighthouse was rendered in perfect detail. It was exactly as I remembered it, right down to the rust on the railing.

But it was empty.

"She is not in there," I said. "The Captain. She was on the beach."

"She is in the coffee," the Old Man said grimly. "Look."

I crept to the edge of the ceramic mug. The surface of the liquid was dark, reflecting the dimming room lights. Floating in the center of the brown swirl was a woman.

She was giant to me, but tiny to the room. She was treading water, her coat of feathers heavy with the liquid. She was surrounded by the rusted hulks of the armada—now just flakes of rust, dissolving in the acidity of the brew.

"She is drowning," I said.

"She is the conflict," the Old Man said. "Conflict dissolves in comfort. The coffee represents the mundane. The safety of the everyday. It kills the story."

"We have to save her."

"We have to save the story," the Old Man corrected. "And to do that, we have to wake the Dreamer."

The eyelids above were almost closed. A slit of white remained, like a crescent moon.

"The Reader said the Dreamer wants a coffee," I said. "But he's not drinking it. He's falling asleep with it on the desk."

"Procrastination," the Old Man spat. "The greatest killer of art."

"If he spills it," I said, looking at the precarious mug, "we all die."

"Then we make him drink," the Old Man said.

"How?"

I looked around the vast landscape of the desk. There were mountains of books. A valley of crumpled tissues. A canyon formed by a fallen pen.

And then I saw it.

The Lighthouse paperweight. It had a mechanism.

I ran to it. I placed my hand on the tiny glass door at the base. To the Dreamer, this was a toy. To me, it was a cathedral.

"The bulb," I said. "It’s not connected to power. It’s just glass."

"But it has a switch," the Old Man said, pointing to the tiny lever on the side of the paperweight.

I climbed up the side of the miniature Lighthouse, digging my boots into the mortar between the stone bricks. I reached the lamp room. The tiny glass bulb was dark.

"If I turn this on," I shouted down to the Old Man, "it won't be bright enough to wake him!"

"It's not about the light!" the Old Man yelled. "It's about the heat! The filament! Look inside the bulb!"

I squinted. Inside the tiny glass sphere, there was a filament. But it wasn't tungsten.

It was a word. A single, tiny word written in cursive gold filament, curled like a spring.

*Remember.*

"If the filament burns," the Old Man said, "the word illuminates. The Dreamer sees it in his peripheral vision. The word triggers the memory. The memory wakes the mind."

I gripped the tiny switch. It was stiff with rust.

"Elias," the Old Man whispered. "Do it. Before the lid closes."

I looked at the mug. The Captain was struggling. Her head slipped beneath the surface of the coffee.

I looked at the eye. The pupil was dilating, shifting into REM sleep.

I pushed the switch.

*CLICK.*

A spark.

Then, light.

Not a beam. Not a sun.

A tiny, pinpoint of piercing, white clarity. It cut through the gloom of the study. It hit the surface of the Eye.

The pupil contracted violently.

The giant hand, which had been retreating toward the comfort of the chair, froze.

The finger twitched.

The Dreamer was seeing it. He was seeing the light of the Lighthouse in the darkness of his study.

A sound rumbled through the room, vibrating through the floorboards and into the soles of my boots.

*GROAN.*

A sound of waking. A sound of distress. A sound of a mind dragged back from the precipice of sleep.

The hand moved.

It didn't go for the bottle. It didn't go for the mug.

It went for the Lighthouse.
