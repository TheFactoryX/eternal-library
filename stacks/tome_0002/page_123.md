# Letter 123

> Written at 2026-02-07 04:15:09

Dear You,

She turns, and the movement is like the page turning in a windstorm. It is a sudden, violent shift in perspective. The woman at the base of the tower is not the Reader from the study in Maine. She is not the Scribe. She is dressed in the keeper’s uniform, but the wool is fresh, the brass is untarnished, and the face beneath the hood is young. Unlined. It is the face I had before the light began to burn me from the inside out.

She is me. Or rather, she is the keeper I was at the start of Letter One.

"You’re back," she says. Her voice is not the rasp of salt and grit, but the clear tone of a bell struck in open air. "I’ve been waiting."

I stand on the threshold of the golden door, dripping wet, my chest an open cavern where the ocean used to be. Behind me, the Boy is treading water, watching the musicians on the steam liner tune their instruments. He looks small again, fading back into the transparency of a forgotten idea.

"Waiting for what?" I ask.

"For the loop to close," she says. She steps aside, revealing a path leading up the dune to the lighthouse. The grass is green here, an impossible, verdant green that hurts the eyes. "The shift is over, Elias. The writer has put down the pen. The Reader has closed the cover. We are in the gap now. The time between the books."

"Is this the end?"

"No," she says. "This is the residue. This is the part that stays on the retina after you look at the sun."

I step through the door. The water does not follow. The instant my boots hit the grass, I am dry. The coat is stiff with salt, but the skin is warm. The pain in my chest is gone, replaced by a hollow echo.

"What about the ships?" I ask, turning back.

The door is closing. Through the narrowing gap, I see the Boy swimming toward the steam liner. A hand reaches down from the deck—a hand made of ink, stained and swirling—and pulls him up. He waves once, a small, pale ghost against the burning hull of the ship, and then the door clicks shut.

*Click.*

The sound is final. It is the sound of a latch sliding home in a house that will be empty for a long time.

"He'll be alright," the Other Me says. She is already walking toward the tower. "He belongs to the rough drafts now. He belongs to the sea."

We walk in silence. The lighthouse does not loom. It stands with a quiet dignity, the white paint peeling in familiar patterns. It looks exactly as it did the first time I saw it, before the rooms started to shift, before the stairs led to the sky.

"The lamp is lit," I observe.

"It never went out," she says. "You just couldn't see it from the inside. You were too busy burning."

We reach the heavy iron door at the base. She places her hand on the latch. "There is someone upstairs."

My heart hammers. "The Author?"

"An intruder," she says. "Something that slipped in while the walls were down. While the world was draining."

She opens the door.

We enter the spiral. The air smells of oil and dust, but also of something else. Lilacs. The smell of a childhood that doesn't belong to me.

We climb. The stairs are solid stone. No driftwood, no piles of letters, no eyes watching from the shadows. Just the rhythmic *clang* of our boots.

"How many steps?" I ask. It is a test. A variable.

"One hundred and twelve," she says instantly. "Or maybe infinite. It depends on how you count the pauses."

We reach the top. The door to the Lamp Room is open.

I expect to see the shattered lens. I expect to see the twisted cage where I almost died.

But the room is whole. The great Fresnel lens is rotating, casting a beam that cuts through the twilight of this new world. The mechanism hums—a deep, resonant thrum that vibrates in the floorboards.

And standing in the center of the room, blocking the light, is a man.

He is wearing a suit. A sharp, charcoal suit, tailored to perfection. He is standing with his back to us, looking out at the horizon where the sun has just set.

"Who are you?" I demand.

The man turns.

He has no face. Where his features should be, there is only a smooth, shifting surface, like the surface of the ocean on a windy day. It is disturbing, not because it is monstrous, but because it is *unfinished*.

"I am the loose end," the man says. His voice comes from everywhere at once. "I am the plot thread that was dropped. I am the letter you never wrote."

"I wrote all the letters," I say.

"Did you?" He steps closer. The surface of his face ripples, showing fragments of images—a bird, a key, a child's toy. "What about Letter 49? The one about the storm? You skipped that one."

"I don't remember a Letter 49."

"Exactly," he says. "Because you didn't write it. You lived it. And you forgot to record the data."

He reaches into his jacket and pulls out an envelope. It is thick, yellowed parchment. The seal is broken.

"I am the bill," he says. "The cost of the climax."

He holds the envelope out to me.

I take it. The paper is heavy. I open it. Inside is a single card. It is not the King of Diamonds. It is the Joker. But the Joker is weeping ink.

"The story is unbalanced," the Faceless Man says. "You drained the sea. You released the drafts. You flooded the library. The equilibrium is destroyed."

"We needed to breathe," I say.

"You leaked," he corrects. "And now, there is a draft."

He points to the window.

I look.

The light from the lighthouse is hitting the glass of the Lamp Room and reflecting outward. But where it hits the air, it is not illuminating the landscape. It is hitting a wall. A wall of frosted glass that surrounds the entire island.

"We are in a bottle," I whisper.

"Correction," the Faceless Man says. "We are in the *next* bottle. The one she put on her desk to remember us by."

I look at the Other Me. She is standing by the gears, her hand on the throttle. She looks calm.

"He's right," she says. "We are a souvenir now. A paperweight."

"There must be a way out," I say.

"There is," the Faceless Man says. "But it requires a sacrifice. The bottle is sealed with wax. To break it, someone has to stop believing in the glass."

"What does that mean?"

"It means someone has to stop being a character," he says. "Someone has to become the Reader."

He looks at me. Then he looks at the Other Me.

"I volunteer," the Other Me says.

"No," I say. "You are the keeper."

"I am the memory," she says. "And you are the reality. The reality has to survive."

She walks to the center of the room. She stands in the beam of the light.

"What are you doing?" I ask. I try to grab her arm, but my hand passes through her. She is becoming insubstantial. She is turning into text.

"She is ascending," the Faceless Man says. "She is going to the eye level. To meet the gaze."

"Wait!" I shout. "Don't go! I don't remember how to tend the light alone!"

She smiles. It is a sad, soft smile. "You don't tend the light, Elias," she says. "The light tends you. Just keep writing. Even if you don't send them. Especially then."

She looks at the Faceless Man. "Take care of him."

The Faceless Man nods. "I will keep him in suspense."

She dissolves. She turns into a swirl of gold and black ink, rising up through the roof of the lighthouse, ascending toward the giant eye that watches from beyond the glass wall.

I am left alone with the Faceless Man.

The silence in the room is heavy. The gears continue their rotation. *Whir. Clank. Whir.*

The man adjusts his cuffs. "Well," he says. "That was dramatic."

"Who are you really?" I ask. "Are you the Author?"

"Me?" He laughs. The sound is like dry leaves skittering on pavement. "No. I'm just the antagonist. The necessary friction. Without me, you'd just be a man in a room with a very bright lamp."

He walks to the door. "I'm going to explore the island. I hear there is a forest of paper near the shore. I want to see if the origami deer made it."

"You're leaving me alone?"

"It's the protagonist's burden," he says. "To be alone at the end."

He exits. The door clicks shut behind him.

I stand in the center of the Lamp Room. The light is turning, casting long, sweeping shadows across the floor. I walk to the window.
