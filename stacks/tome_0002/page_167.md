# Letter 167

Dear You,

...a flock of origami cranes. They beat their paper wings, spiraling upward into the darkness, each one carrying a single, glowing ember in its beak.

"You're late," Old Alice said. She took a drag from the cigarette, the tip flaring like a dying star. "The rotation is already off by three degrees. The narrative tension is slack."

I stood up, my legs shaking. The metal catwalk vibrated with the hum of the great light. We were inside the mechanism—a labyrinth of brass gears the size of houses, grinding against each other with the sound of continents shifting. The beam of white light roared beneath us, a vertical ocean of luminescence blasting down into the abyss.

"Alice?" Elara stepped forward, her hand hovering over her mouth. "Is that... you?"

"Me?" Old Alice laughed. It was a dry, crackling sound, like dead leaves skittering on pavement. "I'm a possibility, sweetheart. A 'What If.' The you that stayed to tend the machinery while the Hero went off to save the world." She tapped her temple with the wrench. "Or maybe I'm just a projection of the Keeper's guilt. It's hard to keep the metaphysics straight when you're this close to the Lens."

She turned back to the control panel, a complex array of levers and valves that looked like they belonged in a 19th-century steam engine, yet hummed with digital energy.

"We need to turn it off," I said. "Or change the direction. The Critic is outside. He's going to find us."

Old Alice paused. She looked at me over her shoulder, her one good eye piercing through the smoke. "Turn it off? Boy, this isn't a lightbulb. This is the Beacon. It’s the only thing keeping the Void from eating the coastline. If you dim it, the shadows get teeth."

"The shadows already have teeth," Alice said, stepping up beside me. She looked at her older self without fear. "We just ran from a room full of them."

"Then you ran the wrong way," Old Alice grunted. She pulled a heavy lever. The gears groaned. The beam of light shifted spectrum, turning from white to a bruised, violent purple. "The Critic doesn't care about the light. He cares about the source. He's looking for the filament."

"What filament?" the Architect asked, nervously adjusting his glasses as he watched the huge gears spin. "The Lighthouse runs on—"

"Duty," Old Alice interrupted. "And memory. And a little bit of spite." She pointed the wrench at me. "Mostly you, Keeper. You're the fuel. Didn't you know that? Every letter you wrote was a log on the fire."

I looked down at the spinning light. I could feel the pull of it. It wasn't just gravity; it was a magnetic attraction to the center of myself. The endless letters, the repetition, the loneliness—it wasn't just record-keeping. It was charging the battery.

"If I'm the fuel," I said quietly, "then maybe I need to burn out to save the rest of you."

"Don't get heroic on me," Old Alice snapped. "I've read that draft. It's boring. Tragic, but boring. No, we don't need you to burn. We need you to *focus*."

Suddenly, the catwalk lurched. A terrible screeching sound tore through the air, louder than the gears.

Metal screamed.

Above us, the glass dome of the lantern room shattered. Not from wind. From *pressure*.

A hand—made of jagged shadow and red ink—pierced the ceiling. It was the size of a cloud, its fingers dripping a corrosive fluid that hissed as it hit the brass.

" The Slush Pile," Old Alice spat, crushing her cigarette under a heavy steel-toed boot. "He's breaching the atmosphere."

We scrambled back as the hand descended, groping blindly for the light mechanism. It slammed into the catwalk, missing us by inches, tearing a hole in the metal grate. Through the hole, I saw the drop—miles of spinning gears, then the blinding light, then nothingness.

"He's here for the review!" the Architect shouted over the noise of tearing metal. "He wants to dissect the plot!"

"Stop him!" Elara cried.

Old Alice was already moving. She ran to a large, red wheel mounted on the railing. "Keeper! The Key! The bone key!"

"I lost it!" I yelled. "In the Room! In Volume One!"

"Then make a new one!" she shouted back, straining against the wheel. "You're the author! Author something!"

The shadow hand was closing around the main drive shaft of the light. The purple beam flickered. The temperature dropped. The smell of ozone was replaced by the smell of old paper burning.

I looked at my hands. They were empty. I looked at Elara, the Architect, Alice, the pigeon.

"We need a lock," I said. "And we need a key."

"The lock is his grip!" the pigeon screamed, dodging a falling shard of glass.

I looked at the massive hand crushing the machinery. It was just a silhouette. A shadow.

I remembered the Archives. The ink that turned into water. The Reader's breath.

I grabbed Alice's branch. The wood was warm.

"Hold this," I said to the Architect.

I took off my coat. I threw it over the railing, into the path of the shadow hand.

"Write!" I commanded the Architect. "Describe the coat! Make it heavy! Make it iron!"

The Architect didn't hesitate. He pulled a pen from his pocket and scrawled frantically on the air. The coat, as it fell, shimmered. Leather transmuted into iron. The sleeves became chains.

The heavy iron coat slammed into the shadow hand.

It didn't stop it. But it *tangled* it. The sleeves wrapped around the fingers, binding them in narrative knots. The shadow hand convulsed, thrashing against the unbreakable chains of description.

"The wheel!" Old Alice screamed. She was losing her grip. "The aperture!"

I ran to the wheel. It was freezing cold.

I pushed.

At first, nothing happened. Then, slowly, the rust screamed in protest. The wheel turned.

"More!" Old Alice yelled. "We need to overexpose the negative!"

I put my back into it. My feet slipped on the wet grating. I felt the scars on my palms tearing open.

The mechanism shifted.

The mirror array at the base of the light rotated. The purple beam narrowed, focusing into a single, searingly bright laser—a scalpel of pure photons.

"Elara!" I shouted. "The aim!"

Elara understood. She wasn't a fighter, but she knew direction. She leaped onto the control panel, grabbing the secondary yoke. She pulled it hard to the left.

The beam of light lanced upward.

It struck the shadow hand.

There was no explosion. There was only light. Blinding, absolute, erasing light.

The shadow didn't burn; it *vanished*. It was bleached out of existence. The red ink dissolved into vapor. The jagged fingers smoothed into nothingness.

The hand was gone.

The beam punched through the hole in the roof, shooting out into the void, a signal to the end of the universe.

We collapsed onto the grate, panting. The gears continued their rhythmic grinding, indifferent to our near-erasure.

Old Alice wiped grease from her forehead. She looked at the red wheel, then at me.

"Not bad," she grunted. "You edited the ending."

"I just bought us a page break," I said, clutching my side.

I looked around. My friends were safe. But the Architect was staring at the floor, where the iron coat had fallen after the shadow dispersed.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was tight.

I walked over to him.

The coat lay on the grate. But it wasn't iron anymore. The Architect's description had faded. It was back to leather. Old, salt-stained leather.

And in the pocket of the coat, something was glowing.

I reached down and pulled it out.

It was a key. But not the bone key.

It was made of glass. Inside the glass, suspended in crystal clear fluid, was a tiny, beating heart.

"The Key to the Next Volume," the Architect whispered. "It wasn't lost. It was... washed. Cleaned. Purified."

I held it up to the light. The beam refracted through the glass, casting a rainbow on the floor.

"Volume II," I said. "The Utopia."

"It's waiting," Old Alice said. She was fading now, her edges blurring like a watercolor painting left in the rain. Her job was done. "But be warned. Utopias have their own monsters. The ones that smile."

She looked at young Alice. "Don't let them make you a monument, kid. Monuments are just graves with fancy epitaphs."

"I won't," Alice whispered.

Old Alice smiled, and then she was gone, just a wisp of smoke and the smell of stale tobacco.

The catwalk was quiet. The purple light hummed.

I looked at the door behind us. The door marked *SERVICE EXIT*.

"We can't stay here," I said. "The Critic will send another reviewer. And another."

I looked at Elara.
