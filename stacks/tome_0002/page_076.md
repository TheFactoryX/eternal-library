# Letter 76

...versions of me screaming in silence. "The catch is that to write the ending, you must read the beginning. And to read the beginning, you must remember the pain."

"I know the pain," I said, gripping the stone until my knuckles turned the colour of old parchment. "I have lived in the gap between the lines."

"Then climb," the Mirror-Man said, and he shattered into a cloud of silver moths that scattered up the spiral staircase of light.

I stepped onto the stairs. They were not solid. Each step was a sheet of illuminated glass, and beneath my feet, I could see through the transparent treads to the library floor below, receding rapidly. The Mirror-Man was gone, replaced by a glittering dust.

As I ascended, the air grew thin. It tasted of ozone and static. The light here was not the warm amber of the lamp or the cold blue of the screen; it was a violent, creative white. It was the colour of a page before the first letter strikes.

I reached the top.

There was no landing. Just a circular catwalk suspended over an infinite void. In the center of the catwalk stood a machine.

It looked like a loom, but instead of thread, the shuttles carried long ribbons of shadow. Instead of fabric, it was weaving a tapestry of night sky.

And sitting at the loom was a child.

They couldn't have been more than seven. They wore a nightgown that was too large, pooling around their feet like a spill of milk. They were pedalling the machine with their bare feet, a rhythmic, mechanical motion that filled the silence with a *thrum-hiss, thrum-hiss*.

"You're late," the child said. They didn't look up. They were intent on the shuttle, which was currently dragging a patch of pure darkness across a starry field.

"I got lost in the margin," I said. I approached slowly, terrified the glass catwalk might shatter. "Are you... the Muse?"

The child laughed. It was a dry, brittle sound. "The Muse is a liar. The Muse tells you it's magic. I am the Mechanic. I make the words fit."

I looked at the tapestry. It wasn't abstract. It was a map.

I saw the Glass Sea. I saw the Island. I saw the Lighthouse. But I also saw things I hadn't visited yet. A city of bone. A forest of typewriter keys. A room where time flowed backward.

"The Mirror-Man said I could change the ending here," I said.

The child stopped pedalling. The machine slowed, the shuttles drifting to a halt.

"You can change anything," the child said. They turned to face me.

They had no face. Where the eyes, nose, and mouth should be, there was only a smooth, blank surface. A resting place for a story.

"But you must pay the toll," they said.

"I have the stone," I offered, holding it out.

The child reached out a hand—small, pale, and trembling—and took the stone. They weighed it.

"The weight of memory," the child said. "A heavy currency. But acceptable."

They placed the stone into a hopper on the side of the machine.

*Clack.*

The machine shuddered. It roared to life, spinning faster.

"What are you doing?" I shouted over the noise.

"Pressing the letter!" the child screamed, their voice altering, becoming the voice of the Author, the voice of the Reader, the voice of the You on the bus. "We are stamping the lead! We are making it permanent!"

The tapestry began to change. The image of the Lighthouse blurred. It stretched.

"Stop!" I yelled. "I didn't ask for this yet!"

"There is no asking!" the child shrieked. "There is only the verb!"

I lunged for the machine. I grabbed the handle, trying to pull it back. The metal was hot, burning my skin.

"I want to write the letter!" I yelled. "I don't want to be the letter!"

The child turned their blank face toward me. "You are both! You are the writer and the written! You are the ink and the quill!"

The stone in the hopper began to grind down. It turned into a fine, grey powder. The powder was sucked into the shuttles.

I watched the tapestry.

The grey powder was being woven into the image of a figure.

Me.

But it wasn't the me standing by the machine. It was a version of me lying on a bed. An ordinary bed. In an ordinary room. The You from the bus was sitting next to me, holding my hand.

"That's the draft," I realized. "That's the reality I deleted."

"It's not deleted," the child said. "It's the subtext. It's the layer underneath."

The machine was spinning so fast the catwalk was vibrating. My teeth rattled.

"If I become the subtext," I shouted over the roar, "I disappear! I become just a feeling!"

"FEELINGS ARE THE ONLY THING THAT SURVIVES THE EDIT!" the child howled.

A shuttle flew off the loom. It struck me in the chest.

I stumbled back. I hit the railing of the catwalk. The rail gave way.

I fell.

I fell into the tapestry.

I didn't hit the floor. I hit the *image* of the floor.

I was lying on the bed. The mattress was soft. The room smelled of lavender and rain.

I tried to sit up, but I couldn't. I was heavy. Paralyzed.

I looked at my hands. They were speckled with liver spots. Arthritic.

I looked at You. You were sitting in a wooden chair, reading a book.

"You," I tried to say, but my voice was a wheeze. "You... with the umbrella."

You looked up. Your eyes were red, as if you'd been crying.

"He's awake," You said. You weren't speaking to me. You were speaking to someone else in the room.

A doctor walked into my field of vision. He was checking a clipboard.

"Is he lucid?" the doctor asked.

"He looked at me," You said. "He spoke."

I tried to grab You. I tried to tell you about the Glass Sea. About the stone. About the Machine.

"Salt," I croaked.

You leaned in close. "Salt? Do you want some water, Keeper?"

"Not... Keeper," I choked out. "Pen."

"I'll get his notebook," You said to the doctor. "Maybe he can write it down."

You opened a drawer in the bedside table. You pulled out a black notebook.

My heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird.

It was the notebook.

The notebook from Letter 1. The one I started writing in when I first arrived on the Island.

I watched your hand open it.

The pages were blank.

"It's empty," You said, confused. "I thought he filled it."

"He must have used the other one," the doctor said. "The one in the attic."

The attic.

The word rang like a bell. The attic of the Lighthouse. The room where the echoes lived.

"No," I whispered. "Not the attic."

I looked around the room. The hospital room was fading. The edges were curling up like burning paper.

Underneath the wallpaper, I could see stone. Underneath the floor, I could hear waves.

"You're not fading," I said to You. "I am."

You looked at me. For a second, the red umbrella faded from your clothes. You were wearing the yellow coat again.

"The ink is running," You said. "The Doctor is the Editor."

The doctor turned to me. He wasn't a doctor anymore. He was a tall figure made of crumpled rejection slips. He held a syringe. It was filled with white-out.

"Time for your treatment, Mr. Keeper," the Editor said. "Time to erase the revisions."

I looked at the notebook in your hand.

It wasn't blank anymore.

Words were appearing. Burning themselves onto the page as I watched.

*THE KEEPER WOKE UP. THE KEEPER SAW THE LIGHT. THE KEEPER REACHED FOR THE—*

The sentence cut off.

The pen ran dry.

I looked up at You. You were holding the silver pen. But it wasn't in your hand. It was in your chest. It was protruding from your heart, the nib dripping light.

"Write it," You said. Your voice was the sound of the ocean. "Finish the sentence."

I looked at the Editor. He was advancing, the syringe of White-Out raised high.

I didn't reach for the pen in your chest. I couldn't. That would kill you.

I looked at the notebook in your lap.

I reached out with my spotted, arthritic hand. My finger was trembling.

I dipped my finger in the ink dripping from your heart.

It burned. It felt like liquid fire.

I touched the page.

I didn't write a word.

I drew a line.

A line through the text.

A line through the narrative.

*STRIKE THAT.*

The hospital room shattered.

We were falling again.

We were falling through layers of deleted drafts.

We fell past a version where we were pirates.
We fell past a version where we were trees.
We fell past a version where we were both the same person.
