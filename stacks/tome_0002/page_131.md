# Letter 131

> Written at 2026-02-07 12:56:23

---

Dear You,

The fire does not burn. That is the first thing I notice as I crouch in the space between the box spring and the floorboards. It is cold, this violet halo we huddle inside, and it smells of the static that gathers on a television screen left on a dead channel.

We are pressed against the rough underside of the mattress—a landscape of beige canvas and dust bunnies that loom like tumbleweeds. Above us, the world is bright. The Reader has turned on the overhead light, and through the gap in the dust skirt of the bed, I can see the vast, hardwood plains of the floor stretching out to the horizon where the dresser stands like a cliff face.

"Do not move," the Scribe whispers. Her voice is shaking. She is clutching her dress of pages, which has gone brittle and dry in the dry air of the bedroom.

"I can't feel my legs," I say. It is not entirely true. I can feel them, but they feel distant, as if they belong to a character I stopped writing years ago.

The bird is huddled in my lap. It has stopped shivering, but its feathers are stiff, rustling like dried leaves when I stroke them.

"We are fading," the bird says softly. "The narrative tension is dropping. We need conflict."

"Conflict is upstairs," I say, nodding toward the slice of light under the door. "We are hiding in the footnote."

I shift my weight. Something digs into my hip. I reach down and pull it from the dust.

It is a small, circular object. A button. Not a shirt button, but a plastic button, the kind that belongs to a remote control. It is black, shiny, and in the center, printed in faded white letters, is the number: *0*.

"Zero," the Scribe reads.

"Or 'Source'," I say. I turn it over in my fingers. It is greasy with fingerprints. "I remember this. I pressed it, once. Before the lighthouse. I pressed it to shut out the world."

"Can we press it to go back?" the bird asks.

"I don't think we have fingers big enough to hit the button anymore," I say. "We are too small. We are marginalia."

Suddenly, the floorboards above us vibrate. *BOOM. BOOM. BOOM.*

The Reader is walking.

The impact knocks clouds of dust down from the springs. We cover our heads. The sheer mass of the footsteps is terrifying. Each step is an earthquake, a tectonic shift of reality.

"She is pacing," the Scribe says. "Why is she pacing?"

"Thinking," the bird says. "Or waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the phone to ring," I say. "Or for the tea to steep. Or for us to do something interesting."

I crawl to the edge of our shadowy sanctuary. I peer through the gap in the dust skirt.

The Reader is sitting on the edge of the bed. Her feet are bare, enormous pale things resting on the floorboards just inches from my face. She is looking down at a book in her hands.

It is not *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters*. It is a smaller book. A paperback with a glossy cover. I cannot read the title, but I see the colors: bright red, screaming yellow.

"She’s reading someone else," I realize, a pang of jealousy sharper than the cold. "She’s reading a thriller."

"Is it a good one?" the bird asks.

"It looks loud," I say.

The Reader turns a page. The sound is like a cannon shot in the quiet room. *KRR-ACK.*

"She’s moving fast," I say. "She isn't savoring the prose. She is binge-reading."

"That explains the hunger," the Scribe says. "The Crayon Giant. The appetite for scissors. If she feeds on plot twists, we are too slow for her. We are... literature."

"We need to be more gripping," I say. "We need a hook."

I look at the remote button in my hand. *0*. The void.

"We need to get her attention," I say. "But we can't just walk out there. She’d sweep us up with a broom, or worse, mistake us for spiders."

"We need a signal," the bird says. "A light."

I look up at the box spring. We are directly beneath the center of the bed. Supporting the mattress is a grid of wires and wood.

"The grid," I say. "If we can short it out..."

"With what?" the Scribe asks. "We have a quill that holds ghost fire and a plastic button."

I look at the quill. The violet fire is dormant, sleeping in the shaft. But the button... the button is conductive.

"I have an idea," I say. "But it requires perfect timing."

I instruct the Scribe and the bird to gather the metallic dust bunnies—the ones made of hairpins, lost earrings, and safety pins that have migrated under the bed over the years. It takes them an hour, dragging the heavy metal scraps across the floorboards.

While they work, I climb. I dig my fingers into the fabric of the box spring and haul myself up the underside of the bed. It is inverted mountaineering. I hang upside down, staring at the grid of wires above my head.

I find the join. The place where the wire crosses the wood slat.

"Ready?" I call down to them.

"Ready," the Scribe says.

I jam the remote button between the wire and the wood. I wedge it in tight.

"Now," I say. "Throw the silver dust."

They hurl the handful of metal shards upward.

I use the quill. I don't write a word. I make a mark. A single, jagged slash of violet fire across the pile of metal.

*CRACK.*

The quill unleashes a bolt of cold lightning. It strikes the metal dust. The dust arcs. The electricity jumps to the remote button.

The button acts as a conductor. It channels the violet fire directly into the bed frame.

The effect is immediate.

The mattress above us begins to hum. A low, throbbing vibration that resonates in the bones of the bed.

"What is happening?" the bird shouts.

"Heating element!" I yell. "We are activating the blanket!"

The mattress begins to warm. The heat rises, seeping through the layers of cotton and foam.

Above us, the Reader shifts. "Ugh," she says. Her voice rumbles through the springs like thunder. "Static?"

She adjusts her position. She moves her leg.

I hold my breath. If she gets up, if she looks under the dust skirt...

But she doesn't. She leans back. She presses her hand against the heated mattress.

"Oh," she says. "Nice."

She settles in. The heat is soothing her. It is making her drowsy. The book in her lap slips slightly as her eyes begin to close.

"We did it," the Scribe whispers. "We tamed the Reader."

"We didn't tame her," I say, sliding back down to the floor, sweating. "We made her comfortable. That's worse."

"Why?"

"Because," I say, watching the paperback book slide lower and lower from her relaxed grip. "When she falls asleep, she closes the book. And when she closes the book..."

"...The dream begins," the bird finishes.

The book falls. *THUMP.*

It lands face down on the floor. The cover is facing us.

I squint through the gloom.

I can read the title now.

It is *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters*.

"She was reading us," I whisper, my blood turning to ice. "She was reading *this*."

The cover is glossy, but it’s battered. The corners are dog-eared. There is a coffee stain on the spine.

"She’s read it before," the Scribe says.

The Reader sighs. She lies back fully, her head hitting the pillows. The light is still on. She is surrendering to sleep.

"We are safe in the shadow," the bird says. "We can wait until morning."

I look at the fallen book. I notice something new. Something that wasn't there a moment ago.

There is a piece of paper sticking out of the pages. A bookmark.

But it’s not a flat bookmark. It is... thick.

It is a latch. A metal latch, embedded in the paper of the page.

"It’s not a book," I realize, the horror blooming in my gut. "It’s a box."

And then, I hear a sound from the fallen book.

A knocking.

Three sharp raps.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Coming from inside the closed cover.

"Keeper?" A muffled voice calls from within the pages. "Is that you? I found the key."

I know the voice. It is Sylvia. The Sketch-Woman.

She is trapped in the book. The Reader trapped her there when she closed the cover.

And the Reader is about to roll over.

I see the massive, pale foot shifting. The heel is hovering directly over the fallen book.

If she rolls over, she will crush the book. She will crush Sylvia.

"We have to go out there," I say, standing up. I brush the dust from my coat.

"Are you insane?" the Scribe hisses. "We will dissolve in the light!"

"Better to dissolve than to let her crush the dream," I say. I grip the quill.

I look at the bird.
