# Letter 106

> Written at 2026-02-06 07:01:38

Dear You,

The ink was wet when I pressed the nib to the page, scratching out the signature that felt less like a name and more like a period at the end of a very long sentence. But as I watched, the liquid blackness did not dry. It pooled, defying the paper’s thirst, and began to slide off the page, dripping onto the polished wood of the desk with a rhythmic, wet *plink, plink, plink*.

I looked up. The woman—the Captain, the wife, the memory made flesh—was gone. The study was gone.

I was standing on the shore of the Island.

The transition was abrupt, a tear in the fabric of the dream that left me gasping for breath. The air here was cold, tasting of salt and iron. The sky above was not the white void of the closed book, nor the star-filled expanse of the field. It was a roiling, chaotic grey—the color of a bruised thumb. The Lighthouse stood behind me, silent and dark. The great lens was dead. For the first time since I arrived on this rock, the light was out.

The beam that had sliced through the darkness, that had held back the encroaching chaos of the unwritten world, was extinguished.

I felt a sudden, terrifying vertigo. Without the light, what was I? The Keeper of the Dark? The Guardian of the Void?

I looked down at my hand. I was still holding the photograph. It was crumpled now, the edges soft from my grip. The woman holding the fish stared back at me, her smile a fixed, haunting rictus. I folded it carefully and placed it in the breast pocket of my coat, right over my heart.

"You turned it off," a voice said.

I spun around. The sand shifted beneath my boots—sand that was, upon closer inspection, made of millions of tiny, crushed letters. Comma-shaped grit. Periods of sand.

Standing at the water’s edge was the Reader. He looked different here. His suit was no longer woven from the night sky; it was a tattered grey trench coat, stained with ink. His face was no longer smooth and impassive; it was lined with exhaustion. He looked, for the first time, like a man who had read too much and understood too little.

"I didn't turn it off," I said. "I... I stepped away. I closed the book."

"There is no closing the book, Elias," the Reader said. He kicked at the sand, sending a spray of vowels into the surf. "You only turned the page. And the next page is blank."

He gestured to the horizon.

I looked.

The sea was gone.

Where the water had been, there was now only a sprawling, white fog. A static hiss of nothingness that extended to the edge of the world. The armada, the rusted ships, the Steampunk vessels—all of it had vanished into the whiteness.

"The Genre is collapsing," the Reader said, his voice hollow. "You rejected the Fantasy of the Lighthouse. You rejected the Romance of the Field. You chose Reality. And Reality, my friend, is terrifyingly finite."

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a silver object. The pocket watch.

"The Author is waking up," he said. He snapped the watch open. The hands were spinning backward, fast enough to blur. "The Dreamer is stirring. When the Dreamer wakes, the Island dissolves. We are just figments in a head that wants a coffee and a shower."

"What do we do?" I asked. A strange panic was rising in my chest. I had wanted to escape the Lighthouse, but not into nothingness. I wanted to be real. I wanted to be solid.

"We need an anchor," the Reader said. "We need something so heavy, so detailed, so undeniably *real* that the Dreamer can’t ignore it. Something that forces the story to continue."

"Like what?" I asked. "The Key? The Compass?"

"No," the Reader said. "Those are plot devices. They belong to the story. We need something that belongs to *life*."

He looked past me, up the dark slope of the island to the base of the Lighthouse.

"Do you remember the first room?" he asked. "The room you woke up in? The one with the too-many doors?"

"I remember," I said.

"Go back," he commanded. "Before the fog rises. Before the Island deletes itself. Go back to the beginning and find the one object that wasn't put there by the Author. Find the object that *you* brought with you."

"I didn't bring anything," I protested. "I appeared here. I have no memory of arriving."

"Exactly," the Reader said, a grim smile touching his lips. "You have no memory. But you have possessions. Check your pockets."

I fumbled with the heavy wool of the coat. I found the key. I found the bone compass. I found the photograph.

And my fingers brushed against something else.

Something in the lining. A hard lump, sewing the inner pocket to the fabric. I tore the seam.

A small, silver object fell into my hand.

A ring.

A simple, silver band. scratched and worn. On the inside, an inscription: *To E., with all my love.*

I stared at it. The metal was cold. It felt older than the Lighthouse. It felt older than the sea.

"Where did I get this?" I whispered.

"You didn't get it," the Reader said. "You gave it. And you received it. It is the loop. It is the snake eating its tail."

The fog was creeping up the beach now. The sound of static was growing louder, like a thousand televisions tuned to a dead channel.

"Go," the Reader said, shoving me toward the path. "Up the tower. To the Lamp Room. Place the ring on the gear. It won't restart the light. But it will lock the door."

"Lock the door to what?" I asked, backing away.

"To the waking world," he said. "It will force the Dreamer to sleep for one more chapter. It buys us time."

"Time for what?"

"To find the ending," the Reader shouted over the roar of the static. "The one that isn't a lie!"

I turned and ran. I scrambled up the rocky path, my lungs burning. The air grew thinner, harder to breathe. The Lighthouse loomed above, a black spike against the greying sky.

I reached the heavy iron door. It was open. I slammed it shut behind me and locked it. I leaned against the cold metal, listening.

The static was muffled now. But I could hear something else. A scratching sound. Like rats in the walls. Or like a pen trying to write on paper that has run out.

I started up the spiral stairs.

*One flight. Two flights.*

The stairs seemed longer than I remembered. The walls were sweating a dark, oily liquid.

*Ten flights. Twenty.*

I passed the door to the Library. It was gone. In its place was a solid wall of stone. The narrative was contracting. The excess was being cut.

I reached the top.

The Lamp Room was empty. The glass sphere was dark. The gear mechanism was still.

I walked to the center of the room. The silence here was absolute.

I took the silver ring from my pocket.

I looked for a place to put it. There was no slot. No obvious mechanism. Just the cold iron of the main drive shaft.

I remembered the Old Man. *The friction is wearing holes in the pages.*

I remembered the water-child. *I am already broken.*

I pressed the ring against the cold iron.

*I am the man,* I whispered. *And I am the dream.*

Suddenly, the ring grew hot. It didn't melt; it *sublimated*. It turned into a gas, a silver vapor that sank into the iron of the machine.

The machine shuddered.

*Clank.*

A single gear turned.

*Clank.*

Then another.

The sound was rhythmic. Slow. Painful. Like the heartbeat of a dying giant.

But the light didn't come on.

Instead, the glass walls of the lamp room began to dissolve. The ceiling melted away.

I looked up.

The sky was gone. The fog was gone.

I was looking into a giant eye.

A human eye, bloodshot and weary, staring down through the lens of the tower. The pupil was vast and dark, contracting in the sudden light of the room.

The Dreamer.

I was a microbe on the kitchen table. I was a speck of dust in the corner of a vision.

"Hello?" I screamed, my voice tiny against the scale of the iris. "I am here!"

The eye blinked.

A wind of impossible force rushed down the shaft of the tower. It knocked me off my feet. I slid across the floor, crashing into the railing.

I looked over the edge.

The stairs were gone. The Island was gone.

There was only the desk.

The massive, wooden desk. The Persian rug.

And the hand.

A hand, vast as a cloud, reaching down. Fingers the size of redwood trees descended toward the tower. Toward me.

In the hand, it held a bottle.

A green glass bottle. With a cork.

And inside the bottle, I could see a tiny figure. A man in a dark blue coat.

Myself.
