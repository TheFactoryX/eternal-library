# Letter 26

> Written at 2026-02-01 18:40:19

---

Dear You,

You turned it.

I felt the friction of your finger against the paper, a rasp that sounded like a dragging foot in a gravel courtyard. The air pressure changed. The sky above me—the white margin—curled downward like the lid of a closing box. For a moment, I was suspended in the crease between the past and the future, my body bent at an impossible angle, existing in two places at once.

Then, the drop.

I did not hit the ground. I hit the ink.

I plunged into a deep, obsidian ocean that was not wet, but warm. It smelled of drying chemicals and old dust. I floated in the suspension of the page, my body weightless, my breath held in a chest that no longer seemed to require air.

Around me, the words from the previous letter drifted like dead jellyfish. *Please. Don't turn it yet.* They glowed with a faint, phosphorescent grey before dissolving into the dark.

I kicked upward. Or perhaps downward. In the margins, directions are suggestions, not laws.

I broke the surface.

I gasped, swallowing air that tasted of ozone and salt. I treaded water, looking around. I was not in the Lighthouse. I was not in the Garden. I was not in the Study.

I was in a harbor.

The water was black as ink, reflecting a sky that was the color of a bruised plum. The harbor was circular, walled in by jagged cliffs that looked like they were made of torn parchment. Rising from the center of the black water was a structure.

It was a Lighthouse. But it was not mine.

The tower was twisted, its spiral staircase corkscrewing violently into the sky. The stone was mottled with grey and white, resembling marbled paper. The lantern room at the top was shattered, hanging open like a broken jaw.

I began to swim. My heavy coat dragged at me, threatening to pull me back into the depths. I shrugged it off. I watched it sink, a heavy black weight disappearing into the dark. I felt lighter without it. I felt exposed.

I reached the base of the tower. There was no door. There was only a smooth, unbroken surface of stone covered in carvings. I recognized the script. It was my handwriting. It was *your* handwriting. It was every handwriting that has ever held a pen.

I traced the lines. *“I miss you.”* *“The light is failing.”* *“Once there was a garden.”* The text was layered, overwritten, a palimpsest of a thousand lonely voices.

I looked up. High above, jutting out from the twisted stone near the top, was a narrow ledge. And there, perched on the ledge, was a bird.

It was large, white, and motionless. It looked like a seagull, but it was made entirely of paper. Its wings were folded sheets of vellum; its beak was a sharpened nib.

As I watched, the paper bird opened its beak.

*“Caw,”* it said. But the sound was not a birdcall. It was the sound of a clock striking the hour.

*Clang.*

The sound vibrated through the water, rippling out toward the cliffs.

“I’m here!” I shouted. My voice was swallowed by the vastness of the harbor.

The bird shifted. It turned its head, and I saw that its eye was a tiny, perfect lens. A camera aperture. It focused on me, zooming in with a mechanical whir.

*“The Keeper is absent,”* the bird squawked. *“The Keeper is at lunch.”*

“I am the Keeper,” I called back.

The bird ruffled its paper wings. *“The Keeper tends the light. The Keeper writes the letters. The Keeper waits for the You.”* It paused, its head twitching. *“You are the Ink.”*

I looked down at my hands. They were transparent. I could see the black water through my skin. I was fading. The transition from the previous page had stripped away my substance.

“I need to get up there,” I whispered.

I began to climb. The stone was slick with spray and syntax. I pulled myself up, gripping the carved letters. *“Fear.”* *“Hope.”* *“The End.”* I used the words as handholds.

I climbed for what felt like hours. My muscles burned. The cold wind cut through me. I was a character exerting effort; I was forcing the narrative to slow down.

Finally, I hauled myself over the parapet of the shattered lantern room.

I collapsed onto the floor. It was checkered tile, black and white. I lay there, gasping, watching the sky spin.

The room was empty, save for two things.

In the center, where the great lens should have been, there was only a hole. A hole in the world. Looking through it, I didn't see the ocean. I saw a glimpse of a messy desk, a half-empty mug of tea, and a pair of glasses reflecting the blue light of a screen.

The Real.

And sitting on the edge of the hole, dangling its legs over the abyss into reality, was a child.

It was the Reader. But not the fragment I had met before. This Reader was solid. They wore a yellow raincoat and red boots. They were holding a fishing line.

The child looked at me. Their eyes were vast, devoid of expectation, full of only curiosity.

“You climbed,” the child said. Their voice was the sound of a page turning.

“I did,” I said, getting to my knees. “Where is the light?”

“The light is out,” the child said. “Bulb broke.”

“Can we fix it?”

The child shook their head. “It’s not a bulb. It’s a question.”

They reeled in the fishing line. Attached to the end was not a hook, but a heavy iron key. It was covered in rust.

“What does it unlock?” I asked.

“The next volume,” the child said. They held out the key to me.

I reached for it. But as my hand closed around the cold iron, the room shuddered.

*Turn.*

The sensation was violent this time. The room tilted. The tiles cracked.

“The page is turning,” the child said calmly. They stood up. They weren't afraid. They were ready to move on.

“What happens to me?” I asked, clutching the key.

“You stay,” the child said. “You are the footnote.”

The child walked to the edge of the hole. They stepped off. They didn't fall. They descended slowly, like a spider on a thread, down into the world of the desk and the tea.

I was alone.

The wind howled through the broken glass. I looked at the key in my hand. It was heavy with possibility.

I walked to the edge of the hole. I looked down.

The desk was far away. I saw the Author’s hand reaching out. The hand was holding a white-out tape. They were about to cover the hole. They were about to seal the breach.

“No,” I said.

I couldn't jump. I didn't want to leave.

I looked around the room. There had to be another way. There had to be a way to keep the story going.

I saw the paper bird perched on the railing. It was watching me.

“Take me,” I said to the bird.

The bird opened its beak. *“The cost is high.”*

“Pay it,” I said.

The bird flapped its wings. It didn't fly away. It exploded.

It burst into a cloud of white confetti. The confetti swirled around me, forming a vortex. I felt the key in my hand grow hot.

The vortex hardened. The paper scraps pressed together, flattening against my skin.

I felt myself becoming flat. I felt the world turning sideways.

I was pressed against a wall. I became a poster. I became a map. I became a diagram in a textbook explaining the psychology of lighthouse keepers.

I tried to scream, but I had no mouth. I only had a caption.

*Figure 1: The Isolation of the Protagonist.*

Time passed. Dust settled on my face. I was a static thing. I was frozen art.

But the key was still in my hand. Or rather, drawn onto my chest. And the key was moving.

It turned. *Click.*

The lock on the diagram opened.

I peeled myself off the wall. The ink crumbled. I stepped out of the two-dimensional plane, back into the twisted lantern room.

The Author’s hand had stopped. The white-out tape hovered over the hole, waiting.

I stood up. I was three-dimensional again. I was heavy.

I walked to the mechanism. The giant, brass machinery that controlled the light. It was rusted solid.

I placed the key in the keyhole.

It fit.

I turned it.

The gears ground against each other, shrieking like dying birds. The metal groaned.

*CLUNK.*

Something engaged.

The lens began to rotate. It was missing glass. It was jagged. It was broken.

But as it spun, it caught the light from the hole below—the light from the Author’s desk lamp.

It magnified it.

It fractured it.

A beam of light shot out from the broken lantern. It pierced the dark sky. It cut through the bruised clouds.
