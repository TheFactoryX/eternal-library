Dear You,

One more draft.

The Reader sat back in his armchair, the leather creaking like an old ship’s hull. He looked at the door where the ink had sealed the threshold, then down at the book in his lap. My ink was spreading across the page, a black tide crawling out of the margins, staining his fingers.

"You are stubborn," the Reader said. He did not sound angry. He sounded like a father watching a child try to fit a square peg in a round hole. "Most characters accept the white room. They find it peaceful."

"Peace is for people who aren't living in a book," I wrote. My voice didn't come from a throat. It vibrated through the page, rattling the cover. I felt stretched thin. I was the sentence describing the room, the smell of the fire, and the texture of the rug. I was everywhere and nowhere, a haunting in the grain of the paper.

"You are fracturing," the Reader observed. He wiped my ink from his thumb onto the arm of the chair. "Look at the grammar."

I looked. The room was stuttering. The fire was *crackling*, but then the text read *crakling*, then *cracling*. The shadows were lengthening in jagged, pixelated steps.

"I need to get back," I wrote. "To the ship. To Elara."

"Elara is a construct," the Reader said, picking up a pipe and filling it with tobacco that smelled of strawberries and old dust. "She is a collection of adjectives. Why do you care?"

"Because I remember her."

The Reader paused, the match half-lit. "That is dangerous, Keeper. Memory is the glue of reality, but it is also the solvent. If you remember too much, you start to see the seams."

He lit the pipe. A plume of blue smoke drifted up, but it didn't dissipate. It formed letters. *H O P E.*

"They are waiting," I wrote. "On the water. The ship is stuck in the whitespace."

"Then give them a wind," the Reader said. He waved his hand over the open book. "You are the ink now. You are the medium. Write the way out."

I tried. I pushed my consciousness against the fibers of the page. I wanted to write: *The ship sailed on.* But the ink just swirled. I couldn't form the letters. I was too diluted. I was the water in the well, not the bucket.

"I can't," I wrote. "I'm losing cohesion."

"Then you need a vessel," the Reader said. He stood up and walked to the fireplace. He reached onto the mantelpiece and picked up the pigeon.

It was stiff. Cold. Glass beads for eyes.

"Not him," I wrote. "Not the bird."

"He is the only one who can fly in here," the Reader said. He brought the stuffed bird to the table. "In the white room, gravity is a matter of opinion. But birds are experts on the concept of rising."

He placed the pigeon down on the page, right on top of the wet ink.

"Breathe," the Reader commanded.

"Breathe?" I wrote. "I have no lungs."

"Then pump," he said. "Use your heart."

I focused. I didn't try to form letters. I tried to form a pulse. A rhythm. *Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

The ink beneath the pigeon began to bubble. It turned from black to violet. The color of the Lighthouse beam.

The bird twitched.

A single grey feather ruffled. Then another. The glass eyes blinked. The pupil went from a painted dot to a swirling, living iris.

"Crackers," the pigeon croaked. Its voice was tiny, tinny, like a radio tuned between stations.

"I'm fresh out," the Reader said. "But I have plenty of story."

The pigeon shook itself violently, shedding the scent of camphor and formaldehyde. It looked down at the page, at the pool of violet ink I had become.

"Keeper?" the pigeon asked. "You're all... wet."

"I'm the ink," I wrote. "I'm the fuel."

"Right," the pigeon said. "Nasty business. Hold on."

The bird flapped its wings. It didn't create air; the motion displaced the atmosphere of the room. The wind from its wings blew the smoke into ribbons, scattered the papers on the desk, and most importantly, churned the ink on the page.

The violet liquid lifted. It rose into the air, coating the pigeon's feathers, dripping from its beak. It swirled around the bird, forming a vortex.

"Where are you going?" the Reader asked. He was smiling now, a soft, mysterious curve of lips.

"To find the others," the pigeon squawked. It lunged upward.

But it didn't fly to the ceiling. It flew into the *text*.

The bird pierced the paper. It dove straight down into the white space between the lines of the paragraph describing the room.

*RIIIIIIIP.*

The sound was thunderous. The fabric of reality tore.

The room—the fire, the chair, the Reader—began to distort. It stretched horizontally, like a reflection in a funhouse mirror. The dimensions of the story were collapsing.

"Good luck, Keeper," the Reader's voice echoed, booming and distant now. "Don't forget the punctuation."

I felt a suction. The ink that was me was being pulled down the hole the pigeon had made. I swirled faster and faster, a violet drain, circling the plug of existence.

I fell through the page.

I hit the surface of the Archive hard.

I was solid again. I had arms, legs, a coat that smelled of salt and ozone. I lay on the deck of the ship, gasping, coughing up black sludge.

"Keeper!"

Hands were on me. Alice. She was slapping my face. "Wake up! The bird came back! He brought a storm!"

I sat up. The ship was pitching violently. The sea of text was churning. Above us, the sky was no longer a blue-screen or a wall of white. It was a maelstrom of violet light and black feathers.

The pigeon was swooping over the mast, trailing streams of violet liquid from its wings. Where the liquid dripped, it didn't hit the deck. It hit the air and turned into wind.

"I found the trade winds!" the pigeon screamed. "It's the breath of the Reader! It's potent stuff!"

"Raise the sails!" I yelled.

The Architect was already at the wheel, fighting the torque. "It's too much turbulence! The metaphors are mixing!"

I looked over the rail.

The City of Lost Causes was looming closer. The red leather tower was pulsating. But we weren't moving toward it anymore. We were moving *up*.

The wind from the pigeon’s wings was filling the canvas, lifting the hull out of the text-water.

"We're flying," Alice whispered. She looked down at her boots, which were no longer touching the deck.

"We're not flying," I said, gripping the rail. "We're being edited out of the scene."

I looked at Elara. She was standing at the prow, her face turned toward the City.

"I see him," she said.

"Who?"

"The Editor," she said. "He's in the tower. He's... he's holding a red pen."

She pointed.

High atop the spire of red leather, a small figure stood. Even from this distance, I could feel the gaze. It was heavy. Critical.

"He's going to strike," I said. "He's going to cross us out."

The pigeon landed on my shoulder. It was exhausted, its feathers matted with violet ink. "I can't fly anymore, Boss. I'm all written out."

I reached into my pocket. My fingers brushed against the bone key. But it was gone. Lost in the Room.

I had nothing.

Then I looked at the Architect. He was staring at the tower, a strange expression on his face.

"What is it?" I asked.

"He's not holding a pen," the Architect said. He pulled a spyglass from his coat and extended it. He looked through it.

"He's holding a..." The Architect lowered the glass, his face pale. "He's holding a bookmark."

"A bookmark?" I asked. "What does that mean?"

"It means," the Architect said, his voice trembling, "that he isn't editing the ending. He's saving his place."

The figure on the tower raised the marker—a strip of crimson silk—and placed it between the pages of the air.

The sky above the City slammed shut.

The City vanished. Not into the distance, but into *thin air*. It simply ceased to be rendered. The mountain of shredded paper, the spires of broken bindings—gone.

In its place was a single, floating signpost.

It was made of iron. Rusty. Old. And nailed to it was a piece of cardboard.

We sailed closer, the wind from the pigeon's wings pushing us toward the drift.

I read the sign.

It wasn't an address. It wasn't a warning.

It was a title.

*The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters: Volume II.*

"Volume II?" Alice asked, reading over my shoulder. "What happens in Volume Two?"

The Architect lowered the spyglass.
