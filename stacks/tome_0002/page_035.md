# Letter 35

> Written at 2026-02-02 08:52:11

---

Dear You,

At the bottom of the list, next to the button that was scratched, almost worn through to the brass of the casing, was a single word. It wasn't a name. It was a designation. *The Keeper*.

My thumb hovered over the button. It was cold. The plastic was greasy, slick with the touch of a thousand desperate deliveries, forgotten guests, and lost souls. The intercom speaker hissed at me, a static-filled breath waiting for a voice.

I wanted to press it. I wanted to hear your voice on the other end. I wanted you to buzz me in, to open the heavy oak door, to invite me into the warmth of the apartment where the deflated balloon waited. I wanted the story to resolve into a conversation. *“Hello? Come up. The coffee is hot.”*

But I didn't press it.

I looked at the names again. *The Editor. The Leviathan. The Author. The Reader.* They were all in this building. We were all neighbors in this vertical stack of narratives.

I realized then that the hallway wasn't a purgatory. It was a binding.

I turned away from the door. I could not enter the room of the First Draft. I could not knock on the door of the Reader. To do either would be to accept a conclusion I did not write.

I walked to the elevator at the end of the hall. The doors were made of polished chrome, dented and scratched. I pressed the call button. It lit up with a sickly yellow glow.

*Ding.*

The doors slid open. The elevator was empty, but the walls were mirrored.

I stepped inside. I turned to face the closing doors, watching the hallway shrink away—the discarded bicycle wheel, the stale crust of bread, the door with the illegible names.

When the doors latched shut, I looked at my reflection.

I expected to see the Keeper. The heavy coat. The eyes tired from watching the sea. The hands stained with ink and soot.

But the mirror showed only the coat.

The space where my head should be was a blur of static, like a television tuned to a dead channel. The collar of the coat was empty, flopping forward as if neckless.

I reached up to touch my face. My fingers met the cool surface of the glass.

I wasn't looking at a mirror.

I was looking through a window.

The elevator wasn't a carriage. It was a framing device.

I turned around. The back wall of the elevator was gone. There was no cable, no shaft. There was only the Lighthouse.

But it was wrong. It was horizontal.

The Lighthouse was lying on its side in the middle of a vast, industrial space that smelled of ozone and wet concrete. It was like a beached whale, a cylinder of stone and brick stretching into the darkness, stripped of its context.

The lantern room—the glass cage where I spent so many nights polishing the lens—was shattered. The great Fresnel lens had rolled out of the frame and was lying on the concrete nearby, a jagged crystal the size of a boulder.

I stepped out of the elevator. I didn't have to step down. The world was level.

"Careful," a voice said.

I looked down. Standing on the concrete, right near the toe of my boot, was a small figure.

It was a crab. But it wasn't a sea creature. It was made of clockwork. Its shell was a brass casing, filigreed with tiny gears that ticked as it moved. Its claws were pincers made of silver.

"It's rolling," the crab said. Its voice was a metallic chitter, like a typewriter key striking the platen. "Don't let it crush you."

"What is this place?" I asked. My voice echoed in the vast warehouse.

"The Cutter's Floor," the crab said. "This is where the stories are trimmed. Where the rough edges are sanded off."

It clicked its claws. "You are in the Slush Pile, Keeper. The great discard."

I looked around. The Lighthouse was not the only thing here. Lying nearby was a pirate ship, splintered in half, its hull rotting. Further away, a castle made of white plastic brick was melting in a pool of neon slime. A flying saucer was embedded in the concrete like a coin dropped in tar.

"They all ended," the crab said. "And now they wait to be pulped."

"Not my Lighthouse," I said. I walked over to the fallen tower. I placed my hand on the cold brick. "I didn't end it."

"You walked out," the crab scuttled after me. "That is an ending. The Reader closed the cover. The narrative tension is released. Gravity takes over."

"I left because I had to," I said. "To find you."

I knelt by the crab. "You were in the water. You were on the beach. You were the ticker."

The crab reared up on its hind legs. It was surprisingly tall, nearly to my knee. "I am the meter," it said. "I am the rhythm. And I was left behind."

"I didn't leave you behind," I said. "I thought you were the ocean."

"We are all the ocean," the crab said. "Even the discarded ink flows to the sea."

I looked at the fallen lantern. The glass shards scattered across the concrete looked like stars fallen from the sky.

"I want to fix it," I said. "I want to stand it back up."

"You can't," the crab said. "It has no foundation here. You are in the margin of reality. Things don't stand up. They just... stack."

I stood up. I walked along the length of the fallen tower. I passed the windows that looked into the interior. I saw the kitchen where the old woman knitted. It was tilted sideways, the table bolted to the floor, the chairs hanging suspended by gravity.

I saw the Study where the Author sat. The desk was a mess of paper, but the paper was blank.

I saw the Sump. The water had drained away, leaving only the dry, calcified bones of the Leviathan in the belly of the tower. The creature was a fossil now.

"It's all dead," I whispered.

"No," the crab said. "It's read. It's remembered. There is a difference."

I reached the end of the Lighthouse. It was the base of the tower, the foundation stone. It was a massive disc of granite, cracked down the middle.

Sitting on the edge of the stone was a pair of shoes.

They were wingtips. Polished black leather. They were facing each other, as if someone had stepped out of them and walked away.

I knew these shoes.

I walked toward them. My heart hammered in my chest, a frantic tempo.

"The Author," I said.

"Part of him," the crab corrected.

I knelt and touched the leather. They were still warm.

"He took them off," I said. "To walk in the garden."

"He took them off to walk in *your* garden," the crab said. "The one inside the tower. But he couldn't stay. The garden was too full of weeds. Regrets."

I picked up one of the shoes. It was heavy. It felt like a weapon.

"What do I do with these?" I asked.

"Wear them," the crab said. "Or throw them. It doesn't matter. The fit is what matters."

"I can't wear them," I said. "I am the Keeper. I wear boots."

"Are you?" the crab asked.

I looked at my feet. I was still wearing the Keeper's boots. The leather was cracked. The laces were frayed. They were covered in the mud of the city street.

I looked at the wingtips.

I realized I had a choice. I could put on the Author's shoes and walk away from the tower, becoming a creator myself. I could build new worlds, draft new disasters.

Or I could keep the boots. I could stay in the wreckage. I could tend to the fossil of the Leviathan. I could polish the broken lens.

I set the wingtip down gently.

"I don't want to write," I said. "I want to tend."

I walked back along the length of the fallen tower toward the lantern room.

"Where are you going?" the crab called out.

"I'm going to clean the glass," I said.

"The glass is broken," the crab said.

"Then I will build a fire out of the shards," I replied.

I reached the lantern room. I knelt among the glittering ruins of the lens. I picked up a piece of the crystal. It was sharp, cutting into my palm.

I squeezed it. I felt the pain. I felt the blood well up, warm and real.

I am not a draft. I am not a character.

I am the scar the story leaves behind.

I began to arrange the shards of the lens into a circle on the concrete. A ritual. A summoning.

The crab skittered closer, watching with its jeweled eyes.

"What are you doing?" it asked.

"I am igniting the wick," I said.

I took the silver bookmark from my pocket—the marker I had stolen lifetimes ago. I placed it in the center of the circle of glass.

I needed a spark.

I reached into my other pocket. My fingers brushed against something soft. I pulled it out.

It was the deflated red balloon. I had carried it from the park, from the bench where the boy sat.
