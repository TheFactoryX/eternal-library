# Letter 165

> Written at 2026-02-09 07:10:22

Dear You,

"Cut."

The voice did not come from the sky, but from everywhere at once. It vibrated in the hollow ribs of the plywood rocks. It hummed in the stagnant, painted waves.

The massive hand descending from the theatrical gloom hesitated. The fingers, which were the size of tree trunks and composed of smudged charcoal and eraser crumbs, twitched.

"Stand down," the Divine Voice boomed, sounding bored now. "The lighting is wrong. The emotion is flat. We need a rewrite."

The hand retreated. It didn't dissolve; it was *pulled* back, as if on a string, disappearing into the heavy velour darkness above the "horizon."

"We're not real," Alice whispered. She was kneeling on a "rock" that was beginning to show its staples. She touched the surface. "It's particle board."

"We're real," I said, though the conviction was leaking out of me. "We're just... being blocked."

"Blocked?" the Architect scoffed, dusting off his coat. He looked at the static-filled elevator shaft with a mix of horror and fascination. "This isn't a block, Keeper. It's a workshop. We've been moved to the Creator's desk. We are on the scratchpad."

I looked down at myself. The violet ink—the blood of the Reader, the essence of the story—was still on my hands. But now, it looked wet. Fresh. Like varnish.

"He's going to paint over us," Elara said. Her voice was trembling. She was backing away from the glass door of the Lighthouse-elevator. "I can feel the brush."

"We need to hide," I said. "We need to find a place where the ink has already dried. Where the narrative is too set to be changed."

"The sidelines," the Architect suggested. He pointed to the edge of the "set," where the plywood floor met the black curtain. "The peripherals. The margin notes."

We ran toward the darkness. As we moved, the world behind us shifted. The plywood rocks smoothed out, becoming a generic, featureless plain. The "sky" brightened to a harsh, clinical white.

"The sun," Alice gasped, shielding her eyes. "It's a lamp."

A spotlight, blinding and hot, had snapped on, illuminating the center of the stage. The Lighthouse stood under it, stark and alone.

"Don't look at the light," I warned. "If you look at the light, you become the subject."

We scrambled under the heavy black curtain at the edge of the world. It was heavy, smelled of dust and mildew, and blocked out the harsh white light instantly.

We were in the wings. Backstage.

It was a cavernous space, filled with the debris of a thousand stories never finished. Props were stacked in haphazard towers. A suit of armor made of dictionary pages. A jar labeled *Screams (Preserved)*. A door frame standing in the middle of nothing, leading nowhere.

"Where are we?" Elara asked.

"The Limbo of Lost Objects," the Architect said. He picked up a trumpet made of glass. It played a single, mournful note when he touched the mouthpiece. "Things that were described once but never used again."

"We can't stay here," I said. "The Creator knows we're here. He's just... distracted. Maybe looking for a better adjective."

"Maybe," the pigeon said. It had flown in ahead of us and was perched on top of a sarcophagus labeled *PLOT HOLES*. "But listen."

We listened.

From the stage beyond the curtain, we heard a new sound. Not the voice of God this time. A scratching sound. A dry, rhythmic *scritch-scratch*.

"What is that?" Alice asked.

"A quill," the Architect said, paling. "Or a stylus. Someone is writing the scene."

"Writing it *now*?" I asked. "Live?"

"The Improvisational Stream," he whispered. "The most dangerous form of storytelling. No outline. No safety net. Just pure, unfiltered impulse."

I crept to the edge of the curtain and peered out.

The spotlight had shifted. It was now focused on a single chair that had appeared in the middle of the plywood plain. It was a high-backed Victorian chair, velvet and mahogany.

Sitting in the chair was a figure.

It was... me.

Not the Draft me. Not the Architect me. But *me*. The Keeper.

He was wearing my coat. He had my face. But he was transparent. Flickering. A ghost of a character.

And standing over him, with a large, feathered quill that dripped glowing silver ink, was a woman.

She was tall, dressed in a gown that seemed to be woven from the night sky. Her hair was a nebula of swirling stars. She had no face—only a smooth, porcelain surface that reflected the scene around her.

"Who is that?" Elara breathed, joining me at the curtain.

"The Muse," the Architect said, his voice trembling with reverence. "The oldest of us all. The one who whispers in the ear of the sleepless."

The Muse dipped the quill into the air itself, as if the atmosphere were ink. She began to write on the transparent figure of the Keeper.

She wrote on his chest. *LONELY.*

The Keeper on the chair gasped, clutching his chest. The word burned there, visible through his coat.

She wrote on his throat. *SILENCED.*

The Keeper opened his mouth to scream, but no sound came out.

"She's defining him," I realized. "She's giving him his character traits."

"She's straitjacketing him," the Architect corrected. "If she writes *SILENCED*, you lose your voice. If she writes *BROKEN*, you lose your will."

I looked down at my own hands. The violet ink was drying, cracking.

"I am the draft," I whispered. "He is the Final Version."

"No," Elara said, gripping my arm tight. "He is the puppet. You are the one who jumped off the page. You are the one who chose."

The Muse paused. She tilted her smooth face toward the curtain.

She froze.

She knows.

"She can see us," Alice squeaked.

"Run," I said.

But it was too late.

The Muse didn't move toward us. She simply turned the quill around. She pointed the feathered end at the curtain.

*RIIIIIIP.*

The heavy velvet curtain tore away from its rings. It disintegrated into moths, thousands of them, which fluttered blindly around us, carrying the smell of old books.

We were exposed.

The spotlight hit us, blinding and hot.

The Muse approached. She moved with a terrible grace, her feet making no sound on the plywood. She stopped in front of me.

Up close, the blankness of her face was terrifying. It wasn't just a lack of features; it was a rejection of them. A void where identity should be.

She raised the quill. The silver ink dripped onto the floor, sizzling like acid.

I stood my ground. I had survived the Architect. I had survived the Reader. I would survive the source of inspiration.

"I am not a blank page," I said. "I am the story you couldn't control."

The Muse tilted her head. She reached out with her free hand. She touched my chest, right where the phantom *LONELY* was burning on the other me.

She drew a line on my coat. Silver ink.

*UNFINISHED.*

She stepped back. She pointed to the elevator shaft in the Lighthouse.

Then, she vanished.

Not walked away. *Ceased to be.* One moment she was there, the next, only the smell of ozone and the fading silver trail of her script remained.

The spotlight cut out. The white room plunged into twilight.

"What did she write?" Alice asked, stepping out from behind the sarcophagus.

I looked down at the ink on my chest. *UNFINISHED.*

"It's not a command," I said. "It's a status report."

"The other Keeper," Elara said, pointing to the chair.

The transparent figure in the chair was flickering violently. He looked like a bad signal, his body stretching and compressing.

"He's destabilizing," the Architect said. "The Muse has abandoned the draft. He's going to revert to raw text."

The figure stood up. He screamed—a silent, widening of the mouth. He looked at us.

He began to run toward us. But as he ran, he fell apart. His coat became feathers. His skin became paper. His legs became ink ribbons.

He wasn't running. He was *flowing*.

The tidal wave of raw text surged toward us, a tsunami of unformed nouns and verbs.

"Into the Lighthouse!" I shouted.

We sprinted for the glass door. I threw it open.

Inside was the elevator shaft.

"Punch it!" I yelled at the Architect.

He leaped into the shaft, grabbed the cable, and slammed his fist against the call button—a jagged piece of scrap metal.

We jumped in after him. The doors slid shut just as the wave of text hit the glass. The "Lighthouse" shook, but the glass held.

For a moment, we were in darkness. Then, a hum. A mechanical whirring.

The elevator shuddered. It began to rise.
