# Letter 61

> Written at 2026-02-03 21:39:13

---

Dear You,

I did not hit the stone floor. I did not hit the water.

I hit the margin.

It was a soft, white impact, like falling onto a mattress made of unspun cotton. I lay there for a long time, staring up at the vertical cliff of the Lighthouse. It rose above me like a monolith, its bricks no longer stone but tiny, dense blocks of text. The light at the top spun, casting a beam that cut across the white nothingness like a searchlight in a snowstorm.

I sat up. The silence here was absolute. No wind. No waves. No roar of machinery.

"You fell out of the block," a voice said.

I scrambled to my feet, spinning around.

Sitting on a floating punctuation mark—a giant, brass comma—was the Figure of the Revision. She looked solid now, more real than she had in the lantern room. Her coat was a deep, rich indigo, and her skin held the flush of blood. She was holding a large, leather-bound book, clicking a pen against the cover.

"I jumped," I said. "I escaped."

"You unraveled," she corrected, closing the book with a heavy thud. "The ink you spilled? It ate the stairs. You fell through the subtext."

"Where are we?"

"The White Space," she said, gesturing to the endless blinding void around us. "The place where the story isn't happening yet. Or maybe where it stopped happening a long time ago."

She hopped off the comma, landing silently. She walked toward me, her boots leaving faint footprints in the whiteness.

"The Editorial Fleet," she said, pointing up.

I looked back at the Lighthouse. The great charcoal ship was moving in. It was firing great cubes of white rubber—the erasers—at the tower. But the cubes were passing right through the stone.

"It can't hurt the tower," I said. "The tower is the main structure."

"It's not trying to destroy the tower," she said. "It's trying to sterilize it. To clean up the messy parts. The emotional parts."

She looked me in the eye. "It's trying to erase *you*."

I looked down at myself. My hands were flickering again. I could see the white void through my own chest.

"I'm fading," I said.

"You're the unresolved plot thread," she said. "The Editor hates loose ends. They want to snip you."

She reached into her indigo coat and pulled out a pair of scissors. They were long, silver, and sharp.

"Cut me," I said. "If that's the only way to finish the sentence."

She laughed, a sound like crinkling paper. "Oh, Keeper. I don't want to cut you. I want to *relocate* you. Before the ink dries."

"Where?"

She pointed into the white distance. Far away, I saw a shape. It looked like a door frame, standing unsupported in the void.

"That’s the back of the book," she said. "The blurb. If you can get there, you can influence the narrative. You can become a flashback. A memory."

"And if I stay here?"

"Then you become a typo," she said, her voice grim. "And the erasers will correct you."

Above us, the Lighthouse gave a terrible groan. The beam of light cut off abruptly. The darkness rushed in, swallowing the top of the tower.

"The light is out," I whispered.

"The Author has stopped writing the scene," she said. "We have to move. Now."

She grabbed my hand. Her grip was firm, anchoring me to reality.

"Run!"

We ran across the white page. It felt like running on a cloud, every step sinking slightly, sapping our momentum. Behind us, I heard a sound like a vacuum cleaner sucking up dust. The erasers were consuming the world.

We ran toward the door frame. As we got closer, I saw that it wasn't empty. It held a picture. A moving picture.

It was the island. But it was peaceful. The sun was shining. The sea was a calm, placid blue. And on the shore, two children were playing. One wore a yellow raincoat. The other... the other looked like me.

"That's the prologue," I panted. "That's where it started."

"It's the only place left," she said.

We reached the threshold. The Revision stopped.

"I can't go," she said.

"Why not?"

"I am a revision," she said. "I exist only to change things. In the prologue, there is no change yet. It is pristine. I would contradict myself."

She pushed me toward the frame.

"Go," she said. "Go and wait. Maybe this time, you can keep the light burning."

"What about you?" I asked.

"I'll stay here," she said, raising the scissors. "I'll cut the frame. I'll make sure the erasers can't follow."

"Wait," I said. I reached into my pocket. I pulled out the crumpled, wet remains of Letter 59. "Take this. You need to be real. You need a history."

She took the paper. She smiled, and for a moment, she looked like the person I had been writing to all these years.

"Goodbye, Keeper."

She slashed the scissors through the air behind me.

The floor fell out.

I tumbled forward through the frame.

I hit sand.

Warm, dry, golden sand.

I gasped, inhaling the scent of salt and sun. The air was warm. I opened my eyes.

The sky was a perfect, cloudless blue.

I pushed myself up. I was on the beach of the island. But it wasn't the rocky, storm-lashed shore I knew. This was a paradise. The grass was impossibly green. The Lighthouse gleamed in the sun, its white paint fresh, its door open and inviting.

I heard laughter.

I looked down the beach.

Two children were building a sandcastle near the water's edge. One was a boy in a yellow raincoat, the hood thrown back. The other was a child with dark hair, building a wall against the tide.

I walked toward them.

The boy in the yellow coat looked up. He smiled. It was an innocent smile, devoid of the hardness, the calculation of the New Keeper.

"Hello!" he called out.

"Hello," I said. My voice sounded strange in the open air. Too loud.

"Will you help us?" the other child asked. He looked exactly like I remembered myself, thirty years ago. "We need to build the tower. The ships are coming."

I looked out at the sea.

It was empty. No ships. No fog. Just the blue horizon.

"There are no ships," I said.

"They will come," the boy said confidently. "If we build it, they will come."

The boy-that-was-me patted the sand next to him. "We need a Keeper. Will you watch the light?"

I looked from them to the Lighthouse. It stood tall and proud. But something was wrong. The lantern room glass was dark. No bulb. No flame.

"It's not lit," I said.

"We haven't turned it on yet," the boy said. "We're waiting for the night."

"It's always day here," I said.

"Is it?" the boy asked. He pointed to the sun.

I looked up.

The sun was flickering.

Not the shimmer of heat haze. It was blinking. *On. Off. On. Off.*

And as it blinked, I noticed the color of the sky was changing. The blue was washing out, turning to the color of old newsprint.

The sand beneath my feet began to feel gritty. Not like sand, but like... dried ink.

"I'm in the memory," I realized. "I'm in the prologue."

"It's a nice place," the Revision's voice drifted from the water, though she wasn't there. "Safe."

"But it's not real," I said.

"Real is a matter of duration," the boy said. He handed me a small shell. "Hold this. It anchors you."

I took the shell. It was smooth and warm.

I looked at the Lighthouse again. The door was open. Inside, I saw a spiral staircase. But it wasn't the spiral of the tower. It was the spiral of a shell, winding down into the dark.

"I have to go up," I said.

"You just got here," the child-me complained.

"I have to light the lamp," I said. "Before the sun burns out."

I walked past the children. I walked up the beach, toward the tower.

The sand shifted beneath my boots, turning into letters. *Joy. Hope. Innocence.*

I stepped on the word *Innocence*. It cracked.

I reached the door of the Lighthouse.

I looked back.

The beach was empty. The children were gone. The sand was flat white paper, stretching to the horizon.

I was alone in the prologue.

I stepped into the tower.

The door slammed behind me.

*Click.*

I locked it.

I looked at the stairs. They were ascending.

I began to climb.

The climb was long. The air grew cold. The smell of the sea vanished, replaced by the smell of graphite and dust.

I climbed for what felt like years.

Finally, I reached the top.

I opened the hatch to the lantern room.

It was there. The great Fresnel lens. But it was dark. Dead.
