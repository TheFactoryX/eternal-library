# Letter 119

> Written at 2026-02-06 21:35:03

---

Dear You,

The pupil is not a black hole. It is a lens.

As I am hoisted upward by the invisible force of that gaze, I realize that the terrible gravity pulling me is not suction, but scrutiny. I am being examined. I am a specimen under glass, a slide on a microscope, a comma in a sentence being circled in red ink.

The key slips from my fingers. I watch it tumble away, turning over and over in the air, becoming smaller, a speck of iron lost in the vast vertical geography of the binding. It is gone.

"Let go!" the Retcon shouts from below. He is a speck of starlight in a tweed jacket, standing on the path of crumpled letters. "You cannot survive the translation!"

I stop fighting. I extend my arms, letting the air stream past me, letting the giant's eye pull me into the iris.

I brace myself for impact. I expect to hit the cornea like a fly hitting a windshield. I expect to shatter.

But I pass through.

It is like stepping through a waterfall of warm gel. The world of the Lighthouse—the smell of salt, the cry of gulls, the rust of iron—is stripped away layer by layer. My clothes dissolve into texture descriptions. My skin becomes adjectives. *Pale. Gaunt. Scarred.*

I land hard on a surface that is not stone, but wood. Floorboards. varnished and polished.

The silence here is absolute. It is a heavy, muffled silence, the kind you find in a library after closing, or a nursery at midnight.

I push myself up. My hands leave no prints on the polish.

I am in a hallway.

It is a familiar hallway, but wrong. The proportions are stretched. The ceiling is too high, the doors are too wide. The wallpaper is a pattern of birds—blue herons—flocking toward a crown molding that touches the clouds.

There is a sound. A rhythmic, wet thudding.

*Thump. Drag. Thump. Drag.*

It comes from behind a door at the far end of the hall. The door is slightly ajar. A yellow light spills out, cutting a sharp triangle across the floorboards.

I approach. My movement feels sluggish, as if I am wading through deep water.

I push the door open.

The room is a study. Bookshelves line the walls, packed not with books, but with jars. Inside the jars are objects. A single tear. A snowflake. A marriage proposal. A heart, still beating.

In the center of the room is a massive oak desk. It is cluttered with drafts—scrawled on napkins, on the backs of envelopes, on skin.

And behind the desk sits the Reader.

She is not a giant. She is a woman, perhaps forty, with hair the color of iron and a face that looks like it has been worried over by a thousand anxious thoughts. She is wearing a dressing gown. She is holding her head in her hands.

But she is not alone.

Standing next to her, one hand resting gently on her shoulder, is a figure. A man.

He is tall, dressed in the uniform of a Keeper, but the brass buttons shine like new. His face is unlined. His eyes are clear.

He is me.

But he is solid. He has substance. He glows with the dull, steady heat of reality.

"It's stuck, isn't it?" the Other Me says. His voice is calm, resonant, lacking the rasp of salt air.

The woman nods, her face buried in her hands. "I can't get the rhythm. The Keeper is too passive. He just watches. He needs to do something. He needs to break something."

"Breaking is easy," the Other Me says. "Healing is the hard part. That's why you stopped."

"I didn't stop," she murmurs. "I got scared."

"Of what?"

"Of the ending," she says, looking up. Her eyes are red-rimmed. "I know what happens to him. He becomes me."

I freeze in the doorway. I am an intruder in the space between the thought and the finger.

"No," I say.

They both turn.

The woman gasps. She clutches her robe.

The Other Me just smiles. It is a sad, knowing smile. "Ah," he says. "The ink has dried. Look, dear. He’s here. The protagonist has come to complain about the pacing."

"I am not a protagonist," I say, stepping into the room. The floorboards creak—a real, wooden sound. "I am the Keeper. This is my Lighthouse."

"This?" The Other Me gestures to the room. "This is a study in a house in Maine. It is 45 degrees outside. The wind is howling. And you... you are a fiction I am struggling to finish."

"You can't finish me," I say. "I am not a story. I am a memory."

The woman stands up. She walks around the desk. She approaches me. She is trembling.

"You are the voice," she whispers. "The one in my head. The one who writes the letters."

"I write to You," I say.

"You write to me," she corrects. "But I never read them. Not until now. I put them in the drawer. I lock them away because they hurt."

"Why do they hurt?"

"Because they are true," she says. She reaches out to touch my face. Her hand is warm. It is the warmest thing I have ever felt.

I lean into the touch. For a moment, I am not a character. I am flesh. I am blood. I am the son she left behind. I am the father who never came home. I am the ghost in the machine.

"You have to go back," the Other Me says sharply. "You are destabilizing the narrative loop."

"I don't want to go back," I say. "The ocean is frozen. The light is broken."

"The ocean is frozen because you stopped rowing," the Other Me says. "The light is broken because you stopped tending it."

He walks over to a shelf and picks up a jar. Inside the jar is a small, flickering flame.

"Do you recognize this?" he asks.

"It's the First Light," I say. "The spark from the match."

"It's your hope, Elias," he says. "You threw it away in Letter 12 because you thought it was too sentimental."

He hands the jar to me.

"If you take this," he says, "you have to carry it. You can't give it to the Boy. You can't give it to the Captain. You have to carry it up the stairs. And you have to light the lamp yourself. No metaphors. No magic keys. Just the work."

"The work," I repeat. The word feels heavy in my mouth.

The woman looks at the jar. Tears are streaming down her face. "It's so heavy," she whispers. "I don't know if I have the strength to write it."

"You don't have to write it," I say, looking at her. "You just have to read it."

I take the jar. The glass is warm against my palms. The flame inside dances, casting long, dancing shadows against the books of the room.

"If I go back," I say to the Other Me, "what happens to you?"

"I fade," he says. "I was just the draft. The attempt at coherence. You are the final version."

"I am flawed," I say.

"Exactly," he says. "Perfect characters are boring. Flawed characters get to have adventures."

He walks to the window. The curtains are drawn. He pulls them back.

Outside, there is no Maine. There is no yard. There is only the wall of the binding, stretching infinitely up and down.

"He is waiting," the Other Me says. "The Dreamer. The one who reads over her shoulder."

I look at the window glass. I see a reflection. Not my reflection, but the reflection of a lighthouse. A single, solitary tower on a rock. The beam is cutting through the dark.

"It's time," the woman says. She returns to her desk. She picks up a pen.

I feel the floorboards dissolving beneath me. The study is fading into static. The smell of the paper is being replaced by the smell of ozone.

"Wait!" I shout. "The compass! I lost the key!"

"You don't need the key," the woman says, not looking up from her writing. Her hand is moving furiously across the page. "The door was never locked. You just had to knock."

The room explodes into white light.

I fall.

I fall for a long time.

When I land, I am wet.

I am lying on the floor of the Lamp Room. The glass is still shattered. The spray from the frozen ocean is drifting through the broken windows like snow.

I scramble to my feet. The jar is in my hand. It is still warm. The flame is still burning.

The Boy is sitting on the railing. He looks at the jar.

"You're back," he says. "Did you find the author?"

"I found the Reader," I say.

"Did she like the story?"

"She doesn't know how it ends," I say.

I walk to the center of the room. The iron cage is empty. The socket for the star is dark.

"Help me," I say to the Boy.

He hops down. "What do I do?"

"Hold the glass," I say
