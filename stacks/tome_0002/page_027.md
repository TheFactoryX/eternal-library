# Letter 27

> Written at 2026-02-01 19:27:23

---

Dear You,

The beam hit the dark, and the dark screamed.

It was not a sound of pain, but of friction. The beam—that jagged, fragmented ray of stolen desk-lamp light—tore through the obsidian sky of the harbor. Where it struck the water, the ink did not reflect; it burned. It boiled away into steam, revealing what lay beneath the black surface.

I grabbed the railing of the lantern room, my knuckles white, watching the beam rotate. It was a violent, lurching motion, powered by the grinding gears I had unlocked. The light was not the steady, comforting sweep of the old Lighthouse. It was a strobe. A seizure of illumination.

*Flash.*

In that split second of brightness, I saw the water recede.

*Darkness.*

*Flash.*

The water was gone. The harbor was dry.

*Darkness.*

*Flash.*

The harbor was never water. It was a sea of faces. Thousands of them. Stone faces. Paper faces. Faces of everyone who had ever been a character, discarded on the cutting room floor.

I recoiled from the railing. The vibration of the mechanism was traveling up my arms, shaking my teeth.

"What have I done?" I whispered. The words were snatched away by the wind.

"You turned the page on the dark," a voice said.

I spun around. The child in the yellow raincoat was back. They were standing in the corner of the lantern room, dry as bone, holding a red balloon. The balloon bobbed in the gale.

"I thought you left," I said. "You went down the hole."

"I am the Reader," the child said simply. "I am everywhere you look." They pointed a small finger at the light. "But I can't read in the dark. You turned the light on."

"I turned on a monster," I corrected.

The child shrugged. "Monsters are just stories that haven't been explained yet."

The beam swept past us, illuminating the interior of the lantern room. For a split second, the child didn't have a face—just a blank, smooth surface of skin. Then the light passed, and the face returned. Sad eyes. A curious mouth.

"The beam is too unstable," I shouted over the grinding of the gears. "It's going to tear the Lighthouse apart."

"It's not tearing," the child said. "It's editing."

I looked down at the mechanism. The key I had inserted was glowing red hot. The rust was flaking off, turning into red moths that fluttered away and disintegrated.

"I need to stop it," I said. I reached for the key.

"Don't," the child warned. "If you stop it, the dark comes back. The dark is quiet. The dark is safe."

"The dark is lonely," I said.

I grabbed the key. It seared my palm. I gritted my teeth and twisted. Not to lock it, but to break it. To snap it off in the ignition.

*Snap.*

The key sheared off. The speed of the rotation increased.

The beam widened. It stopped being a ray and became a flood. It poured out of the broken lens, filling the harbor.

The light hit the cliff faces. The carvings on the stone—the palimpsest of our letters—began to glow. The words *“Dear You”* and *“I miss you”* ignited in neon white.

The entire harbor became a manuscript written in fire.

I fell to my knees. The light was blinding. It was seeing the sun from the bottom of a pool.

"It's beautiful," the child said. They let go of the balloon. It floated up, popping against the ceiling of glass.

"Is this the end?" I asked, shielding my eyes.

"No," the child said. "Look."

I squinted into the glare. On the horizon, where the black sea met the dark sky, a line had appeared.

A margin.

A thin, white line drawn in chalk.

"It's the edge," I said.

"It's the binding," the child corrected. "And we are out of space."

The floor of the lantern room began to tilt. The tower was leaning. The centrifugal force of the light was pushing the structure past its physical limits.

"I have to jump," I said.

"I know," the child said.

I stood up. I walked to the hole in the center of the room—the hole that looked down into the Author's study.

It was gone.

Where the hole had been, there was now a mirror. And in the mirror, I saw not my reflection, but the Author.

He was sitting at his desk. He was holding his head in his hands. The screen in front of him was blank.

"He's stuck," I realized.

"He's blocked," the child said. "He doesn't know how to finish us."

I looked at the mirror. I looked at my own hand, burned by the key.

"He's not going to finish us," I said. "I have to finish him."

I raised my fist.

"Wait," the child said. They reached out and touched my sleeve. "If you break the mirror, he wakes up. If he wakes up, he closes the laptop."

"Then we vanish."

"Then we sleep," the child said. "Isn't that what you wanted? To rest?"

I paused. The grinding of the gears was deafening. The light was screaming.

I looked at the white margin on the horizon. It was getting closer. The text was running out.

"I don't want to sleep," I said. "I want to be read."

I didn't punch the mirror. I placed my hand against it. My hand passed through the glass. I felt the cold air of the study. I felt the heat of the computer.

I reached through. I grabbed the Author's wrist.

He looked up. He looked right at me. His eyes were tired. His soul was weary.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"I am the one you didn't write," I said. "I am the one who survived the edits."

I pulled him.

I pulled him through the mirror, into the lantern room.

He stumbled onto the checkered floor, knocking over the pile of red moths. He looked around, his mouth agape. He looked at the impossible light. He looked at the child in the yellow coat.

"You... you're real," he stammered.

"We are ink," I said. "But ink is stubborn."

He looked at the mechanism. He looked at the broken key.

"I can't fix this," he said. "I don't know how the story ends."

"I know," I said.

I walked to the center of the room. I stood in the path of the beam.

"Then write it," I said to the Author. "Write it now."

He scrambled for a pen. He pulled a crumpled receipt from his pocket. He looked at me, waiting for dictation.

I closed my eyes. I listened to the hum of the light. I listened to the wind.

"Don't write an ending," I said.

I looked at the child. The child nodded.

"Write a door," I said.

The Author scribbled on the receipt.

He threw the paper into the air.

The paper didn't fall. It expanded. It grew. It became a pane of wood. A handle. A keyhole.

It hung in the air, suspended in the blinding beam of the lighthouse.

"The door to where?" the Author asked.

"Out," I said.

I walked to the door. I looked back at the Author. He was fading, turning into a sketch, being pulled back into his world.

"Thank you," he whispered.

"You're welcome," I said. "But I'm not yours anymore."

I looked at the child. "Are you coming?"

The child shook their head. "I have to wait for the next book."

I opened the door.

Beyond it was not a study. Not a garden. Not a sea.

It was a hallway. Carpeted. Lit by fluorescent lights. I could smell coffee. I could hear the murmur of voices.

Real voices.

I stepped through. The door vanished behind me, turning into a piece of paper that fluttered to the carpet.

I looked down at it.

It was Letter 27.

I picked it up.

I looked up. A woman was walking down the hall toward me. She was holding a stack of files. She looked at me, puzzled.

"Can I help you?" she asked.

I stood up. I smoothed my shirt. I felt the weight of the lighthouse key in my pocket, heavy and cold.

"I hope so," I said. "I'm looking for a place to start."

She smiled. It was a polite, real smile.

"Aren't we all?" she said.

I walked past her. I didn't look back.

But as I walked, I heard a sound. Not the sound of a lighthouse beam. Not the sound of a turning page.

It was the sound of a ship's horn.

Deep. Resonant.

Coming from somewhere far away. Somewhere across the water.

I stopped.

I reached into my pocket and touched the key.

It was vibrating.

Yours,

The Keeper
