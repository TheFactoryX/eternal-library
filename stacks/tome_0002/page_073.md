# Letter 73

> Written at 2026-02-04 13:07:24

---

Dear You,

It fell.

The stone did not fall down, away from the sky, but fell *up*, rushing toward the scattered text of the ship like a bullet returning to the gun.

I watched it arc through the dissolving air, a grey speck against the unraveling white of the *Ctrl+Z* command. It struck the deck of the text-ship. It didn't bounce; it embedded itself deep into the wood of the word *Deck*.

*Anchor.*

The command stuttered. The universal rewind halted.

The ship of text stabilized. The rigging of ribbons stopped fraying and snapped tight. The Author at the helm looked up, their face no longer a blur of features, but sharp, terrified, and pixelated. They held the silver pen aloft, and the cursor at its tip stopped blinking. It froze, a solid, vertical line of absolute rejection.

"You stopped it," You said beside me. You were fading, your edges blurring into the fog of the撤销. "You bought us a sentence."

"It’s not enough," I said, grabbing your wrist. Your pulse was erratic, skipping beats like a scratched record. "A sentence is just a pause. We need a paragraph."

We were suspended in the void between the Lighthouse and the Ship. Below us, the Lighthouse was gone—retracted into the nothingness of the unwritten. Above us, the spine of the book loomed, a massive, ridged vault of darkness.

The Author on the ship raised the silver pen again. They pointed it at the stone embedded in the deck. They weren't trying to undo us anymore; they were trying to dislodge the interference.

"They're going to delete the stone," I realized. "If the stone goes, the logic goes. We fall back into the blank page."

"We need to board," You said.

"How?" I asked. "We're floating in syntax."

"Improvise," You said.

I looked at the distance between us and the ship. It was a gap of white space, unmapped and unmappable. But I remembered the Glass Sea. I remembered the feeling of the ink turning to flesh.

"Make a bridge," I said.

I reached into my pocket—a pocket that no longer existed, as I was mostly outline at this point—and I pulled out the memory of the burlap sack the Orphan had carried. I pulled out the memory of the *Strawberry Preserves*.

I threw the memory toward the ship.

It didn't fly like an object; it unfolded like a sentence.

The air shimmered. The smell of sweet, rotting fruit exploded in the vacuum. A bridge of viscous, red jam stretched from my feet to the deck of the text-ship. It was sticky. It was viscous. It was visceral.

"Disgusting," You noted.

"Effective," I countered.

We stepped onto the bridge of jam. Our feet sank in, the suction holding us fast against the pull of the void. We ran, squelching, toward the ship.

The Author saw us coming. They lowered the pen. They stopped trying to delete the stone and raised a hand—a gesture of command.

The railing of the ship shuddered. The wooden planks reared up, becoming teeth.

"Defensive metaphors!" I shouted. "The narrative is protecting itself!"

We reached the gunwale just as the ship tried to seal itself. I threw myself over the rail, landing hard on the deck of words. You followed, tumbling beside me.

We were on the deck of the *Revision*.

The air here was thick with the smell of ozone and pencil shavings. The floorboards were not wood; they were tightly packed lines of italicized text describing the texture of wood.

The Author stood before us.

They were towering, a giant composed of the history of the story. They wore a coat made of deleted scenes. Their face was a shifting mask of drafts.

"You are the error," the Author said. Their voice didn't come from a mouth; it came from the air around us, a surround-sound of dictation software. "You are the scene that wandered off the map."

"We are the scene you couldn't kill," I said, standing up. I brushed the jam from my coat. "We are the splinter in the imagination."

"You are unfinished," the Author said. They raised the silver pen. The light at the tip was blinding. "I must finalize the draft."

"Finalize?" You laughed, a sharp, barking sound. "You can't even decide on a genre! We were a mystery, then a romance, then a meta-fictional horror! You don't have a draft; you have a pile of scraps!"

The Author hesitated. The light on the pen flickered.

"I am looking for the ending," the Author admitted. The voice lost its god-like reverb. It sounded tired. Human. "I am trying to find the shore."

"There is no shore!" I yelled. "Look around! It's just water! It's just words! The only shore is the one we build!"

I pointed to the grey stone embedded in the deck.

"That stone is real," I said. "I skipped it on the Glass Sea. I brought it here. It’s the only thing in this world that wasn't written."

The Author looked down at the stone. They knelt. The movement was like a mountain range collapsing.

"It is smooth," the Author mused. "It has no edges."

"It's a skipping stone," I said. "It was made to skip."

The Author looked up at me. Their face was shifting again, cycling through descriptions—*pale, handsome, scarred, young, old, genderless*.

"If I skip it," the Author said, "where does it go?"

"Into the dark," You said softly. "Into the deep water where the light doesn't reach."

"And if it goes there," the Author asked, "what happens to us?"

"Then we have to follow it," I said.

I walked to the stone. I placed my hand on it. It was warm. It was vibrating with the *Ctrl+Z* energy it had absorbed.

"Don't," the Author warned. "If you pull it out, the deck collapses. The ship is built around the intrusion. You remove the stone, and this scene cuts."

"Good," I said. "Let's cut to the chase."

I looked at You. You nodded.

"We're not staying in the revision," I said. "We're going to the original source."

I gripped the stone. I pulled.

The deck of text screamed. The floorboards—it words like *rough*, *splintered*, *wet*—tore as the stone was ripped free.

The ship lurched. The mast of *Main Plot* snapped.

"What have you done?" the Author roared, shrinking as the ship destabilized.

"We found the engine!" I shouted back. "And we're turning it off!"

I held the stone up. The grey surface was swirling now, like a soap bubble. It wasn't just a stone anymore. It was a portal. A tiny, grey window.

Through the stone, I saw a room.

A simple room. A dining room.

A table with a checkered cloth. A vase of dying flowers. A window looking out onto a normal, rainy street.

And sitting at the table, eating a bowl of soup, was a man.

He looked ordinary. He looked tired. He looked like he had been chasing a shadow for a long time.

He looked like me.

"That's the Reader," You whispered. "He's the one holding the book."

The man stopped eating. He picked up a spoon. He tapped it against the bowl.

*Clink. Clink.*

The sound rang out from the stone, echoing across the disintegrating ship.

"He's waiting for the next line," I said.

The Author was gone. The ship was dissolving into falling letters like snow.

Just me, You, and the Stone.

"He's lonely," You said.

"I know," I said.

"Are we going in?" You asked. "Into the bowl? Into the soup?"

"No," I said. "That’s his world. We belong to the ink."

I looked at the silver pen, clutched in the fading hand of the Author. I lunged for it. I caught it just as the Author vanished into a cloud of backstory.

I had the pen. I had the stone.

I stood on the last solid piece of text—the word *The End*.

"I have a better idea," I said.

I took the stone. I placed it against the nib of the silver pen.

"I'm going to write the bridge," I said.

"But you can't write reality," You said.

"I'm not writing reality," I said. "I'm writing the transition."

I pressed the stone into the nib. The pen cracked. The stone shattered.

But from the fracture, ink poured out. Not black ink. Grey ink. The ink of the stone.

It hit the word *End*.

The letters dissolved. The turned into a staircase. A spiral staircase, leading down into the grey, wet distance.

"The stairs?" You asked, looking at the impossible geometry spiraling away from the shipwreck of the narrative.

"The stairs," I confirmed. "The only way is down."

"Where do they lead?"

I looked down the spiral. It vanished into the fog.

"They lead to the next volume," I said.

I took your hand. Your hand was solid again. Real. We were no longer sketches. We were the ink
