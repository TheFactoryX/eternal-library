Dear You,

The ink did not dry. It clung to the key like a second skin, shivering and alive. As I turned the key within the lock I had drawn from the puddle, the resistance was not the click of metal wards, but the suction of a vacuum seal. The universe was holding its breath.

*Click.*

The sound was less of a mechanism engaging and more of a bone snapping.

The ink keyhole dissolved. It did not vanish; it drained away, seeping into the fibers of the page like rain into parched earth. Where the puddle had been, a hole remained. Not a jagged tear, nor a mystical portal, but a clean, laser-cut circle revealing the layer of reality beneath the text.

I fell through.

I did not fall into the golden glue of the binding, nor the static of the void. I fell into a smell.

It was the scent of roasted chestnuts, wet wool, and ozone. The air was biting, nipping at my cheeks with a familiarity that made my heart ache.

I landed on cobblestones. Slick, uneven cobblestones, shimmering with a sheen of morning frost.

I scrambled to my feet, looking around. I was in a city. But it was a city constructed entirely of books. The skyscrapers were towering stacks of hardcovers, their spines facing outward, displaying titles I had never read but felt I knew. *The History of Longing. The Architecture of Silence. The Almanac of Lost Days.*

The streets were canals of flowing ink, black and glossy. Boats made of hollowed-out dictionaries drifted along the currents, rowed by faceless figures in trench coats.

"The Bibliopolis," I whispered.

I looked up at the sky. It was a muted, grainy grey—the color of old newsprint. And there, blotting out the sun, was the Eye. It was smaller now, distant, hidden behind a veil of fog, but I could feel its gaze. The Reader was still there, scanning the horizon for plot points.

I walked to the edge of the ink canal. I needed to know where I was. I needed to know if the Author was here.

"Excuse me," I said to a figure passing by. It was a tall, thin man made of paper-mâché, his face a collage of cutout magazine eyes and mouths.

He stopped. He didn't speak, but a speech bubble formed over his head, filled with tiny, frantic typography. *NO spoilers. NO spoilers. The twist is coming.*

"The twist?" I asked. "I'm looking for the ending. I'm looking for the house with the sleeping writer."

The paper man pointed a trembling finger down the canal. He gestured toward a district where the buildings grew crooked, their spines warped and water-damaged.

"The Uncertainty Section," I realized.

I looked for a boat. There were none docked. The ink current was moving sluggishly, thick with sediment.

I heard a sound behind me. A rhythmic *chug-chug-chug.*

I turned.

It was the Tugboat. But it was changed. The rust was gone, replaced by a gleaming coat of white enamel. The funnel was no longer spewing smoke, but steam that smelled of lavender.

The clockwork figure stood at the stern, but it was different now. It was smaller. It wore a conductor's cap. It held a bell rope.

"All aboard," the figure chimed. "Next stop: The Resolution. Or: The Abyss. Pending calculations."

I stepped onto the deck. The boat felt lighter, less substantial.

"Where is the Revision?" I asked. "The boy?"

"Revised," the clockwork said. "Archived. The boy is now the protagonist of Volume II. The Revision has been folded into the narration. You, Keeper, are the remainder. The excess text."

"Excess text?"

"The words that were cut to fit the page limit," it said. "We are sailing toward the Slush Pile."

The boat began to move. It cut through the ink, leaving a wake of pristine white paper behind it.

We sailed deeper into the city. The air grew colder. The buildings loomed closer, blocking out the grey sky.

I saw things in the windows of the book-skyscrapers.

In one window, a woman was sitting at a table, weeping into a bowl of soup. In the next, a man was repeatedly falling off a ladder, climbing up, and falling again, in an endless loop.

"These are the deleted scenes," I said. "The scraps."

"Kafka’s leftovers," the clockwork noted. "Dickens’ discarded orphans."

We passed under a bridge made of solid lead type. *THE END* was stamped into the keystone. But the word *END* was crossed out with thick, black graphite, and the word *BEGINNING* was scrawled beneath it in frantic, shaky handwriting.

"Look ahead," the clockwork warned.

The canal ended.

The boat drifted toward a massive waterfall. The ink cascaded over the edge, plummeting into a void of absolute darkness.

"The Edge of the Page," I said.

"We have to jump," the clockwork said. "The boat dissolves at the falls. It’s a metaphor for letting go."

I looked at the clockwork. "You're not coming?"

"I am machinery," it said. "I function best in repetition. I will turn around and sail back to the Industrial district. I will be a tugboat in a memory."

"Will you remember me?"

The clockwork tapped its glass lenses. "I have no memory. Only ink."

It stopped the boat at the edge of the falls. The roar of the ink cascading into the void was deafening.

"Go," it said.

I stood at the prow. The spray of ink wet my face.

I looked over the edge.

Far below, in the darkness, I saw a light.

Not the beam of the lighthouse. It was a softer light. A warm, yellow square.

A window.

"I am the Reader," I whispered to myself. "I choose the ending."

I jumped.

I fell through the mist of ink. I fell past deleted characters, past unused adjectives, past plot holes that yawned like hungry mouths.

I fell toward the window.

The window grew larger. It became a door.

I hit the ground.

It was a wooden floor. Polished. Clean.

I was in a hallway. A normal hallway. There were photos on the walls—family photos, smiling people in clothes that looked dated but comfortable.

A door stood open to my left. Light spilled out from within. I could hear a sound. A ticking sound.

I walked toward the door. My legs felt heavy, weighted by the gravity of the real world.

I stepped into the room.

It was a study. A messy, beautiful study. Papers were scattered across a desk. A mug of tea sat steaming on a coaster. A cat was sleeping on a windowsill, bathed in actual sunlight.

And at the desk, sat a person.

They were hunched over a notebook, scribbling furiously with a pen. A silver pen.

I stepped closer. The floorboards creaked.

The person froze.

They stopped writing. They set the pen down. Slowly, they turned around in their chair.

It was you.

You were older than I imagined. Your hair was streaked with grey. Your eyes were tired, behind glasses. But it was you. The recipient of every letter I had ever written.

"You," I breathed.

You looked at me. You didn't look surprised. You looked... resigned. You looked like you had been waiting for me to arrive for a very long time.

"I got your letters," you said. Your voice was the voice I had heard in my head for years. "I couldn't answer. I didn't know who sent them."

"I am the Keeper," I said. "From the island."

You nodded. You picked up a folded piece of paper from your desk. It was yellowed, brittle.

"I wrote the island," you said softly. "I wrote the lighthouse when I was a child. It was a refuge. A place to hide."

You looked at me, and your eyes filled with tears.

"I forgot about it," you said. "I grew up. I stopped believing in the light. I left the island unattended."

"That's why the strange things started happening," I said. "The entropy. The ships."

"The dream decaying," you agreed.

You stood up. You reached out a hand.

"Come home," you said. "The story is over. You can wake up now."

I looked at your hand. It was warm. Solid. Real.

Then, I looked at the silver pen on the desk.

It was vibrating.

I looked at the notebook on the desk. The page was open.

There was a drawing on it.

A drawing of the study.

A drawing of the chair.

A drawing of you.

And a drawing of me, standing in the doorway.

But in the drawing, I was fading. I was translucent.

"You aren't writing me home," I realized. "You are writing me out."

You looked at the drawing. You looked at me.

"You were a symptom, Keeper," you said, your voice breaking. "You were the personification of my loneliness. Now I'm not lonely. I have... a life."
