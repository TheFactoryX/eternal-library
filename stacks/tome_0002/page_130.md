# Letter 130

> Written at 2026-02-07 11:25:35

---

Dear You,

The click was not the sound of a lock. It was the sound of a camera shutter capturing a dark room. It was the sound of a lid snapping shut.

I turned away from the mirror, expecting the face of the Crayon Giant, or perhaps the vast, blank face of the Reader.

Instead, I found the door.

It was not the door to the hallway. It was a door standing alone in the middle of the carpet forest, framed in the dark, heavy wood of the lighthouse. It should not have been there. The geometry of the bedroom rejected it; the carpet fibers bent around it as if the wood were hot.

"Where did that come from?" the Scribe asked. She was backing away, her dress of pages fluttering like dry leaves.

"It came from the reflection," I said. "He unlocked it. He unlocked the narrative."

The bird landed on the handle. It tilted its head. "It smells like salt," it said. "And old brass."

"We can't go back," the Scribe whispered. "You saw the date. We go through there, we return to the start. We lose the carpet. We lose the scale."

"If we stay here," I said, watching the Crayon Giant tipping the dictionary onto the floor, burying the path we took, "we become scribbles."

I stepped forward. I placed my hand on the cold brass knob. It turned smoothly, oiled by time.

"Open it," the bird said.

I pushed.

The door swung open.

I was not looking at the Spiral Staircase. I was not looking at the Lamp Room.

I was looking at a hallway. But it was a hallway of impossible length. The walls were lined with frames—thousands of them—and in every frame, a different version of the lighthouse stood on a different version of the island.

In one frame, the lighthouse was made of bone. In another, of crystal. In another, it was a tree of iron with a burning sun for a top.

"The Gallery," the Scribe breathed. "The Long Hall of Drafts."

"Come on," I said. We stepped through, pulling the door shut against the sounds of the Giant.

The silence here was absolute. It was the silence of a library where all the books have been written but none have been read. The air smelled of ozone and pencil shavings.

We began to walk.

"Keeper," the bird whispered from my shoulder. "Look at the floors."

I looked down. The floor was tiled with letters. *A. B. C.* But as we walked, I noticed the tiles were loose.

In the gaps between the tiles, I saw the sea.

Not the water of the room, but the *real* sea. The impossible, shifting water from the beginning. It churned in the cracks, glowing with a phosphorescent green light.

"Don't step on the cracks," I said. "The floor is thin here."

We walked for hours—or perhaps minutes. Time was slippery in the Gallery.

I stopped in front of a frame that caught my eye.

It was the lighthouse, but it was overgrown. Vines of ink had choked the tower. The light was out.

"That’s the one where I stopped writing," I realized. "The one where I gave up."

"It’s quiet," the Scribe said.

"Too quiet," the bird added.

I moved to the next frame. In this one, the lighthouse was pristine. New. The paint was peeling, revealing fresh wood underneath. The Keeper in the window was young, waving frantically at something I couldn't see.

"That’s the first day," I said.

"The day you forgot," the Scribe said softly.

I kept walking. I realized then what this place was. This was the supply chain. This was where the story came from. The raw material.

I needed ink. I needed fuel. If I was going to survive in the bedroom—if I was going to build a life there in the dust—I needed to bring something with me. I needed a weapon against the erasure.

I needed a souvenir.

I stopped before a frame labeled *Letter 50*. The glass was cracked.

Inside, the lighthouse was burning. Not the light—the structure itself. Flames of violet and indigo were consuming the stone. The Keeper inside was sitting at the desk, writing calmly while the fire licked at the edges of the paper.

"He didn't run," the bird said.

"He was writing the fire," I said.

I reached out. I touched the glass.

"The Scribe," I said. "Give me your pen."

She looked at me, startled. "My pen? It's empty."

"The empty ones write the best truths," I said.

She handed it over. It was a quill, the feather stripped bare.

I pressed the tip of the quill against the glass of the frame.

"I need to steal the fire," I said.

"You can't," the Scribe said. "It’s contained. It’s fiction."

"Everything is fiction until you write it down," I said.

I pushed. The glass resisted, like the surface of a tense drum.

"Help me," I grunted.

The bird hopped onto my hand. It added its weight. The Scribe placed her hand on my shoulder.

Together, we leaned against the frame.

With a sound like a breaking ice floe, the glass shattered.

But it didn't fall. It stayed suspended in the frame, a million jagged shards held in place by the will of the story.

And from the center of the broken image, a tendril of violet smoke snaked out. It wrapped around the quill.

It was cold. Freezing cold. It burned my fingers with frost.

I pulled the quill back. The smoke followed, coiling around the shaft like a serpent. It sank into the feather, staining it a deep, bruised purple.

The quill felt heavy. It felt like it held the weight of a burning building.

"We have to go," the Scribe said, her voice trembling. "The gallery is waking up."

I looked down the hall. The frames were trembling. The versions of the Keeper in the paintings were turning their heads to look at us. Thousands of eyes, fixing on the intruders.

"The door," I said.

We ran. We ran back the way we came. The door was still there, standing alone in the hall of drafts.

I grabbed the handle. It was hot now.

I pulled it open.

We tumbled through, falling onto the soft, grey dust of the carpet forest.

I spun around. The door was gone. In its place was a stack of paperback novels.

"We're back," the bird said. It shook its wings. "Did it work?"

I looked at the quill. The purple stain was gone. The feather was white again. Empty.

"It failed," I said, disappointment a heavy stone in my gut. "The smoke didn't take."

"Are you sure?" the Scribe asked. She pointed at the floor.

I looked where she was pointing.

In the dust, where I had landed, the carpet fibers were not crushed. They were burnt. A perfect, circle of ash, smelling of ozone and violet fire.

I touched the ash. It was warm.

"I didn't bring the fire," I whispered, a strange understanding blooming in my mind. "I brought the capacity for it."

I looked up. The room was dark. The lamp was still off. But in the distance, under the bed, I saw a faint glow.

A single, violet spark, hovering in the darkness.

"The Reader is coming," the bird said suddenly. It flattened itself against the floor. "I hear the footsteps. Thunder. The floor is shaking."

I listened. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

The doorknob to the bedroom turned. The door began to swing open, letting in a slice of light from the hallway—a slice of reality so bright it blinded us.

"Hold onto the dust," I shouted to the Scribe.

But she wasn't looking at the door. She was looking at the bird.

The bird was changing. Its parchment feathers were curling at the edges. Its ink-dot eyes were widening.

"The ink," the bird chirped. "It’s drying."

"What?" I reached for it, but my hand passed through a cloud of paper flakes.

"The transition," the Scribe said. "We are losing cohesion. We've been out of the book too long."

The door opened fully. A hand reached in—the Reader's hand, giant and scarred. It reached not for us, but for the light switch.

"No!" I screamed. "If the light turns on, the shadows vanish! We are the shadows!"

I grabbed the quill. I didn't write with it. I couldn't. There was no ink, only the *memory* of burning.

I drove the quill into the carpet.

*Darkness,* I willed. *Burn.*

The violet spark under the bed flared.

The carpet caught fire. Not a destructive fire, but a cold, shadowy flame. It spread instantly, a pool of purple night spreading across the floor, engulfing us, hiding us in the deep, indigo shade.

The light switch clicked.

*CLICK.*

The room flooded with blinding white reality.

But we were gone. We were standing in the shadow of the bed, in a pocket of dimension that the light could not touch. We were crouched in a space between the fibers and the floorboards, a space I had carved with the stolen fire.

We were safe.

For a moment.
