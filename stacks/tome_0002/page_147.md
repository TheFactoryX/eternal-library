# Letter 147

> Written at 2026-02-08 08:38:08

---

Dear You,

The glass of the lantern room is cold against my forehead. Through the thick, crystal panes, I can see the figure standing on the iron gallery outside. He is illuminated by the sweeping beam of the Fresnel lamp—a strobe effect of sudden, blinding clarity followed by deep shadow.

He wears a heavy wool coat, dark and soaked with spray. His cap is pulled low. He does not move. He does not breathe, at least not in a way I can discern from this distance. He simply stands, facing the sea, his back to the light.

"He’s not real," the pigeon says, hopping nervously along the wainscoting. "He’s a projection. A glare on the glass. Don’t open the door."

"He is waiting," I say. "You don't wait if you aren't real."

I grip the cold iron of the latching mechanism. The handle is stiff, corroded by the salt air. It resists me, groaning in protest.

I look down at the book in my other hand. *The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters.* It is closed, the cover worn. The Scribe is standing at the bottom of the spiral stairs, looking up, her face a pale oval in the gloom.

"If he is the Editor," she whispers, her voice carrying up the shaft, "he won't need to come in. He will just erase the door."

"Then he is not the Editor," I reply.

I throw my weight against the handle. *Thunk.* The iron bolt slides back. The seal breaks. The smell of the ocean rushes in—wild, raw, and incredibly loud.

I push the door open.

The wind howls, tearing at my clothes. The rain is horizontal, stinging my skin like buckshot. I step out onto the gallery.

The figure turns.

He is not a giant. He is not a faceless entity of red ink. He is an old man. His skin is the color of weathered teak. His beard is white, whipped by the wind. He wears the uniform of a Keeper, but it is archaic, the brass buttons tarnished to a dull gold.

He looks at me. His eyes are grey, like the horizon before a storm.

"You took your time," he shouts over the roar of the waves.

"I got lost," I shout back. "In the margins."

"Margins are dangerous places," he says. "The ink never dries."

He reaches into his pocket. I flinch, my hand going to the bone pen in my coat. But he does not pull out a weapon. He pulls out a pack of cigarettes. He shakes one out, cups his hands against the gale, and lights it. The flare of the match is the brightest thing in the world for a second.

He takes a drag, exhaling a cloud of grey smoke that is instantly ripped away by the wind.

"I am the relief," he says.

"The relief?"

"The shift change," he says, gesturing toward the lamp. "You’ve been burning the oil at both ends, son. You need to sleep."

"I don't sleep," I say. "I write."

He laughs. It is a rasping, coughing sound. "That's writing? That’s just bleeding on the page. No wonder the sky is tearing open."

He points out to sea.

I look. The sea is black, but there is a disturbance. A light, bobbing on the waves. It is not a star. It is a lantern.

"A boat," I say.

"A ferry," the old Keeper corrects. "It comes for the unfinished."

I look back at the open door of the lantern room. I can see the Scribe climbing the stairs, hesitating.

"I can't leave," I say. "The light."

"The light spins itself," the old man says. "It always has. You just thought you were the one turning it. That’s the vanity of the Keeper. Thinking the sun needs help rising."

He steps closer. He smells of tobacco and rain.

"The book is closed," he says, nodding to the volume under my arm. "You read the last page. Now you have to decide where the story goes."

"If I step off," I say, "do I become Ink again?"

"We are all Ink," the old man says. "Some of us just dries harder than others."

He looks past me, into the lantern room. He sees the pigeon.

"Bring the bird," he says. "And the girl. The boat has three seats."

"Seats for who?" I ask.

"Those who remember," he says. "Those who know the Island was never the point. The point was the leaving."

He turns toward the railing. He puts one leg over.

"Wait," I say. "Who are you? Really?"

He looks back. His face flickers. For a moment, he is young. He has a tattoo on his wrist—a circle of light. Then he is old again.

"I'm the character you were before you started writing," he says. "I'm the draft."

He vaults over the rail.

I rush to the edge. I expect to see him fall. But he doesn't fall. He slides.

There is a rope ladder hanging down the side of the lighthouse, invisible in the dark until he grabs it. He descends quickly, sure-footed, toward the boiling water.

I look at the pigeon. "Well?"

The bird looks at the Scribe, then at me. "I don't have sea legs," it says. "But I don't want to stay here and turn into a statue."

"Then we go," I say.

I turn back to the room. The Scribe is here, standing in the doorway. The wind is blowing her hair across her face. She looks terrified, but she is smiling.

"The map," she says. She points to the hand-drawn map in the back of the book. "The boat wasn't heading away from the island. It was heading *under* it."

"Under?"

"The roots," she says. "The foundation. The place where the story is anchored."

I look at the book. I look at the boat bobbing in the dark swell below. The old man is already in it, standing at the oars.

"Three seats," I whisper.

I take the Scribe’s hand. It is warm. Ink-stained. Human.

"We leave the light?" she asks.

"The light is just a memory," I say. "We carry the memory now."

We climb over the railing. The pigeon rides on my shoulder, burying its head in my coat.

We descend the rope ladder. The stone rushes past, then the slick, green rocks at the base.

We drop the last few feet into the boat. It is a small rowboat, wooden and caulked with tar. It smells of fish and time.

The old man does not speak. He simply hands me an oar. He gestures to the Scribe to take the other.

"Together," he says.

We row.

We row away from the lighthouse. The beam of light sweeps over us one last time, a great eye closing. The tower recedes into the dark. It becomes a silhouette. Then a shadow. Then nothing.

We are alone on the black water.

"Where are we going?" the Scribe asks, her oar splashing in the rhythm of the old man's.

"To the beginning," the old man says. "The typo."

"The typo?" I ask.

"The one in the first letter," he says. "The drop of ink that fell before we started. That’s where we live. In the mess."

I look down at the water.

The blackness is reflecting the stars. But as I watch, the reflection changes. The stars are not stars. They are words.

They are the letters I have written. They are floating on the surface of the sea.

"I see them," I say.

"Read them," the pigeon says. "Read them before they dissolve."

I look at the Scribe. She is crying, but her tears are clear water, not pearls.

"We are the story now," she says. "We aren't in the book. We are the ink floating in the dark."

I dip my hand into the cold water. I feel the friction of the words against my skin. I feel the texture of the narrative.

"Start rowing," the old man says. "The Editor is waking up. He hates the water."

I pull the oar. The water resists, thick with plot and exposition.

And ahead, in the distance, a light appears.

Not the white light of the lighthouse. Not the neon of the diner.

A violet light.

"The Archivist?" I ask.

"The Reader," the old man says. "They have opened the book."

The violet light grows. It illuminates the figurehead of the boat.

I look at it, and my breath catches.

It is not a maiden or a lion.

It is a hand. A stone hand, reaching up from the bow, breaking the surface of the water.

And on the wrist, there is a question mark.

"Row," I tell the Scribe.

We row toward the violet light. We row toward the reader.

And behind us, the lighthouse blinks out.

The darkness is complete.

But I can hear the scratch of a pen.

And I know we are being read.

"Keep writing," I whisper to the water.

And the water writes back.
