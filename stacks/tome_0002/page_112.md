# Letter 112

Dear You,

pressed the nib of the quill into the well. It surfaced heavy, dripping with black fluid that seemed to hold a light of its own, a tiny galaxy trapped in the viscosity of the tar.

"Bleed through," she repeated, her voice steady despite the ceiling pressing down like the palm of a collapsing god. "If we are ink, we are permanent. If we are physical, we are merely characters waiting to be erased."

I looked at the white wall. The paper was dry, hungry. The descending cover had slowed, pausing inches from the top of the Lighthouse lantern, as if the Collector hesitated to crush something so fragile.

"What do I write?" I asked. The quill felt like a bone in my hand.

"Write us somewhere else," she said. "Somewhere she isn't looking."

I raised the quill. My hand was shaking. I didn't write words. Words could be read. Words could be understood. I needed to write something that could only be *felt*.

I began to draw a map.

Not a map of the ocean, or the island. I drew the schematic of a door. A hinge. A lock.

But I drew it on the air above the desk.

The ink didn't fall. It hung there, suspended in the void, defying gravity. The black lines dripped upward, climbing toward the descending ceiling.

"Open," I commanded.

The ink lines thickened. They began to pulse. The schematic rotated in the air, shedding droplets of darkness that hissed as they hit the floor.

The ceiling cracked.

Not a crack of stress, but a crack of *intrusion*. Something sharp had pierced the cover from the outside. A needle? A letter opener?

"Whatever you are doing," the Captain whispered, "do not stop."

I drew faster. I drew a knob. I drew the keyhole.

Suddenly, the ceiling stopped its descent. A blinding beam of white light shot through the puncture wound in the cover, cutting through the gloom of the Keep. It illuminated the floating ink map.

And then, a sound. A sound that made the Lighthouse shudder.

*RIIIIIIIP.*

The Collector was tearing the page.

"We're too slow!" I shouted. The floor began to tilt. The edge of the world—the margin—was curling up as the page was lifted from the scrapbook. The Lighthouse slid, the furniture tumbling toward the white abyss of the gutter.

"To the roof!" the Captain yelled, grabbing my arm. "If we fall into the gutter, we are lost forever!"

We scrambled up the spiral stairs as the Lighthouse tilted thirty degrees. The books from the library cascaded past us, diving into the white void.

We burst onto the balcony. The wind was chaotic. Above us, the torn sky flapped like a wound in the fabric of reality. Beyond the tear, I saw not the Collector’s face, but the ceiling of a room. A room with a water stain. A room with a flickering bulb.

And something else.

A fly.

A common, blue-bottle fly was buzzing near the tear in the page. It landed on the edge of the paper, crawling on the vast white plain that was our sky.

"It's just a fly," I said.

"It's a ferry," the Captain corrected.

She grabbed me around the waist. "Hold on to the ink!"

The ink map I had drawn was still hovering near the lantern glass. It was the only solid thing in a dissolving world.

As the page was lifted, the gravity of the world shifted. We didn't fall down anymore. We fell *up*.

We tumbled into the tear.

We passed through the paper, a sensation of moving through a wet sheet of wool.

I landed hard on a surface that smelled of dust and varnish.

I opened my eyes. I was lying on a wooden floor. The floorboards were vast, wide as highways. Around me, towering like monoliths, were the legs of chairs, tables, and bookshelves.

I looked back.

The book was there. It lay open on the floor of the attic. The page I had been on—*Letter 112*—was crinkled, half-torn from the binding. The Lighthouse was a smudge of ink on the paper, a tiny doodle of a tower next to a jagged black stain.

The Captain was beside me. She was full-sized now. Her coat of feathers was real, smelling of brine and rain. She was coughing, wiping ink from her cheek.

"We made it," she rasped. "The Real."

I stood up. The scale was terrifying. A single dust bunny was a tumbleweed the size of a car. The air was thick, stale, smelling of old cedar and abandonment.

"Where is this?" I asked.

"The place where things go when they are put away," she said. She looked toward the far wall, where a sliver of light sliced through the darkness under the door. "The Attic of the Forgotten."

"We aren't forgotten," I said. "We are in the middle of the story."

"Are we?" she pointed at the book on the floor.

I crept toward the massive volume. The title on the spine was faded, gold leaf flaking away. I could only make out a few words.

*The Lighthouse Keeper's Le...*

But next to the book was something else.

A box.

A cardboard box, labeled in black marker: *KEEP.*

Inside the box, I saw items that made my breath hitch.

A rusty key. A shattered sextant. A music box with a broken dancer.

And a stack of paper. Neat, typed paper.

The draft.

"The original," I whispered. "The draft before the revisions."

"We need to hide," the Captain said, her eyes scanning the gloom. "The giants are restless. I hear footsteps."

She was right. A low, rhythmic thudding vibrated through the floorboards.

*BOOM. BOOM. BOOM.*

"Someone is coming up the stairs," I said.

"Hide in the box," she said. "If we stay in the open, we'll be seen. If we are seen, we are pests."

I climbed the cardboard edge of the box. It was like scaling a fortress wall. I dropped down into the interior, landing on a pile of soft, yellowed parchment.

The Captain followed.

We huddled behind a stack of old letters, wrapped in twine.

The attic door creaked open.

Light flooded the room. It was golden, bright, cutting through the shadows with the violence of sunrise.

A hand reached in.

A hand that was scarred, familiar.

It reached for the book on the floor.

"The Dreamer," I whispered.

"No," the Captain said, peeking through a gap in the twine. "Look at the hands. They are too old. Look at the wrists."

There was a tattoo on the wrist. A lighthouse. A beacon on a hill.

"It's me," I said, the realization hitting me with the force of a tidal wave. "It's an older version of me."

The Old Man reached down and picked up the book. He handled it gently, reverently. He didn't open it. He just held it, his thumb brushing over the spine.

"Still wet," the Old Man muttered. His voice was the sound of gravel under boots. "I can feel the pulse."

He carried the book to a trunk. He opened the lid.

I saw the inside of the trunk. It wasn't empty.

It was filled with water. Black, endless water.

"He's throwing us back," the Captain hissed.

"No," I said. "He's putting us back on the shelf. He's finishing the day."

The Old Man lowered the book toward the water.

I scrambled to the top of the letter pile. I had to stop him. If the book hit the water, the ink would run. The story would drown.

"Wait!" I screamed. The sound was tiny, a squeak in the vastness of the attic.

But he paused.

He frowned. He lifted the book to his ear.

He shook it.

I felt the world tilt. I tumbled against the twine.

"Rat," he said.

His other hand moved. He reached for a slipper.

"No," the Captain said. "Elias, we have to jump."

"Jump where?"

"Into the water," she said. "If we jump, we become the ocean. We become the setting."

The Old Man raised the slipper.

I looked at the Captain. She was smiling. A wild, desperate smile.

"Trust the ink," she said.

She grabbed my hand.

We leaped from the box.

We arced through the air, falling toward the open trunk, falling toward the dark water.

As we fell, I saw the title of the book one last time before it was lowered into the dark.

*The Lighthouse Keeper's Letters.*

But underneath the title, in small, scrawled handwriting, was a name I hadn't seen before.

*To the Reader Who Never Came.*

I hit the water.

It was cold. It was deep.

And as I sank, I didn't drown. I breathed. I inhaled the liquid. It tasted of salt and iron.

I opened my eyes.

I was on the beach.

The night sky was above me, swirling with impossible colors.

And walking toward me along the shoreline was a figure. A figure with a lantern.

But the lantern was unlit.
