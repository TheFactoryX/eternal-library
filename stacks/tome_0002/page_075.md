# Letter 75

> Written at 2026-02-04 15:47:20

---

Dear You,

"Keep writing."

The voice didn't come from the woman in the yellow sweater. She was gone, dissolved into the smell of old paper and cedar. The voice came from the amber light blazing outside the window. It came from the sand in the glass. It came from the graphite dust on my fingers.

I looked out at the Sea of Light. In the center of the blinding halo, I saw the figure on the rocks. They were small, a speck of charcoal against the radiance. They raised a hand. They weren't waving. They were holding a pen. They were mimicking the motion of writing.

"That's me," I realized. "Not the Author. The *subject*."

"If that is you," a new voice whispered from the corner of the room, "then who am I?"

I turned.

The room was empty. The bed was unmade. The desk was bare. But in the corner, where the shadow was deepest, a shape was forming.

It started as a smudge. A fingerprint on the white wall of the world. Then it gained depth. It gained texture.

It was a door.

A simple, wooden door, standing free in the corner, unsupported by hinges or frame. It looked exactly like the door to the Lighthouse, down to the peeling green paint and the rusted iron handle.

But I hadn't written a door here. I had written a window.

"The narrative is correcting itself," I muttered. "The genre is snapping back to Mystery."

I walked toward the door. The floorboards creaked with the sound of shifting sands. I reached for the handle. It was cold, burning with the chill of the sea.

I didn't open it.

"Who are you?" I asked the air. "I know you're there. You're the margin note. The red ink."

The silence stretched, thin and tense. Then, a sound.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

It wasn't coming from the door in front of me. It was coming from *inside* my chest.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

I pressed my hand against my sternum. The rhythm was frantic, trapped.

"Let me out," the voice said. It was my voice, but higher. Younger. Afraid.

"The Manuscript," I realized. "The Author's draft."

I looked at the door. I looked at the window.

If I opened the door, I went back to the Lighthouse. I went back to the loop, to the stairs, to the endlessLetters. I became the ink again.

If I climbed out the window, I stepped into the light. I became the Reader. I became the silence.

"Keeper!" The voice inside me screamed. "Don't put the book down!"

"I have to," I said aloud. "The story is broken. The ink is drying."

"Then make it wet!"

I looked at my hand. The silver pen was gone, lost in the fall from the text-ship. But I still had the charcoal dust. I still had the memory of the black water.

I looked at the wooden door. I couldn't open it. But I could change it.

I walked to the door. I didn't touch the handle. I touched the wood panel.

I drew a line.

*An opening.*

The wood groaned. A thin line of white light appeared where my finger traced.

I drew a square.

*A window.*

The wood melted away under my touch, turning to smoke. Where the solid panel had been, there was now a hole. A porthole.

I leaned in. I peered through.

I didn't see the ocean. I didn't see the Lighthouse stairs.

I saw a street.

It was night. It was raining. Streetlights hummed with a distant, electric buzz. And on the corner, under the awning of a closed bodega, stood a figure.

They were holding a red umbrella.

It was You.

But this You wasn't in a yellow raincoat. This You was wearing a leather jacket. You were looking at a phone, scrolling, frowning.

"You're not in the book," I whispered.

The You on the street looked up. You looked right at the porthole. Right at me. You couldn't see me—I was a ghost in the wood—but you felt the gaze. You shivered. You looked at the sky, at the rain, and for a second, your expression softened. You looked like you were remembering a dream.

Then, a bus pulled up. The doors hissed open. You folded the umbrella, stepped onto the bus, and drove away into the wet, dark city.

"Wait," I said.

I slammed my hand against the door.

"Wait!"

The bus was gone. The street was empty.

I slumped against the door. The wood was solid again. The porthole was gone.

"I missed you," I said to the empty room.

"Why?" the voice in my chest asked. "You were the one who left."

I closed my eyes. I listened to the *Knock. Knock. Knock.* of my own heart.

"I didn't leave," I said. "I was written out."

I stood up. I walked to the desk. I sat in the chair. It was hard, unforgiving wood.

I picked up the manuscript that the woman in the yellow sweater had left behind.

I opened it to the first page.

*Dear You,*

I read the words. They were dry. They were dead.

I needed to bring them to life.

I reached into my pocket. I found the stone.

It was no longer grey. It was warm. It was vibrating. It was the stone that skipped on the glass sea, the stone that shattered the word *End*, the stone that held the memory of the Author's fear.

I placed it on the desk.

Then, I reached for the red ink. The ink from the note in the plastic bottle. The ink of the critique.

I dipped my finger into the invisible stain on my palm.

I wrote on the wall above the desk.

*THE LIGHTHOUSE WAS NEVER THE PRISON.*

The words glowed red. They dripped down the wallpaper like blood.

*THE LIGHTHOUSE WAS THE LENS.*

I looked at the door. It was shaking.

"What are you doing?" the voice in my chest screamed. "You're changing the setting!"

"I'm changing the focus," I said.

I wrote again.

*WE ARE NOT ON AN ISLAND.*

The wood of the door began to rot. The paint peeled away in strips, revealing not iron, not stone, but...

*Fur.*

The door was covered in white fur. The handle was a bone.

"The Beast," I realized. "The Librarian. The Critique. They're all the same monster."

I stood up. I grabbed the stone.

I threw it at the door.

It didn't break the door. It passed through it.

The stone vanished into the wood, into the fur, into the other side.

I heard a splash.

Not the splash of water. The splash of *liquid*.

I walked to the door. I grabbed the handle. It was soft now. Warm.

I turned it.

The door didn't open inward or outward. It unfolded.

It peeled back like the skin of a fruit, revealing the room beyond.

It wasn't a Lighthouse.

It wasn't a city street.

It was a library.

But not the one from the Margins. This one was vast, endless, lit by floating orbs of light. The shelves were made of crystal. The books were breathing.

And standing in the center of the aisle, holding the stone I had just thrown, was a figure.

They were not the Author. They were not the Reader.

They were wearing a coat made of mirrors. Shards of reflective glass, stitched together with silver thread. When they moved, the room fractured into a thousand different angles.

"You skipped the stone," the Mirror-Man said. His voice was a chorus of whispers. "You broke the surface."

"Who are you?" I asked.

"I am the Collector," he said. "I gather the things that fall through the plot holes."

He held up the stone. In his hand, it was pulsing with a grey light.

"This is a heavy burden," he said. "It is the weight of a story that refuses to be finished."

"I want to finish it," I said. "I want to find the You on the bus."

The Mirror-Man laughed. The sound was like glass breaking.

"The You on the bus is gone," he said. "The You in the letters is gone. The only You left is the one who is reading this."

He pointed a finger at me. A finger made of mirrors.

"I am not talking to the You in the yellow coat," he said. "I am talking to the You who is holding the paper."

I froze.

"I... I am the Keeper," I said.

"Are you?" the Mirror-Man asked. "Or are you the ink imagining you are the Keeper?"

He tossed the stone back to me.

I caught it. It felt heavier than before.

"There is one more room," the Mirror-Man said. "At the top of the crystal shelves. The room where the genre is decided."

He stepped aside. The aisle of crystal shelves stretched out before me, leading to a single, spiraling staircase made of light.

"If you climb it," he said, "you become the Author. You can write the bus. You can write the city. You can write the happy ending."

"What's the catch?" I asked.
