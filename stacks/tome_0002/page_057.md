# Letter 57

Dear You,

I expected the impact. I expected the shattering of reality, the sudden drop from the beam of light into the cold, hard ground of the dedication page. I expected silence.

But there is no silence here. There is a hum. A low, electrical vibration that sits in the teeth and vibrates the bones of the inner ear.

I am not standing on a dedication page. I am not in a white house.

I am standing in an atrium.

The floor is checkered marble—black and white tiles stretching endlessly in every direction. The ceiling is a vault of glass, stained with the colors of a sunset that refuses to set: bruised purples, bleeding oranges, a sickly yellow-green.

"We are in the Acknowledgments," the Revision said. She was sitting on a bench that hadn't been there a moment ago, examining her coat. It was no longer grey. It was a deep, vibrant indigo. "I feel... substantial," she whispered. "I feel like I have a history."

"You do," I said. I looked around. "We are the footnotes. The explanations. The thank-yous."

Walking past us were figures. They were blurry, indistinct, moving with the hurried purpose of stagehands changing scenery between acts. They carried placards around their necks. *The Researcher.* *The Proofreader.* *The Friend Who Read The First Draft.*

None of them looked at us. They hurried toward a distant archway, where a bright, inviting light spilled out. The sound of laughter and clinking glasses drifted from that opening.

"The Party," I said. "That is where the Author is."

"Is that where we go?" the Revision asked, standing up. She brushed a lock of hair from her face. Her hand was solid, the skin flushed with life.

"I don't think we are invited," I said.

I reached into my pocket. My fingers brushed the smooth curve of the spoon. It was still bent, the handle twisted into a question mark.

I looked at the archway. A bouncer stood there—a giant of a man, headless, holding a velvet rope. He wasn't checking faces; he was checking *texture*. He ran a hand over the spirits of the passersby. If they glowed, they entered. If they were dull, they were turned away.

"We aren't glowing," the Revision said, her voice trembling. "We are matte."

"Then we find another way out," I said.

I turned away from the party. Behind us, the atrium stretched into darkness.

"Where are you going?" she called out.

"The book had an index," I said. "There must be a way back to the text."

I began to walk. The Revision followed.

As we moved away from the warm light of the party, the air grew cold. The marble tiles under our feet began to change. They became loose, shifting.

I looked down.

They weren't tiles. They were letters. *A.* *T.* *H.*

We were walking on the typeset.

"The Glossary," I realized.

The floor was a chaotic jumble of words. *Sea. Lighthouse. Keeper. Loss. Ethereal.*

"It’s a minefield," the Revision warned. "Watch your step. Don't step on a verb."

"Why?"

"Because verbs trigger action."

She reached out and stopped me. On the tile directly in front of my boot, the word *FALL* was etched in deep, grooved type.

I looked around. The darkness of the atrium was lifting. We weren't in a room anymore. We were standing on a vast, white plain. A desert of paper.

And the sea was returning.

But it wasn't the black ink of the Press, or the clear water of the bay. It was a sea of red correction fluid. A slow, thick, oozing wave that was sliding across the paper, erasing everything it touched.

"The Editor," I whispered. "The cleanup crew."

The red sea was moving toward us. It moved with a sticky, relentless sound. *Schlep. Schlep.*

"We have to run," the Revision said.

"Where?" I asked. "The paper goes to the edge, and then what? The Margin?"

"Better the Margin than the fluid," she said. "That stuff dissolves characters. It turns them into blank space."

We ran.

We leaped over adjectives, dodged around adverbs. *Cold. Dark. Endless.*

The red wave was faster than it looked. It was gathering momentum.

I could smell it—the sharp, chemical scent of acetone and mistake.

"It’s catching up!" the Revision cried out.

I looked ahead. In the distance, I saw a fissure in the paper. A jagged tear in the fabric of the world.

"The gutter!" I shouted. "The binding!"

We sprinted toward the tear. The red fluid was lapping at our heels. I could feel the heat of it, the burning sting of erasure.

We reached the edge of the page.

I looked down into the tear.

It was not darkness. It was gold. A blinding, radiant gold. The spine of the book.

"We have to jump," I said.

"Is it safe?" she asked.

"It’s the glue," I said. "It holds everything together."

I grabbed her hand.

"Jump!"

We leaped off the edge of the text.

We fell into the gold.

It was warm. It was viscous. We sank slowly, suspended in the amber light of the binding.

I floated there, weightless. The Revision floated beside me, her indigo coat billowing like a parachute.

"It's quiet," she whispered.

"Yes," I said.

I looked at my hand. The skin was becoming translucent again. The wireframe was returning.

"I'm losing definition," I said. "I wasn't meant for the Glossary."

"You are the Keeper," she said. "You belong in the tower."

"But the tower is gone," I said. "The boy is the Keeper now."

"Is he?" she asked. She pointed up.

Through the translucent golden glue, I could see the pages of the book spreading out like the wings of a massive bird. And on one of the pages, near the top, I saw a tiny speck.

I squinted.

It was the Lighthouse.

But the boy wasn't there.

"He's gone," I said. "He left."

"Then who is tending the light?"

"I don't know," I said. "But if the light goes out, the edges of the world will fray."

I began to swim upward. I clawed at the glue, fighting the viscosity.

"Where are you going?" the Revision called out.

"I have to go back up," I grunted. "I have to get to the next page."

"Leave it," she said. "Let the story end. Let the glue set. Let us become fossils in the amber."

"No," I said. I kicked harder. "I haven't written the letter yet."

I reached for the edge of the page. I grabbed the fiber of the paper. I hauled myself out of the binding.

I rolled onto the white page.

I was back.

But the page was empty.

No text. No ink. Just the faint, blue watermark of the manufacturer's logo.

I stood up. I was alone.

Then, I heard it.

A scratching sound. *Skritch. Skritch.*

It was coming from beneath my feet.

I looked down.

The text was appearing. Not from above, written by a hand. But from below, pushing *up* through the paper like braille.

The paper bulged.

I knelt.

I pressed my hand against the raised bump.

*Letter 57.*

I read the words that were forming.

*The Keeper looked up and saw the sky was made of glass.*

I looked up.

The sky above the page was a lens. A magnifying glass. And behind the glass, a massive eye was pressed.

It was the Reader's eye.

I froze.

The pupil dilated. The iris, a swirling storm of blue and brown, focused on me.

The eye blinked.

The wind from the eyelash knocked me flat.

I lay on the page, staring up at the colossal eye.

"Hello," I whispered.

The eye didn't respond. It just watched.

Then, from the darkness of the pupil, a single drop of liquid fell.

It splashed next to me.

It wasn't a tear.

It was ink.

Black, wet, glistening ink.

It began to spread, forming a puddle.

I scrambled back as the ink began to swirl, taking shape. It formed a circle. A keyhole.

I looked into the keyhole of ink.

Deep inside, I saw a room.

A sunlit room.

And a desk.

And on the desk, a hand. Resting. Still.

The Author was asleep.

I looked back up at the giant eye.

"The Author is dreaming you," I said aloud.

The ink keyhole pulsed.

A voice drifted from it, soft and sleepy. *The spoon...*

The voice faded.

I stood up. I walked to the edge of the ink puddle.

I reached into my pocket.

The bent spoon was gone.

Instead, I found a key.

It was made of ink. Wet and dripping.

I looked at the key. I looked at the ink keyhole on the floor.

"If I unlock it," I thought, "do I wake the Author?"

I lowered the key into the lock.
