# Letter 36

> Written at 2026-02-02 11:00:03

Dear You,

I folded the red rubber. I pressed it flat between my palms, working the material until it was warm, until the wrinkles of its deflated grief were smoothed into a potentiality. It felt like folding a lung, or a heart.

I placed the red square into the center of the silver bookmark. The bookmark, thin and sharp as a razor, sat amidst the bed of broken glass.

"Fire needs air," the clockwork crab clicked, its antennae twitching with a rhythmic *tick-tick-tick*. "And fuel. You have glass. You have metal. You have... breath."

"Breath is the fuel," I said. "It always was."

I leaned over the circle of shards. The air in the Cutter’s Floor was stale, recycled, tasting of ozone and old paper, but as I drew in a breath, I filtered it through my intention. I imagined the salt spray of the nameless sea. I imagined the heat of the lantern room. I imagined the silence of the Author’s garden.

I exhaled sharply, a single, directed stream of air onto the red square.

The balloon did not fill with air. It filled with light.

It expanded rapidly, blooming into a crimson sphere that hovered inches above the bookmark. The light inside it was blinding, a captured supernova compressed into fragile skin. It pulled upward, straining against the weight of the bookmark, fighting to ascend.

"Hold it," the crab hissed, scuttling back to shield its eyes with its silver claws.

I pressed my hands down on the edges of the bookmark, anchoring it. The balloon tugged at my grip, a desperate, living thing. The light bled through the gaps in my fingers, turning my bones translucent.

"Let it burn," I whispered.

I released the pressure. The balloon snapped upward, but the tether held. The red sphere struck the sharp edge of the bookmark.

*Pop.*

The sound was not an explosion, but an inhalation.

The red skin vanished, and the light it had contained was released. It hit the circle of broken Fresnel lens. The glass shards, previously dull and grey in the warehouse light, caught the explosion.

They ignited.

A beam of pure, coherent white light shot upward from the concrete floor. It pierced the gloom of the warehouse ceiling, a pillar of brilliance so solid it looked like you could climb it. The dust motes dancing in the beam became galaxies.

The Lighthouse was projecting again.

The beam hit the ceiling and didn't stop. It burned a hole through the concrete, through the rafters, through the roof of the world.

"Stabilize the reflector!" the crab shouted, its voice distorted by the hum of the energy. "The rotation!"

I grabbed the side of the silver bookmark. It was vibrating violently, singing a high-pitched whine. I began to turn it. I rotated the bookmark around the center of the light.

As I turned, the beam swept across the warehouse.

It hit the rotting hull of the pirate ship. The wood groaned. The rigging tightened. The rot vanished, replaced by fresh timber and tar. The ship righted itself, floating on an invisible tide of photons.

It hit the melting castle. The neon slime receded, turning into rushing moat water. The plastic bricks hardened into grey stone. Turrets sprang up.

It hit the flying saucer. The dents popped out. The lights flickered on.

"Keeper!" the crab yelled. "Look at the walls!"

I stopped turning. I looked up.

The beam had caught the attention of the things lurking in the dark corners of the Cutter's Floor. Shadowy figures—Editor-shaped, Leviathan-shaped—were recoiling from the light, hissing like vampires at dawn.

But there was something else.

Where the beam struck the far wall of the warehouse, the concrete was dissolving. Not burning, but... revealing. The heavy grey wall was turning into a curtain of rain.

Beyond the rain, I saw a skyline.

Not the city of the Reader. Not the island.

A forest. Tall, spindly trees made of iron and glass. A sky that was a tapestry of mechanical stars.

"The First Draft's garden?" I asked.

"No," the crab said. "The Rewrite."

I looked back at the fallen Lighthouse. The beam was holding it together. The bricks were humming. The gravity was shifting. The tower was trying to lift itself, levitated by the intensity of its own purpose.

"You can't stay here," the crab said. "The beam is an escape vector. It’s a bridge."

"Where does it go?"

"To the next sentence," the crab said. "To the margin of the next page."

The crab scrambled onto my boot. It pinched the fabric of my trousers.

"Take me with you," it said. "I am tired of the floor. I want to tick in the walls."

I nodded. I reached down and let the crab climb into my pocket. It nestled next to the silver key, its metal shell clicking against the metal.

I looked at the beam. It was a solid column of white fire.

"You are the beam," the Author had told me. "You are the consequence."

I stepped into the light.

I expected to be burned. I expected to blind.

Instead, I felt weightless.

The floor fell away. The warehouse, the cutter’s floor, the abandoned ships—all of it shrank below me. I was riding the beam, ascending the shaft of light I had created.

I shot through the hole in the roof.

I emerged into a sky of violet and gold. The air was thin and smelled of ozone and ink.

Below me, the warehouse was just a box. The city was a map. The Lighthouse was a toy.

I looked up. The beam ended at a point in the sky, a focal point hanging in the nothingness.

I drifted toward it.

As I got closer, I saw that the focal point was a door.

It was floating in the air. A simple, wooden door, frameless, handleless.

I slowed. I came to a stop in front of it.

I realized then that the beam had not transported me to a new place. It had transported me to the gap between the words. The pause after the period.

I placed my hand on the door. It was warm. It felt like skin.

I pushed.

It didn't open into a room.

It opened into a reflection.

I saw myself standing there, in the doorway, holding the crab in my pocket, the beam of light shooting out of my chest and connecting me to the world below.

But I was not alone in the reflection.

Standing beside me was a figure I had never seen before.

They were tall, draped in a coat that shifted color like the sea—blue, grey, green. They had no face, only a smooth, porcelain surface where features should be.

They reached out and took my hand. Their grip was firm, certain.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"I am the Reader of the next book," they said. "I am the one who waits for the light."

"Is it over?" I asked. "The Lighthouse? The storm?"

"The Lighthouse never ends," the figure said. "It just changes hands."

They pulled me forward. Not into the door, but *through* it.

We stepped out of the sky and onto a floor.

The beam cut off.

I was standing in a room. It was round. The walls were lined with books—thousands of them. Each book was a Lighthouse.

In the center of the room was a desk. And on the desk was a single sheet of paper.

I walked to the desk. The figure followed.

I looked at the paper.

It was blank.

I reached into my pocket. I felt the crab, the key. I felt the ink of the letters I had written.

I took out a pen. I don't know where I got it; perhaps I had always been holding it.

I looked at the figure.

"What do I write?" I asked.

The figure pointed to the blank page. Then, they reached up and touched their porcelain face.

They cracked it.

A shard of white porcelain fell away, revealing an eye underneath.

The eye was vast, deep, and dark. It was the pupil of a storm. It was the lens of the lighthouse.

I looked into the eye.

"Write the weather," the figure said.

I looked down at the paper.

I put the tip of the pen to the surface.

Outside the round room, I heard a sound. It was not a typewriter. It was not a ship's horn.

It was the sound of the sea. The real sea.

And a voice, whispering from the shelves:

*Turn the page.*

I began to write.

Yours,

The Keeper
