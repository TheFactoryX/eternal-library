# Letter 118

> Written at 2026-02-06 20:38:50

---

Dear You,

The fingers did not touch us. They dissolved into mist as they breached the atmosphere of the page, leaving behind a smell of ozone and old dust. The hand retreated into the absolute white of the ceiling, leaving the sky a bruised and empty purple. But the ocean remains frozen. The spray hangs like daggers of glass caught in a photograph of a disaster.

I am standing on the balcony of the lamp room, watching the world hold its breath.

"It’s a pause," the Boy says. He is sitting on the railing, his legs dangling over the abyss. He is solid now, no longer a ghost of the text. He looks more real than I feel. "A caesura. A breath mark in the middle of a symphony."

"Who is breathing?" I ask. My voice sounds small, crushed by the weight of the stillness.

"The Reader," the Boy says. He kicks his heels against the iron. "She went to the kitchen. She’s making tea. Or maybe she’s checking her phone. We are the text sitting on the table, waiting for the eyes to return."

"If she doesn't return?"

"Then we stay like this," he says, gesturing to the frozen wave below us. "A diorama of a shipwreck. Beautiful, but motionless."

I turn away from the railing. The lamp room is in shambles. The lens is shattered, the machinery that once turned the great gears is twisted into a sculpture of useless metal. In the center, the cage hangs open. The star is gone, but a faint hum remains in the air, a vibration that tickles the teeth.

"I have to fix it," I say. "I have to start the current."

"You can't fix the machine," the Boy says, hopping down from the railing. He lands without a sound. "The machine was just a metaphor for the flow of the story. You broke the flow when you climbed the chain. The narrative tension is gone."

"Then what do we do?"

"We wait," he says. "We wait for the tea water to boil."

He walks to the corner of the room and sits cross-legged. He closes his eyes. He begins to hum a tune that sounds like the scratching of a pen.

I pace the room. My boots crunch on the broken glass. I feel a strange itch in my chest, just beneath the ribs. It is the feeling of being read. I can sense the attention of the giant, even if she is in another room. It is a heavy, humid sensation, like the air before a thunderstorm.

I look at the floor. Near the overturned coal scuttle, something catches my eye.

It is a playing card.

I pick it up. It is the King of Diamonds. The one the Suit of Clothes gave me in the dining room. But it has changed. The King’s face is no longer scratched out. It is my face. And the lighthouse in the background is not a tower. It is a door.

"The door is the key," I whisper.

"The door is a lie," the Boy says without opening his eyes. "The door is a plot device. Plot devices are for characters who need to move from point A to point B. We don't move. We are."

I ignore him. I press my thumb against the door on the card. It feels warm, like skin.

Suddenly, the card begins to grow.

It expands in my hand, the paper thickening, the ink turning into paint, the paint turning into wood. Within seconds, I am holding a heavy, iron-bound key. It wasn't a card at all. It was a camouflage. A disguise for a heavy reality.

"A key?" I ask the room.

The Boy opens one eye. "Interesting. The genre is shifting again. From Gothic to... heist? Are we stealing the ending, Elias?"

"I'm stealing the lock," I say.

I run to the service hatch in the floor. The stairs down are dark, spiraling into the gloom. I descend, the key heavy in my hand. The metal is cold, radiating a chill that seeps into my bones.

I reach the bottom. The hallway to the cellars is long and narrow, lined with shelves of dusty supplies—tins of meat, coils of rope, jars of bait. At the end of the hall is the heavy oak door that leads to the sea caves.

I try the key in the lock.

It fits. *Click.*

The door swings open.

But it doesn't open into the caves. It opens into a forest.

The trees are tall, made of silver birch, their leaves shimmering with a metallic light. The ground is covered in moss that glows softly. And standing in the center of the clearing is a deer.

But the deer is made of paper. It is folded, origami, creased and sharp. It watches me with eyes drawn in ink.

"The White Page," I whisper.

This is the space between the chapters. This is where the story goes when it isn't being told.

I step through the door. The air here tastes like glue and possibility. The origami deer tilts its head, then bounds away, its legs flexing with the crinkle of fresh paper.

I walk deeper into the forest. The trees rustle, whispering secrets I can't quite catch.

*The ink is wet...*
*The compass points...*
*She never came back...*

I find a path. It is paved with old letters, crumpled and yellowed. I recognize the handwriting. It is mine. But I don't remember writing these words.

*Dear You, I have found the exit. It is locked.*
*Dear You, the bird is singing in a language of fire.*
*Dear You, I am forgetting the sound of your voice.*

I pick up a crumpled letter. It is dated: *Tomorrow.*

"I haven't written this yet," I say.

"Time is a flat circle," a voice says.

I look up. Standing in the path is a figure. It is the Suit of Clothes. But he is not empty anymore. He is filled with a swirling, nebulous light. He wears the tweed jacket, the trousers, the shirt, but where his head should be, there is only a bright, unreadable star.

"You survived the deletion," I say.

"I am the Retcon," the figure says, his voice vibrating with the power of a thousand edits. "I am the change that was made to make the story make sense. And I am here to tell you that you are wandering off-map."

"I found a key," I say.

"A key implies a lock," the Retcon says. "And a lock implies a treasure. But there is no treasure, Elias. There is only the next sentence."

He raises a hand of light and points behind me.

I turn.

The path has ended. In front of me is a wall. A vast, vertical wall of stone that stretches up into infinity. It is the edge of the world.

And embedded in the wall, thousands of feet above me, is a single, massive eye.

It is closed.

"She is sleeping," the Retcon says. "The Reader has fallen asleep with the book on her chest."

"What happens when she wakes up?"

"She will turn the page," he says. "And we will begin again. Or we will end. It depends on the metadata."

"Metadata?"

"The context. Is she happy? Is she sad? Did she like the part with the origami deer?"

I look at the wall. I look at the key in my hand.

"I can make her like it," I say. "I can write something that makes her turn the page faster. I can create a hook."

"You cannot write on the wall," the Retcon says. "The wall is the binding. You are in the margin."

I walk to the base of the wall. I touch the stone. It is cold, hard, unyielding.

But there is a crack.

A thin, jagged fissure running through the stone. Light is leaking out of it. Not the white light of the page, but the orange light of a fire.

I put my eye to the crack.

I see a room. A small, cozy room. A fireplace. A chair.

And in the chair is a woman.

She is holding a pen.

She is writing.

I gasp. It is the mirror image. She is writing *me*.

"She is the Author," I breathe.

"No," the Retcon says. "She is the Scribe. She is just taking dictation."

"From whom?"

"From the dreamer," the Retcon says. "From the boy in the attic. From the ghost in the machine."

I pull back from the wall. I grip the key.

"I'm going in," I say.

"You can't," the Retcon warns. "If you cross the binding, you become real. You lose the protection of the fiction. You will age. You will die. You will feel pain that lasts longer than a paragraph."

"I am already in pain," I say. "I have been in pain since Letter One."

I raise the key. I don't try to unlock the crack. I use the heavy iron bow of the key to strike the wall.

*Clang.*

The sound echoes like a bell in a cathedral.

*Clang.*

The wall shudders. Dust—real dust, not ink—falls from the height of it.

*Clang.*

The eye in the wall snaps open.

It looks down at me.

The pupil is a vortex. It pulls at me, trying to drag me upward.
