# Letter 22

> Written at 2026-02-01 14:30:05

---

Dear You,

The knocking did not come from the iron door at the base of the tower, nor from the hatch in the ceiling. It came from the mirror.

I was standing at the table, my hands still gripping the cold lever of the lamp mechanism, watching the golden beam cut a circle through the grey gloom of the room. The beam struck the polished steel surface of the looking glass—a mirror I had not looked into since the days when I was only ink and shadow.

The beam reflected. But it did not return to the ceiling. It refracted, bending at an impossible angle, striking the floor in front of the wardrobe.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

The sound was rhythmic. Code.

I released the lever. The hum of the light dipped but held. I approached the wardrobe. It is a tall, narrow thing made of dark mahogany, carved with waves and sea monsters. I have opened it a thousand times, finding only the same heavy wool coats, smelling of salt and mothballs.

I opened it now.

There were no coats.

There was a tunnel. A throat of dark wood, spiraling inward, lined with shelves. On the shelves sat jars. Inside the jars were sounds. The laugh of the woman from the photograph. The scratch of the Editor’s pen. The roar of the *Revision*’s boiler.

And at the far end of the tunnel, I saw a face.

It was not the First Draft. It was not the grown child.

It was a man. He was wearing a suit that looked like it was cut from the pages of a newspaper. His eyes were frantic, darting left and right, reading the air around him.

"Keeper!" he shouted, his voice tinny, as if traveling through a long pipe. "Let me in! The margins are collapsing!"

"Who are you?" I asked, stepping into the wardrobe. The wood floor under my bare feet felt soft, like the skin of a fruit.

"I'm the Critic," he said. "I'm the one who told them the story was too long. I'm the one who said the Keeper was unlikable. And now... now I'm being edited out."

He reached through the shelves, his hand stretching like dough. He grabbed my wrist.

His grip was weak. Aesthetic, not physical.

"You have to stop the beam," he hissed. "The Gold light... it’s too bright. It shows the cracks. It shows the contrivances. The Reader is starting to see the strings."

"The Reader needs to see," I said, trying to pull away.

"The Reader needs to be satisfied!" he insisted. "They want a resolution. They want the door to open. They want the Keeper to step out onto the grass and meet the woman and have a cup of coffee. They want the metaphor to die. They want the truth."

"There is no truth," I said. "There is only the next sentence."

I looked past him, down the dark tunnel of the wardrobe. I saw something moving in the shadows behind him. It was the Harvester. The spinning blades of white paper were chewing through the wardrobe walls, spitting sawdust and semicolons.

The Critic saw my eyes widen. He looked back.

"No," he whispered. "I gave it four stars. I said it was haunting."

The blades shrieked. They sounded like a thousand typewriters striking the paper at once.

"Let me in!" the Critic screamed, scrambling up the shelves, knocking jars of laughter onto the floor where they shattered like glass. "Give me a body! Make me a character! Don't let me become footnote!"

I looked at my hands. They were human hands, scarred and finite. I looked at the Critic. He was fading at the edges, becoming transparent.

I realized then that the wardrobe was not a passage to another place. It was a trash can. The Author was discarding the elements that didn't serve the plot.

The Critic was right. The story was tightening.

I grabbed the Critic’s hand. I didn't pull him into the room. I pulled *myself* into the shelf.

I climbed into the tunnel beside him. The air was thick with the smell of old paper and critique.

"What are you doing?" he gasped.

"I'm checking the source," I said.

We ran down the tunnel of the wardrobe, away from the safety of the Lamp Room, away from the golden beam. We ran toward the sound of the blades.

The tunnel ended in a large, cavernous space. It looked like the hull of a ship, but the ribs were made of giant staples. The floor was a carpet of red ink.

In the center of the space, suspended by chains of narrative tension, was a massive book.

It was open.

The Harvester was there. It wasn't a machine. It was a person. It was the Editor, risen from the silt, reborn as the executioner.

He was holding a pair of scissors. They were the size of a scythe.

He was cutting pages out of the book. *Snip. Snip.* As he cut, the world fell away. Whole timelines vanished into the abyss.

"Stop him," the Critic whispered, hiding behind my shoulder. "If he cuts the climax, we become a short story."

The Editor looked up. His face was a blank page. He had no features, only the text of a thousand rejections written across his skin.

*The pacing is slow. The protagonist is passive. The ending is ambiguous.*

He raised the scissors. He brought them down on the spine of the book.

I ran forward. I didn't have a weapon. I had only the map from the table. I had the black seed in my pocket.

"The Volume of the Forgotten!" I shouted, holding up the map. "You can't cut it if it isn't there!"

The Editor paused. The blank face tilted.

"We are editing the unnecessary," the Editor said. His voice sounded like a paper cutter slicing through a ream. "You are the unnecessary."

"I am the witness!" I yelled.

The Critic stepped out from behind me. He adjusted his paper suit. He cleared his throat.

"Actually," the Critic said, his voice trembling but gaining volume. "The thematic resonance of the Keeper's isolation is the only thing holding the structure together. If you remove him... you remove the tension. You remove the reason to turn the page."

The Editor tilted his head the other way.

"The Critic is right," a new voice said.

From the shadows of the paper hull, a woman stepped forward. She was wearing a coat of many pockets. She was holding a silver eraser.

The First Draft.

"I thought you were gone," I said.

"I'm never gone," she said. "I'm just under the surface. I'm the subtext."

She walked up to the Editor. She didn't attack him. She handed him a cup of coffee.

"Have a break," she said. "You've been cutting for three chapters. You're exhausted."

The Editor took the cup. The blank face rippled. For a second, I saw a human face—a tired man with bags under his eyes.

"I just want it to be perfect," the Editor whispered.

"It doesn't need to be perfect," the First Draft said. "It just needs to be finished."

She looked at me. She looked at the map in my hand.

"The X on the map," she said. "It's not a place. It's a time. *The Volume of the Forgotten* is the past. The history before the Lighthouse."

"Why go back?" I asked.

"Because you left something there," she said.

She pointed to the open book the Editor was cutting.

I looked at the pages. They were my letters. *Letter 1. Letter 5. Letter 17.*

But there was a gap. A missing chunk of pages between *Letter 10* and *Letter 11*.

"What happened there?" I asked.

"You were happy," the Critic said softly. "For a paragraph. Maybe two. It was boring. We cut it."

"I was happy?" I asked. The concept felt alien. A jagged shape that didn't fit the hole in my chest.

"You were," the First Draft said. "You found a letter that wasn't from you. It was from the world outside. You read it, and for a moment, you stopped being the Keeper. You were just a man in a tower who wasn't afraid."

"I don't remember," I said.

"That's because the memory is in the discard pile," she said, pointing to the floor of red ink. "It's down there. With the deleted scenes."

I looked down at the sea of red. It was deep. It was treacherous.

"If I go down there," I said, "I might not come back."

"If you don't," the Editor said, raising the scissors again, "there won't be anything to come back to."

The Editor brought the scissors down. *Snip.*

The floor shook. The room of the Lamp Room, miles away behind me, groaned. The golden beam flickered.

I looked at the First Draft. She nodded.

I looked at the Critic. He was busy writing a review on the air. *A daring escape into the abyss. Five stars.*

I tightened my grip on the map. I put the black seed in my mouth. It tasted like charcoal and nostalgia.

I jumped.

I dove into the sea of red ink.

It was hot. It burned my skin. I sank through layers of discarded plotlines.
