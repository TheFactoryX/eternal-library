I paced the deck of the *Iron Duke*. The wood groaned under my boots, a sound like a spine cracking. Up here, in the Dry Dock of the Sky, the air is thin and tastes of static electricity.

"We are too heavy for potential," I said, gripping the iron railing. "We have mass. We have the coat. We have the key."

I looked down at the objects in my arms. The coat was heavy, smelling of mildew and the salt of the *real* sea. The key was cold, a jagged piece of geometry that hurt to hold.

"They are anchors," You said. You were standing by the ship's wheel, your yellow coat flapping like a gold leaf in the grey wind. "Anchors keep you in place. But we are not in a harbor. We are drifting."

"Drifting where?"

"Toward the Warehouse," You said, pointing a finger at the horizon.

I looked.

The sky was not endless. It ended in a wall of corrugated metal, rusted and riveted, stretching from the clouds above to the abyss below. It was the side of the container. The Box we had felt moving beneath us.

And on the horizon, silhouetted against the rusted metal wall, was a fleet.

Hundreds of ships. Galleons with tattered silk sails. Steamships trailing black smoke that hung in the air like dirty cotton. Triremes with oars frozen in mid-row.

They were all grounded on the cloud layer. A graveyard of narratives.

"The Shelves," I realized. "He’s putting us on the shelf."

"Or he is selling us," You said. "The second-hand bookshop. The bargain bin. The donation box."

I walked to the edge of the deck. I looked over the side. The *Iron Duke* was resting on a bed of thick, grey fog. It felt solid enough, like packed wool, but I could see through the gaps in the mist.

I saw streets. A city made of dust.

"What is that?"

"The Gutter," You said. "The space between the paragraphs. The place where the ambiguity lives."

Suddenly, a sound tore through the silence.

*CLANG.*

I jumped.

The sound came from the mast. It rang again, sharp and metallic.

*CLANG.*

I looked up. High in the rigging, a bell was swinging. But there was no wind. The bell was swinging because something was hitting it.

I grabbed a spyglass hanging from the railing—a rusted copper tube that felt greasy to the touch. I raised it to my eye.

I scanned the yards, the shrouds, the crow's nest.

There.

Perched on the mainmast, sitting on the crossbeam like a gargoyle, was a bird.

But it wasn't a bird. It was a construct of wire and parchment. Its body was a ball of crumpled rejection slips. Its wings were made of razor blades. Its beak was a fountain pen nib, dripping black ink.

It opened its beak and a sound came out—not a squawk, but a voice.

*Typing. Fast. Insistent.*

"The Scrivener," I whispered. "The Author’s shorthand."

"It’s a scout," You said. "It’s here to catalogue us."

The bird turned its head. The eye was a camera lens. It focused on me.

*Whirrrrrr.*

The shutter clicked. The sound was like a bone breaking.

"It's taking the picture," I said. "It's capturing the metadata."

"What does it see?" You asked.

"It sees the coat," I said. "It sees the key."

The bird launched itself from the mast. It didn't fly. It fell, straight down, like a stone. It plummeted toward the deck, razor wings extended.

"Get down!" I shouted.

I tackled You. We hit the deck just as the bird streaked over our heads. It missed us by inches. The wind from its passage smelled of solvents.

The bird crashed into the quarterdeck. It scuttled across the wood, its razor wings scratching gouges into the planks. It hopped, turned, and faced us.

It began to peck at the air.

*Peck. Peck. Peck.*

With every peck, a dot of ink appeared on the wood.

*Peck. Peck. Peck.*

It was forming a line. A dotted line.

"It's writing a path," You said. "It's herding us."

"Where to?"

I looked where the line was heading. Toward the Captain's cabin.

The bird stopped. It twitched its head, waiting.

"We have to go in," I said.

"It's a trap," You said.

"It's a scene," I corrected. "And we need a scene to stay alive."

I stood up. I kept the coat wrapped around me, a shield of wool and memory. I gripped the key until my knuckles turned white.

We walked past the bird. It watched us with its camera eye, the lens dilating, clicking, recording every step.

We reached the cabin door. It was made of heavy oak, banded with brass. The handle was a lion's head, similar to the tiny door in the crate, but this one was roaring.

I tried the handle. Locked.

"Of course," I said.

I looked at the bird. It hopped closer. It was pecking at my boots now, trying to anchor me to the spot.

"Do it," You urged.

I took the iron key. I slid it into the lion's mouth.

*Click.*

The lion's jaw unhinged. The door swung open.

We stepped inside.

The cabin was dark. The only light came from the portholes, which showed the swirling grey fog outside.

The room was small. A bunk. A desk. A chair.

And on the desk...

A bottle.

It was a green glass bottle, stoppered with wax. And inside the bottle was a single, rolled-up piece of paper.

"A message," I breathed. "A letter in a bottle."

But there was something else on the desk.

A mirror.

It was a simple, oval mirror, hanging above the bunk. The glass was spotted with black rot.

I approached the mirror. I was afraid to look. I was afraid I wouldn't see myself, or worse, that I would see the Author.

I looked.

I saw me.

I was wearing the coat. I was holding the key. My face was pale, my eyes dark.

But there was something behind my reflection.

A shadow. A tall, looming darkness.

I turned around.

Nothing there. Just the cabin.

I looked back at the mirror. The shadow was still there, looming over my shoulder.

"Who is that?" You asked, standing beside me.

"I don't know," I said. "It looks like..."

I squinted at the glass.

The shadow didn't look like a person. It looked like a lighthouse. A tall, spiraling tower of darkness.

"It's the structure," You whispered. "The narrative arc. It’s looming over you."

The shadow in the mirror moved. It reached out a hand made of fog.

I reached for the bottle. I needed to know what was inside.

But my hand passed through the bottle.

It wasn't a reflection. The bottle wasn't there. The desk wasn't there.

The whole room...

"The walls," I said. "Look at the walls."

You looked.

The wood paneling was moving. It was shimmering.

"It's not wood," You said. "It's pixels."

"No," I said. "It's not pixels. It's grain. It's... paper."

I reached out and touched the wall. My finger tore through the "oak" like wet tissue.

I ripped a strip of the wall away.

Behind the wood was... grey sky.

I ripped another strip. More sky.

"The room is a facade," I said, panic rising in my throat. "We are standing on a painted set."

"It's a diorama," You said. "We are in the display case."

I looked through the hole I had made in the wall.

Outside, I saw the giant room. The linoleum floor. The fluorescent lights.

And a face.

A giant face, peering into the diorama.

A child.

He was holding the box. The *Iron Duke* was in his hands.

"He's shaking it," I realized.

"Grab the book!" You shouted.

"What book?"

"The manuscript! The one we burned! It must be here!"

I scrambled to the desk. I grabbed the bottle. It was solid now. Real.

I smashed the bottle against the desk.

Glass shattered. The stopper flew across the room. The rolled-up paper fell onto the floor.

I grabbed it.

I unrolled it.

It wasn't a letter.

It was a photograph.

A black and white photograph, glossy and curling at the edges.

It showed a lighthouse. A real lighthouse. And standing in front of it, standing on the rocky shore, holding a yellow umbrella...

Was You.

And standing next to You, holding the camera...

Was me.

But we weren't looking at the camera. We were looking at each other. And we were smiling.
