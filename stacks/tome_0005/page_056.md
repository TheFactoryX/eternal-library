**Origin:** The Hydro-Ceramic Atolls of *Moana-Papa* (The Living Pottery)
**Arrival Status:** The postcard is a circular shard of bisque-fired terra cotta, porous and rough, yet smelling faintly of tropical rain and wet clay. The ink is a high-gloss glaze that is still slightly tacky to the touch.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from a hammock woven from living vines, strung between the two smoking chimneys of a kiln that serves as a café. I have arrived in **Moana-Papa**, the Great Kiln of the Pacific, an archipelago where the line between the ocean and the earth is blurred by fire. It is a place where the water is thick as slip, and the islands are still soft enough to be sculpted by a careless hand.

You recall I was riding the *River-of-Retcons*, heading for the **Arboretum-of-Archive**? The river took a sharp turn—I blame a rogue editor deleting a tributary—and I found myself cascading down a waterfall of liquid kaolinite. I washed up on a beach that wasn't sand, but a massive, undulating slab of unfired stoneware. The sun here doesn't just shine; it bakes. It is a relentless, high-firing kiln in the sky, turning the world hard and permanent with every rotation.

The geography is malleable and perilous. **Moana-Papa** consists of a thousand islands, none of which are finished. The locals—the **Potters**—must constantly reshape the landmasses to prevent them from slumping into the sea during high tide. I walked to the edge of *Clay-Cay*, and the ground simply drooped into the turquoise water like a melting candle. "Careful where you step," a local warned me. "That peninsula is still leather-hard."

The locals are beautiful, smooth-skinned people with cracked, dry skin who value hydration above all else. They communicate through *Klik-Klak*, a language of tapping and knocking. One knock on a table means "I am here"; two knocks mean "The clay is drying too fast." A sharp *crack* is an emergency, signaling structural failure. I met a sculptor named *Glazia* who was busy applying a layer of blue copper oxide to her husband’s back. "He is looking a bit dull," she said, brushing him with a soft sponge. "A nice gloss finish will make him pop."

Language here is distinct. They don't say "Hello," they ask, "Are you firing today?" To say "Goodbye," one says, "Keep your core temperature up." I attempted to compliment the weather, but I used the word for "biscuit" instead of "sunny," and was immediately led to a large oven. I had to explain that I was already fired in the metaphorical sense, which they found hilarious and deeply tragic.

Dining here is a visceral experience. I visited a tavern carved entirely out of a volcanic vent. I ordered the **Stew-of-Sediment**, a chunky broth loaded with pebbles, grit, and bone. It tastes like the bottom of a riverbed and settles in the stomach like a brick. I also tried the **Loaf-of-Loam**, a dense bread made from sifted river mud and yeast. It must be eaten quickly, or it hardens into a weapon. For dessert, I had *Fig-Fritters* glazed in a sugar so thick it nearly sealed my lips shut. The waiter, a man with a shiny, vitrified arm, had to chip the glaze off my teeth with a chisel.

The wildlife is eclectic and fragile. There are **Shard-Crabs**, creatures made of broken porcelain that scuttle sideways and cut your feet if you aren't wearing shoes. I saw a flock of **Kiln-Birds**, birds with wings of fire-resistant feather that fly into the hottest part of the noon sun to preen their plumage. But the most majestic are the **Porcelain-Whales**, massive, bone-white creatures that breach the surface with a sound like breaking china. They don't spout water; they spout steam.

I found a message from the library scratched into the wet clay of a drying dock. It read: *Everything breaks eventually. Even the tides.*

The heat is drying out my ink, and I feel my skin growing harder by the hour. I need a place where the ground moves, but not because it's being baked. I need the rumble of wheels and the logic of the grid.

I am heading *North-by-Northwest*, following the **Ferry-of-Fire** to the **City-of-Cogs-and-Coffee**. I hear the trains run on time because time is the only fuel they use.

Mind the gap.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
