# Postcard #007

**Origin:** The Sargasso Archipelago of High-Tide (Currently docked at Atoll-Vagrant)
**Arrival Status:** Saturated; smells of coconut milk, ozone, and freshly printed paper; the card is dry to the touch but yields no moisture when squeezed, as if the water is merely painted on.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from the deck of a floating tavern that is currently tethered to the shadow of a cloud. I have arrived in **The Drift-Sea of Tuamotu-Possible**, a cluster of islands that refuse to be mapped because they are jealous of their privacy.

You recall I was heading for the city inside the thunderstorm? I took the train, but it derailed somewhere over the Pacific when the lightning conductor union went on strike. I fell through a cloud and landed in the soft, violet embrace of a giant lily pad the size of a football field. This is the nature of the **Drift-Sea**: the land here is not fixed. The islands are nomadic. They migrate across the ocean on the backs of immense, silent turtles made of coral and patience, following the warm currents of the Gulf Stream and the colder whims of the moon.

The geography here is liquid and temporary. The beaches are composed of sand grains that are actually tiny, crushed hourglasses, so walking on the beach makes a strange crunching sound, like stepping on time. I visited a place called **Bora-Bora-Unseen**, which is visible only out of the corner of your eye. If you look directly at it, the island shimmers and vanishes, leaving nothing but empty ocean. To enter the village, you have to wear a blindfold and trust that the locals will catch you. I spent a wonderful afternoon on a beach I couldn't see, drinking a cocktail I couldn't identify, surrounded by people I couldn't describe. It was incredibly relaxing.

The locals are the **Kai-Rere** (The Runners-On-Water). They have evolved feet that are broad and webbed like the petals of a lotus, allowing them to stride across the surface tension of the sea as if it were solid pavement. They do not swim; they simply stroll out to the middle of the ocean to fish. Their language is not spoken, but sung in a polyphonic harmony that vibrates in the chest. A simple greeting sounds like a chord progression on a cello. I tried to greet a fisherman yesterday and accidentally insulted his boat. It rowed away in a huff.

I have developed a craving for the local cuisine, which relies heavily on ingredients that are usually considered abstract. I went to a market floating on a bed of seaweed and bought a **Fruit of Anticipation**. It looks like a bright red mango, but it has no flavor while you are eating it. The taste hits you exactly three hours later, usually when you are thinking about something else. I ate one this morning and was suddenly overwhelmed by the ghost of chili and lime while I was trying to read a map.

For dinner, I had **Raw Data**. No, not the numbers—the fish. Here, the fish are bioluminescent because they have eaten so much information from the undersea fiber optic cables. They glow with the blue light of emails and streaming video. When you eat them sashimi-style, you get tiny flashes of news headlines and pop song lyrics in your head. It was a bit noisy in my mouth, but delicious.

The most impossible feature of this place is the **Rain of upwards**. In the Drift-Sea, the water cycle is inverted. The rain falls *up* from the ocean, gathering in the sky to form vast, heavy lakes that float above the islands. The locals have to pump the water back down with great, siphoning towers made of bamboo. It creates a bizarre weather pattern where you have to hold an umbrella over your head to keep dry, but you also have to wear boots to keep from getting soaked by the groundwater dripping from the clouds.

I did, however, find a moment of familiarity. I met a trader here selling navigation charts. He was using the stone lion carving I sent back from the Time River as a paperweight. He said he bought it from a merchant who claimed it fell out of yesterday. It seems my gift made the journey through the calendar, though it arrived slightly before I sent it.

The sun is setting, and the sky is turning a color that doesn't have a name in your language—a sort of bruised peach. The upward rain is starting to fall, drifting lazily toward the moon. I need to secure my hammock; the turtles are starting to migrate for the night, and the motion can be quite rocky if you aren't strapped in.

I am heading West now, toward the horizon that looks slightly jagged. I have heard rumors of **The Limestone Labyrinth**, a city that is built entirely of echo-chambers, where nothing you say is ever lost, only stored in the stone walls.

I hope your world is holding still.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
