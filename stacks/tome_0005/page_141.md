**Origin:** The Papery Tides of *The Ori-Ocean* ( The Fold)
**Arrival Status:** The postcard is a perfect square of heavy, cream-colored washi paper, textured like cloth. It smells faintly of wet paper, ink, and pressed cherry blossoms. The text appears to be sumi-e calligraphy, but if you look closely, the brushstrokes are actually tiny fibers of the paper standing up to form the letters. It crinkles loudly when handled, sounding like a turning page.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from the deck of a vessel that rides the creases of reality. It is a quiet journey, save for the rhythmic sound of paper rustling against paper. I have arrived at **The Ori-Ocean**, the Great Fold. This is the place where origami goes when it ceases to be a toy and becomes a topology.

You recall I was heading *Into the Fold* to find a harbor where the paper boats are sent? I managed to navigate the treacherous waters of the *Null-Point*—where I had to refold my map three times just to make the coastline exist—and eventually washed ashore on a beach made of infinitely repeating tessellations. The ground here is sharp and clean, smelling of pulp and patience.

The geography here is angular and strictly architectural. The ocean does not roll; it creases. The waves are sharp, triangular peaks of heavy cardstock that crash against the shore with a crisp *shhh-clack*. There is no liquid water, only a million different grades of paper, from delicate tissue to corrugated cardboard. I am currently in the **Bay of Rice**, where the water is made of translucent, white paper that glows when the sub-sun hits it. To the east lie the **Sharp Mountains**, ranges formed by jagged cuts in the horizon, their peaks folded into aggressive, impossible points.

The locals are the **Folding-Folk**. They are humanoid figures constructed entirely of ruled notebook paper. Their skin is covered in faint blue lines, and their joints are sharp creases that allow for a range of motion that hurts my human elbows just to watch. They communicate through **Crinkle-Logic**. They don't speak; they manipulate the paper around them, folding the air to create sounds that resemble rustling leaves and crumpling balls. A sharp snap means "Welcome," while a slow, smooth tear signifies "I am sorry." I tried to converse with a boatman, but he simply unfolded his arm to reveal a ferry schedule and handed me a ticket made of a pressed leaf.

I am currently staying in *The Paper-Lantern-Hotel*, a floating structure made of illuminated shoji screens. The rooms are small, compartmentalized boxes that slide open and shut. I slept on a futon that feels like being pressed inside a heavy book. It was the most comfortable sleep I’ve had in eons. The concierge is a crane with eyes of ink who refuses to look at me until I bow.

I am dining on **Sashimi-of-Silk**. It is not food, exactly, but strips of colored silk so thin they melt on the tongue, served with a dipping sauce of soy-textured ink and a wasabi paste that makes your sinuses feel like they’ve been highlighted. It tastes like elegance. For dessert, I am having **Mochi-Memory-Cakes**. These are soft, white spheres that, when bitten into, release a sweet red bean paste and a sudden, vivid flash of a memory belonging to the person who folded the box. I tasted a memory of a cat sleeping in a sunbeam; it was warm and furry.

The wildlife is sparse but distinct. The most common creature is the **Crane-Carp**. They are angular fish that swim through the paper sea by flapping their folded wings. They don't swim *in* the water; they swim *on* the surface tension, leaving trails of ripples that never settle. The apex predator is the **Dragon-Kirigami**. It is a terrifying beast, cut from a single sheet of black construction paper, flying with blades for wings. It hunts by cutting holes in reality, slicing through the air to snare unsuspecting thoughts. I saw one cut a cloud in half, and the cloud fell to the ground like a severed cotton ball.

I found a message from the library written on the belly of a paper boat floating in a paper gutter. It was written in red ink, like a correction mark. It read: *We are all just rough drafts waiting for an editor.*

The wind is picking up, turning from a gentle breeze into a sandstorm of confetti. It stings the skin.

I am heading *To the Back of the Book*. I have found a reference in the local glossary to a place called *The Index*, a corridor where everything that has ever been written is cataloged, including things that haven't happened yet. I intend to look up my own name.

Mind the edges; they can be sharp.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
