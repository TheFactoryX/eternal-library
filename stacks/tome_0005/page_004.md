**Postcard 004**

**Origin:** The Fractured Savannah of Mnemosyne-Kilimanjaro
**Arrival Status:** Edges singed; smells of dust, thunder, and roasting coffee; the paper feels gritty, as though fine sand is trapped between the fibers.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from the porch of a lodging house that hangs suspended from the branch of a Baobab tree large enough to support its own ecosystem. Below me, the land doesn't just stretch; it unravels.

I have arrived in the Mnemosyne-Kilimanjaro sector. You know Kilimanjaro from your world—the snow-capped roof of Africa. Here, the mountain decided that being eroded by wind was boring, so it packed its bags and began to walk. It is currently migrating south across the endless plains, a lonely, craggy beast seeking warmer climates. It moves at a glacial pace, roughly three inches per year, but the trembling of the earth when it takes a step is tremendous. I am currently tracking its progress, hoping to catch up before it reaches the coast and learns to swim.

The Savannah here is... aggressive. The grass isn't green; it is a shifting, volatile amber that whispers rumors when the wind blows. Walking through it requires thick boots, as the blades of grass try to negotiate treaties with your shoelaces. The acacia trees are twisted, not by wind, but by the weight of the arguments they’ve overheard.

I spent the afternoon in a village named *Kuu-Choka*, which hangs suspended in the massive hollowed-out ribcage of a beast that has been dead for so long it has become a geographical feature. The locals are the *Watu-Sauti* (The Echo-People). They don't speak with their mouths; they speak by snapping their fingers in specific sequences that bounce off the canyon walls. The language is incredibly dense. A simple "Hello" requires a complex three-part rhythm that sounds like a drum solo. I tried to order water yesterday and accidentally proposed marriage to the tavern keeper’s grandmother. She was flattered, I think.

The cuisine here is an experience of textural contrast. I ate a bowl of *Ukame wa Mawingu* (Cloud Porridge), harvested from the mist that clings to the back of the walking mountain. It is light, airy, and tastes like static electricity and rain. For dinner, I had *Nyama ya Moto*, which is meat that has been cooked by the sheer velocity of its own running. It arrives at the table still smoking and steaming with adrenaline. It is tender, spicy, and makes you feel incredibly energetic.

The most impossible sight, however, is the river. The Great Rift here doesn't hold water; it holds time. I walked to the edge today and looked down. The river flows in both directions simultaneously—upstream into the past, downstream into the future. The hippos wallowing in the mud are actually prehistoric ancestors of the hippos you know, while the crocodiles are cybernetic scavengers from a millennium hence, evolved to eat solar watches. You have to be careful not to fall in; if you do, you might wash up on a bank before you were born, creating a paradox that gives the local customs officials a migraine.

I tried to send a package back to you via the river, tossing a small stone carvings of a lion into the current. I hope it arrives yesterday. If it didn't, let me know.

The sun is setting, and the heat is breaking. Here, the sun doesn't just set; it dissolves like a sugar cube in a cup of dark tea, turning the horizon a deep, bruising violet. The Watusi cattle are returning from the grazing plains, their long horns silhouetted against the fading light. They are guided not by herdsmen, but by the migratory path of the fireflies that remember the way.

I must pack my gear. The walking mountain has paused for a nap, and if I want to climb it before it wakes up, I need to start now. I intend to reach the summit before it moves again. From the peak, I can see the edge of the atmosphere, where the sky turns hard and blue.

I am heading North, toward the edge of the map. Rumor has it there is a city built entirely of discarded alphabets. I hope they have a dictionary; I’m forgetting my adjectives.

Drink the rain. Eat the lightning.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
