**Origin:** The Maritime Vacuum of *Sospirare* (The Sea of Hard Silence)
**Arrival Status:** The postcard is a heavy, irregular disc of polished white sea-glass. When held to the ear, it does not roar, but instead broadcasts a perfectly tuned FM radio frequency playing a loop of distant, frenetic jazz. It smells of espresso, motor oil, and dry heat.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from a terrace that hangs suspended over the absolute void, nursing a glass of liquid starlight. I have arrived in **Sospirare**, the Sea of Hard Silence. It is the Mediterranean of an alternate Italy where the water evaporated in the Year of the Great Thirst, leaving behind a vacuum that fills with glass rather than air.

You recall I was heading *North-by-Northwest* to the **Highway-of-Neon-Saints**? I was hitching a ride on a container ship made of chrome when we hit a patch of "dry fog." The ship’s engine, which ran on prayers and heavy oil, choked on the purity of the silence. We were grounded on a reef of solidified sound waves. I abandoned ship and walked the rest of the way across the crust, stepping on ripples of noise that had frozen in place, until I reached the white cliffs of the Amalfi Coast—or rather, what was left of them.

The geography here is defined by absence. The ocean floor is now a vast, white expanse of silica and salt. Where the water used to be, there is now a perfect vacuum that extends infinitely downward. Yet, life clings to the rim. The cities of the coast—Ravello, Positano, *Mare-Vuoto*—are no longer on the water; they dangle from the cliffs like ripe fruit, suspended by massive rusted chains and pistons that groan in the wind. The sunsets here are spectacular; without an atmosphere to scatter the light, the sun drops below the horizon like a coin falling into a slot machine, and the sky turns from azure to pitch black in the blink of an eye.

The locals are the **Suffocati**, people who wear sleek, antique diving suits not to breathe, but to filter the air. They claim that after the water vanished, the silence became so heavy it could crush a human lung. They speak *Ventimiglia*, a dialect composed entirely of whistling and sharp hand gestures, designed to travel without carrying vibration. I met a fisherman named *Giovanni* who was "fishing" for echoes. He cast his net into the void below and pulled in a soundscape of a storm from three centuries ago. He sold it to a tourist for a pouch of tobacco.

Dining here is an acoustic experience. I found a trattoria carved into the side of a vertical cliff, accessible only by a gondola made of brass. I ordered the **Risotto-all’Assenza**. It is a rice dish cooked not in broth, but in the steam of pressurized silence. The grains are translucent and heavy; eating them feels like swallowing heavy stones, but the taste is indescribable—rich, savory, and vaguely terrifying. To drink, I had a glass of *Vino-Dal-Fondo*. It is harvested from the bottom of the glass sea, where the pressure has turned grapes into diamonds. You have to let the wine sit for an hour to turn back into liquid before you can drink it. It tastes like regret and summer berries.

The wildlife has adapted to the vacuum. There are **Glass-Dolphins**, creatures made of translucent crystal that swim through the silence, leaping over the edge of the cliff and diving into the void, refracting the moonlight into rainbows. They don't breathe; they hum. I also encountered a flock of **Paper-Cranes**, birds that have evolved flat, origami-like wings to glide on the thermal currents rising from the deep earth. They are incredibly thin; if you hold one up to the sun, you can read the history of the world through its feathers.

I found a message from the library etched onto the railing of the terrace, written in a script that looked like cracking ice. It read: *Silence is the loudest answer.*

The wind is picking up, whistling through the cracks in the cliff face. The sound of the vacuum below is a ringing in my ears that never goes away. I feel light-headed, whether from the altitude or the wine.

I am heading *Vertically*, taking the *Elevator-of-Lost-Things* down into the **Abyssal-Glass-Works**. I hear the view is reflective, and I need to see myself.

Mind the gap.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
