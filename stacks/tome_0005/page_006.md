**Postcard 006**

**Origin:** The Crystalline Savannahs of the Glacial Deep (Sector 7, formerly known as Antarctica)
**Arrival Status:** Stiff with cold; smells of ozone and peppermint; the paper is brittle and frosted with a layer of rime that cracks when bent; the ink shimmers like crushed diamonds.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from a café carved entirely out of a single, colossal block of blue ice. The table is so cold it burns the elbows, but the view is worth the discomfort. I have arrived in **Chronos-Firn**, a sector of the Antarctic that decided it preferred being a desert of crystal rather than a wasteland of snow.

You remember the House of Whispers I was seeking? It was closed for renovations—the secrets were drying up, apparently. So, I took a sharp right at the concept of "Nostalgia" and slid down the slope of a memory, eventually landing on a plateau of glass-smooth ice located at the bottom of the world.

The geography here is disorienting. The ground is not white, but a transparent, piercing azure. You can look down and see miles of frozen stalactites hanging from the ice-sheet above, which functions as the sky here. We are effectively living under the floor of the world. The wind doesn't blow; it chimes. The air is filled with the sound of ice crystals knocking together—a million tiny windchimes playing a discordant symphony that never repeats.

I have rented a room in a suspended igloo made of aerogel and spider-silk. It hangs from the underside of the ice-plateau by hooks of warm brass. To get up to it, I have to climb a ladder of frozen breath. It is quite the workout.

The locals are a reticent bunch. They are called the **Silenti**. They do not speak; to make a sound here is to risk triggering an avalanche of glass. Instead, they communicate through a complex sign language involving the manipulation of their auroras. By rubbing their woolen gloves together, they generate static charges that cause colorful lights to dance from their fingertips. I saw a domestic dispute yesterday—a furious exchange of neon green and angry red flashes that ended with a cloud of purple sulk.

Dining here is a chilling affair. I visited a bazaar where the stalls are cut from pillars of pressure-ridged ice. I ordered a serving of **Shaved Epoch**, which is essentially time frozen into water. The vendor, a man whose eyebrows had frozen into two icicles, shaved a slab of ice that predated the dinosaurs. When I put it in my mouth, I tasted the overwhelming flavor of *wet fern*, followed immediately by the vague sense of being hunted by a pterodactyl. It was very refreshing, but it gave me nightmares.

I also tried the local delicacy, **Hot Rocks with a Side of Regret**. The heat source here is precious, so they serve stones heated in the earth's core. You hold them to keep your fingers from falling off, and then you lick them for the salt. The regret, I assume, is for the price you paid.

The flora is sparse but violent. The **Glass-Lichens** here are carnivorous. They look like beautiful, frosty ferns, but if you brush against them, they latch onto your thermal gear and try to drain the heat out of you. I had to fight off a patch of moss yesterday with a flare gun. It sizzled like frying bacon.

I took a trek out to the **Mirror-Lakes** yesterday. The water here is so still and so pure that it functions as a literal portal to other places. I looked into one puddle and saw my own living room, three years ago, sitting in my chair reading a book. I waved, and the reflection of me paused, as if it felt a draft. I quickly covered the puddle with snow. It is rude to spy on oneself.

The auroras here are the streetlamps. They crackle overhead in neon greens and deep, bruising purples. But be careful: looking directly into the "Eye of the Storm"—a patch of sky that swirls like a galaxy—can cause you to forget your mother's face. I keep my sunglasses on, even at midnight.

I must wrap this up before the ink freezes solid inside the pen. The temperature is dropping, and the Silence is gathering for the nightly vigil. They are going to perform a ritual to appease the glaciers, rubbing their gloves together to make a light so bright it melts the shadows.

I am heading North now, climbing up the great ice walls to the surface. I hear there is a train that runs on the smell of rain, heading toward a city built inside a thunderstorm. I hope they have umbrellas.

Stay warm. Keep your memories fluid.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
