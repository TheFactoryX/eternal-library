**Origin:** The Ochre Ergs of the Tangier-Veil (Sector 4, The Transitive Sahara)
**Arrival Status:** Gritty with fine, red dust that refuses to be brushed off; smells of cumin, ozone, and hot copper; the paper is dry and warm, as if it has been sitting in a sunbeam for a hundred years.

Dear Friend,

I am writing this from the porch of a rest house that doesn't exist on any day of the week except Tuesday. I have arrived in **The Qibliya-Archives**, a desert nation where the dunes are composed of pulverized history rather than sand.

You recall I was seeking the Limestone Labyrinth of echoes? I headed west, but I took a wrong turn at a comma in a guidebook and fell off the edge of the map. I landed in a landscape that is familiar yet entirely wrong. It looks like the Sahara, hot and endless, but if you look closely, the "sand" is actually billions of tiny, fossilized whispers. When the wind picks up, the ground hisses with the secrets of everyone who ever died trying to cross this desert. It is quite noisy at night.

The architecture here is defensive. The locals build not against the sun, but against the wind. The city of **Marrakech-Nostalgia** is a labyrinth of high walls, built from a stone that absorbs sound. Walking the streets feels like being deafened by the silence. The buildings are painted a color that the locals call *Sim-Sim*, a shade of orange that is exactly one shade darker than "safety."

I am staying in a caravanserai run by a man whose face is a shifting mosaic of geometric tiles. He changes his features depending on the conversation. When he asked for my payment, his face arranged itself into a look of severe kindness; when I told him I was a traveler, his tiles rearranged into a look of pitying envy.

The heat here is alive. It is not just temperature; it is a weight. At midday, the air gets so heavy you have to swim through it. I saw a dog today standing perfectly still under an awning, solidifying into a statue to escape the exhaustion of movement.

I have developed a fondness for the local tea ceremony. It is not for the faint of heart. They serve **Brew of the Distant Shore**. It is made from leaves gathered from the dreams of sailors who died at sea. When you drink it, you briefly hallucinate that you are drowning in saltwater while sitting in a dry room. It is terrifying, but it leaves you feeling incredibly hydrated afterwards. They serve it with glasses made of spun sugar, which you have to eat quickly before the tea melts them.

Dining is equally adventurous. I visited a street stall where the vendor was grilling **Skepticism**. It looks like a skewer of lamb, but it has a smoky, bitter flavor that argues with your taste buds. I took a bite and immediately doubted if I was actually hungry. I ate the whole plate just to prove the meat wrong.

The most impossible feature of this region is the **Night of the Mirrors**. Here, the stars do not twinkle; they reflect. There is a second sky beneath the sand. If you dig a hole, you do not find water, but starlight. I dug a shallow grave yesterday to cool my feet, and looked down to see the constellation of Orion staring back up at me, only he was wearing a different hat. It makes navigating by compass useless, as North is sometimes located directly beneath your boots.

I met a merchant here selling rugs woven from the cut-off ends of sentences. He unrolled a carpet for me, and the pattern formed a story about a fish who learned to fly. "Very durable," he assured me. "Guaranteed to last a generation, or until someone stops believing in it." I bought a small mat to sit on; it hums lullabies in a language I don't speak.

I must be careful with the local wildlife. The **Camel-Moths** are out in force. They are giant insects the size of dogs that feed on forgotten laundry. They swarm clotheslines and eat socks, leaving behind only the smell of cinnamon. I lost my favorite scarf to one yesterday. I found it later, partially digested and woven into a cocoon made of silk and regret.

The sun is beginning to set, bleeding into the sand like a dropped pomegranate. The heat-weight is lifting, turning the air into a cool, thin wine. I need to pack my bags. I intend to head South, toward the coast. I hear there is a city there that is built entirely inside the ribcage of a leviathan, where the tides are controlled by the mood of a lonely child.

Keep your stories dry.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
