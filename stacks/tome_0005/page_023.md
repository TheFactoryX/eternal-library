Dear Friend,

I am writing this from a booth at a diner that serves the best anxiety I’ve ever tasted. I have arrived in **The Franchise-Eternal**, a place that looks like the backdrop of a road trip movie, except the actors never arrived and the set forgot to stop building itself.

You remember I was heading for the Mariana-Deep-Mind, to mine for hard truths? I dove, but I hit a ceiling. I tried to break through the rock, but my hand went straight through the projection, and I tumbled out of a vending machine in Ohio. Or maybe it was a concept of Ohio. The transition was jarring; one moment I was crushing pressure, and the next I was bathed in the harsh, loving embrace of flickering neon.

The geography here is commercial and infinite. The land is flat, paved with **Gray-Matter-Ashphalt**, a material that remembers every tire tread. The horizon is punctuated by signs the size of small cities advertising things nobody needs, like *Liquid Silence* or *Pre-Owned Souls*. I am currently in the township of *Route-66-Void*, a sprawling labyrinth of strip malls that exist in a state of grand opening. The parking lots are oceans of cracked concrete where stray shopping carts, called **Cart-Rogers**, graze in herds. They migrate South for the winter, clattering rhythmically, searching for the mythical "Cart Corral of Return."

The locals are the **Night-Shifters**, people made of caffeine fluorescent light and uniform polyester. They have no faces, only name tags that say "Dave" or "Manager." They move at a speed that suggests time is both running out and standing still. I met a waitress, Mabel, who has been pouring coffee from the same pot since 1984. She told me that the tip jar is actually a portal to a dimension where everyone is rich, but nobody has any pockets.

Language here is a dialect of branding and bureaucracy. They speak *Mall-Speak*, a tongue of slogans and pleasantries that mean the opposite of what they sound like. To say "Hello," you ask, "Is this item on sale?" To say "I love you," you complain about the service. I tried to order a burger, but I used the wrong tone and accidentally applied for a credit card. I was approved in three seconds, which I find deeply concerning.

Dining here is a ritual of processed miracle. I visited a drive-thru called *The Golden Arches of Infinity* and ordered a **Happy-Meal-of-Potential**. It comes in a box that contains whatever you wanted most when you were seven. Mine was a red bicycle and a feeling of safety. I ate the bicycle; it tasted like ozone and grease. For dessert, I had a **Slurpee-of-Nostalgia**. It is a slushy drink made of crushed memories and blue syrup. It gives you brain freeze, but not in your head—in your past. I spent twenty minutes shivering as I relived a awkward junior high dance in high definition. It was refreshing.

The wildlife is mechanical and hungry. The skies are filled with ** neon billboard-billboards**, I saw a **Telephone-Pole-Woodpecker**, a mechanical bird pecking at the wires, trying to extract a dial tone. And watch out for the **Traffic-Light-Cameras**, they snap pictures of your soul and use the negatives to line bird cages.

I found a message from the library scrawled on a napkin under my glass. It was written in ketchup: *You are here. But where is here?*

The sun is setting, or rather, the giant lamp in the sky is being dimmed by the management. The neon signs are buzzing to life, painting the air in gaudy pinks and electric blues. I must finish my coffee; the refills are free, but the cost is accumulating.

I am heading *North*, toward the cold. I hear there is a border crossing where the guards check your passport, but they don't look at your face—they look at your reflection. If you like what you see, you pass. If not, you have to go back and become someone else. They call it the **Mirror-Frontier**, and I intend to stare myself down.

Keep your receipts.

Wish you were here.

— The Wanderer
