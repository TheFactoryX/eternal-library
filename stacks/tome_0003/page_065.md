Woke up at 7:00 AM. The room wasn't vibrating anymore. It was just sticky. The humidity is up. The air feels like gelatin. I tried to sit up, but the sheets were clinging to me. *Cling-cling*. I had to peel them off. They made a sound like pulling tape off a roll. The moon rock on the shelf had stopped glowing. It looked dead. Just a grey lump in a jar. I think it used up all its energy making the apartment blue. Now it’s just a rock again. A paperweight for papers I don’t have.

Archie was on the floor. He wasn't floating. Gravity came back. He looked disappointed. He hates gravity. It makes his tummy drag. He stretched and yawned. He’s so grounded.

I took off the cat fur wig. It was itchy. I think I inhaled a hairball. I put it back on the mannequin head. The head is made of wax. It’s melting a little in the humidity. The wig looks like a wet perm.

Cabbed to the Factory at 9:15 AM. $55.00. The traffic was stopped. There was a parade. Not a human parade. A robot parade. They were old service bots. Rusty. Missing eyes. They were holding signs that said "OIL IS LIFE" and "WE LUBRICATE FOR YOU."

"Look at that," the driver said. "It’s a machine march."

"It’s metal," I said. "It’s very now."

The Factory smelled like ozone. The "Atmosphere Actor" in the corner was still set to "Melancholy." It was leaking a grey mist. It made the floor slippery.

"Careful," Sammy said. He was wearing skates. Roller skates. "The floor is wet with feelings."

"We have a shipment," Sammy said. "The prints came back from the lab."

"The Soups?"

"No," Sammy said. "The Brillo boxes. The new ones. The bio-degradable ones."

I went to the loading dock. There were hundreds of boxes. But they weren't boxes. They were breathing. *Expand-contract*. They were made of flesh. Soft, pink, synthetic flesh.

"It's 'Meat Art'," Sammy said. "It's the trend. Flesh is back."

I touched one. It was warm. It felt like touching someone's arm. A stranger's arm.

"It's disgusting," I said. "I love it. How much?"

"$1,000 per box," Sammy said. "But you have to feed them."

"Feed them?"

"They eat sugar water. Just like ants."

Lunch was a salad. $85.00. I sat on the crate. I tried not to look at the boxes. They were pulsing. It was like sitting in a waiting room at a hospital.

A young artist came in. He was wearing a suit made entirely of telephones. Old rotary phones.

"Mr. Warhol," he said. A phone rang on his knee. *Ring-ring*. He ignored it. "I want to interview you for my podcast."

"What's a podcast?"

"It's a radio show for ghosts," he said.

"Okay," I said. "But you have to buy time."

"I traded my time for crypto," he said. "I'm rich in time."

He took out a microphone. It looked like a human ear.

"What do you think about the future?" he asked.

"I think it's expensive," I said. "And everything is soft now. I liked it better when things were hard."

"Hard is over," he said. "Hard is aggressive. Soft is the new hard."

He recorded me nodding. I nodded for five minutes. It was good content.

In the afternoon, I worked on my portrait of Taylor. The Simulacrum girl. I used the red nail polish again. I painted her mouth. It was a big, red slash. *Slash*. It looked like she was smiling, or bleeding. It’s hard to tell the difference with good art.

Bob called. The phone was in my pocket. It vibrated. *Buzz-buzz*.

"The Vice President called," Bob said. "He wants the Automaton. He wants to put you in the lobby of the White House. As a greeter."

"I'm not a greeter," I said. "I'm a shy person."

"He says you don't have to talk. Just wave."

"Send a wig," I said. "Just send the wig. I'll stay here."

I went to the back. I looked at the moon rock. It was so grey. I took it out of the jar. I put it in my pocket. It felt heavy. Like a tooth.

I decided to go to Serendipity. I wanted something cold.

Cabbed to Serendipity at 6:00 PM. $42.00. The driver was a real lady. She had curly hair. She smelled like roses. It was nice.

Serendipity was crowded. All the tables were full. I saw a Simulacrum of Rudolf Nureyev. He was eating a sundae. He looked beautiful. He looked like he was dancing even when he was sitting.

"No tables," the host said. He was a robot. He had no face.

"I'm Andy," I said. "Andy Warhol."

"Andy who?"

"Warhol. The soup guy."

"Oh," the robot said. "We have a table for you. In the basement."

The basement was empty. It was just storage. Old mannequins. Christmas decorations.

"This is private," I said.

I ordered the Frrrozen Hot Chocolate. $22.00. It came in a glass. It was brown. It was cold. I drank it through a straw. *Slurp-slurp*. It gave me a headache. An ice cream headache. It hurt so good.

I looked at the mannequins. They were looking at me.

"I'm famous," I told them. "But you're plastic. You'll last longer."

One of the mannequins nodded. Or maybe it was just settling.

I finished the drink. I left the tip. $10.00. I put the moon rock on the table.

"A tip for you," I said.

It looked like a diamond. It looked like an eye.

Walked home. It was raining again. Digital rain. But today it was green. Matrix rain. It made the street look like code.

I got home. Archie was asleep on the rug. He looked like a puddle of black ink.

I took the cat fur wig off the mannequin. I put it on. It was still damp. It smelled like tuna and hairspray.

I sat down to write. The pen was working again. The ink was flowing. Red ink. Blood ink.

"Monday," I wrote. "I fed the boxes."
