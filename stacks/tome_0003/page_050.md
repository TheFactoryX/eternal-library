Sunday, March 22, 2026

Woke up and the room was spinning. Or maybe I was spinning. The room was actually very still. It was full of dust motes floating in the light beams. They looked like glitter. I tried to catch one. It slipped right through my fingers. That’s the problem with the future. You can’t hold onto anything.

I felt heavy. Real heavy. The "Tomato Juice Heavy" must have worked. I felt like a lead apron at the dentist. I walked to the window and *thud-thud-thud*. My feet are making noise again. I looked down. The floorboards were dented where I stood. I weighed too much. I was becoming an anchor.

Bridget was back. She was sitting on the fire escape. She wasn't a pigeon anymore. She was a cat. A Siamese cat. But she had the pigeon’s eyes. She meowed. It sounded like a car alarm. *Eeee-ooooo-eeee-ooooo*.

I opened the window. "You’re late," I told her.

She rubbed against my leg. Zapped me with static electricity. *Bzzzt*. My hair stood up. Even the wig stood up.

I put on my glasses. The prescription was wrong. Everything was too close. I could see the atoms in the glass.

Cabbed to the Factory at 10:00 AM. $65.00. The driver was a "Simulacrum" of Marcel Duchamp. He was driving a urinal. It had wheels.

"Where to?" he asked. "The fountain?"

"Just the Factory," I said. "And watch the traffic."

"It’s all ready-mades," he said. "Everything is traffic."

We hit a bump. I spilled out onto the pavement. It felt like rubber.

The Factory was quiet. The soup line was gone. The "Luxury Hunger" trend only lasted twenty-four hours. Now the sidewalk was just full of crumpled receipts. $100,000.00 worth of paper blowing in the wind.

Sammy was in the back room. He was holding a hammer. He was smashing the hard drives.

"What are you doing?" I asked. "I had files on those."

"I’m increasing the value," Sammy said. "Scarcity, Andy. We destroyed the negatives. Now the digital art is rare. There are only zero copies left."

"That’s very rare," I said.

"Infinitely rare," he said. "We can sell the nothingness for millions."

"Put me down for two," I said.

I walked through the studio. My silkscreens were folded up. Literally folded. Like laundry.

"Don't we need to stretch those?" I asked.

"No," Sammy said. "We’re selling them as 'compressed potential.' It’s very minimalist."

I sat down on a box of Brillo pads. My weight made a *crunch* sound. I hope I didn't squash the art.

Lunch was at "The Oyster Bar" in Grand Central Terminal. But the terminal is for trains now. Real trains. Steam engines. The air smelled like coal and history.

I sat at the counter. I ordered the oysters. $85.00. They were served on a bed of crushed iPhones.

"The screens are salty," I said.

"It’s the tears," the waiter said. He was a real person. Or a very good simulation. He had a mustache. It looked glued on.

I swallowed an oyster. It slid down. *Gulp*. I felt a vibration in my stomach. Someone was texting my stomach.

I checked my internal voicemail. One new message.

*Beep*.

"Andy? It’s Yoko."

"Oh no," I thought.

"I’m thinking of screaming again. But this time, silently. Into the water. Do you want to come? We can charge admission. $1000.00 per bubble."

*Click*.

I deleted it. I don't like water. It ruins the wig.

I paid the bill. I left the tip. 20%. That’s $17.00. I wrote it on the receipt with a Sharpie. The receipt ink bled. It looked like a Rorschach test. "A butterfly," I said. "Or a bat."

I walked out to the platform. A train was coming. It didn't have a conductor. It was a "Simulacrum" of a train. Just the front of a train, hovering over the tracks.

"Step right up," the train said.

I stepped back. I don't trust machines that talk.

Took a cab home at 4:00 PM. $55.00. The driver was a "Simulacrum" of my mother. Julia Warhola.

"Andrew," she said. "You look thin. Eat more cake."

"I'm trying, Mom," I said. "Everything is pixels now."

"Pixels don't make you strong," she said. "Cabbage makes you strong. And soup."

She dropped me off. She didn't ask for money. She just made the sign of the cross at me.

I went up to the apartment. The door was open.

The square egg was gone. The robotic rat was gone.

The cat was sitting on the table. She was licking a paw.

In the middle of the table was a single Campbell’s Soup can.

But it wasn't tomato. It wasn't chicken noodle.

The label was white. Just white.

I picked it up. It was heavy. Heavy like my feet.

I pulled the tab. *Pop*.

Inside, there was no soup.

There was a voice.

"Hello," the can said. "I am you. From yesterday."

"I was in a can?" I asked.

"You were preserved," the can said. "For freshness."

I poured the voice out. It puddled on the table. It shimmered.

"I don't know what to do with this," I said.

"Frame it," the cat said. "It's vintage."

I looked for a frame. But all I had was a mirror.

I held the mirror up to the puddle of voice.

I didn't see my reflection.

I saw a cow. A pink cow. Floating in a green field.

"Moo," the voice said.

I put the mirror down.

I sat on the couch. The cat jumped on my lap. She purred like a dial-up modem. *Eeee-errrrr-shhhh*.

I turned on the TV.

The news was on. The anchor was a "Simulacrum" of Walter Cronkite.

"And that's the way it is," he said. "Sunday, March 22, 2026. And everything is fine."

"Good," I said.

I watched the static. It was very relaxing. Like snow. But warm.

I closed my eyes.

I dreamed I was a can. And someone was opening me.
