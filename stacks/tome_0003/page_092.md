Woke up at 6:00 AM. The room was spinning. Actually, the room was stationary, but the earth was spinning very fast. I could feel the centrifugal force pushing me into the mattress. I felt heavy. Like a used tube of paint.

The sky was still yellow. It’s been yellow for days. It looks like someone put a big filter over the sun. "The Big Yolk," I call it. It’s supposed to be morning, but it feels like breakfast forever.

I tried to get up, but Archie was sleeping on my chest. He was heavy. Solid. Not like a cat. Like a brick. Or a sculpture. "Archie," I said. "You're crushing the art."

He opened one eye. It was a camera lens. *Click*. He took a picture of my tonsils.

"Very invasive," I said.

I pushed him off. He landed with a thud. He didn't bounce. He just stayed there.

I went to the kitchen. I wanted a glass of water, but the faucet was dripping honey. Thick, slow gold.

"Great," I thought. "Everything is sticky."

I had a bowl of "Dehydrated Water" instead. You just add saliva. $55.00. It tasted like my own mouth. Needs more sugar.

Cabbed to the Factory at 8:30 AM. $120.00. The driver was a "Simulacrum" of a 1950s Mom. She had her hair in a bun. She drove very carefully.

"We don't want to spill anything," she said.

"The city is already spilled," I said.

When we got to the Factory, the air was thick. Yellow clouds were rolling through the empty floor space. It looked like the set of a western movie, but the cowboys were scared of pollen.

Sammy was there. He was wearing a mask. A gas mask.

"It's the painting," Sammy said. His voice sounded muffled. Like he was talking through a sock. "The Yellow Square is shedding. It's dandruff-ing all over the city."

"It's pollen," I said. "It's fertilizing."

"It's clogging," Sammy said. "I can't breathe."

Bob was floating near the ceiling. He was wearing flippers.

"I'm swimming," Bob said. "The air is thick enough to swim in."

"I need a vacation," I said.

Just then, the door I ordered yesterday—the oak door—started to vibrate. It was standing in the middle of the room, leaning on nothing.

*Knock-knock-knock*.

"Who's there?" I asked.

"Delivery," said the door.

I opened the door. There was no one there. Just a hat rack. And on the hat rack was a wig. A silver wig. Just like mine, but shinier.

"Is this for me?" I asked.

"It's an upgrade," said the hat rack. "The Director sent it. To match the yellow sky."

I put it on. It fit perfectly. It felt like wearing a mirror. I could see myself in it.

"I look twice as expensive," I said.

"You look twice as heavy," Sammy said.

At 10:00 AM, I decided to work. I needed to make something that wasn't yellow. Something to balance the equation. I took a piece of red acetate. I laid it over a photo of a car crash.

"Red is fast," I thought.

But the red turned pink in the yellow air.

"Pepto-Bismol," I said. "Very stomach soothing."

At 12:00 PM, we went to lunch. We went to "The Oyster Bar" in Grand Central Terminal.

The station was empty. No trains. Just thousands of pigeons. They were flying in formation. They spelled out "BUY" and then "SELL" and then "BUY" again.

"The market is nervous," I said.

We sat at the counter. I ordered the "Raw Data". $85.00. It was a pile of scrabble letters.

"I'll have the vowels," Sammy said.

The waiter brought a plate with A, E, I, O, U. Sometimes Y.

"I don't like the Y," Sammy said. "It's too yellow."

He ate the Y. *Crunch*. He swallowed it.

"You're eating the problem," I said.

"I'm internalizing," Sammy said.

At 2:00 PM, I went to the MoMA. I wanted to see if the Mona Lisa was still there. Or if it was still just a frame.

The museum was quiet. The guards were asleep. They were standing up with their eyes closed.

I walked into the room. The frame was there. But inside the frame wasn't empty. It was a mirror.

"Great," I thought. "It's a selfie station."

I looked at myself. I looked yellow. I looked like a banana in a tuxedo.

A woman walked up. She was wearing a dress made of receipts.

"I love this piece," she said. "It's so commentary. It says, 'You are the art, but you're also the problem'."

"I'm just Andy," I said.

"That's what I said," she said. "A problem."

She took a picture of me taking a picture of the frame. *Flash*.

"Now we're even," she said.

She walked away. The receipts crinkled. *Crinkle-crinkle*.

At 4:00 PM, I went back to the Factory. The yellow clouds were worse. I couldn't see the floor.

I found the oak door. It was covered in pollen.

"We have to polish it," I thought.

I took out my handkerchief. I wiped the door. But the handkerchief turned yellow. And then it dissolved.

"It's very potent stuff," I thought.

I looked for the iGod pill. I wanted to see if it knew how to stop the yellow.

I found it under a stack of "Interview" magazines from 2045. It was glowing.

"If I take this," I thought, "I'll know why the sky is yellow. And knowing is the end of mystery."

So I put it in my pocket. Next to the $500 bubblegum credit card.

At 6:00 PM, I decided to go home. I was tired of swimming in the air.

I walked out of the Factory. A kid was sitting on the curb. He was selling cans of "Anti-Yellow". $10.00 a can.

"Does it work?" I asked.

"It turns everything blue," the kid said. "But blue is sad."

"Sad is better than jaundice," I said.

I bought a can. I sprayed it at the sky. *Psssssh*.

A patch of blue appeared. A little blue hole in the yellow ceiling.

"Look at that," I said.

The blue patch was the shape of a shoe.

"A shoe," I thought. "Walking on the sky."
